# Book I: The Price of Standing

## Chapter 1: Money at the Curb

The first man who handed me money had mistaken me for my father. I tried to explain this while he pushed a roll of hundreds into my palm, but he was already backing into the barber shop, bowing with the frantic courtesy of somebody reversing out of a narrow parking space.

“I'm Aiden,” I called after him. “Minh's son.”

He caught the door before it shut, considered the difference, and came back for a photograph.

The second man knew exactly who I was. He apologized for giving me less than the first, unclasped his watch, and dropped it on top of the money. I stood beside our car with my suitcase between my ankles and a gold bracelet slowly disappearing into the roll of bills. We'd been on Bell Street for perhaps ninety seconds. Shawna was still looking for her sunglasses.

We had come for noodles.

By the time my wife got out, I had nine thousand dollars, a gold watch, and a parking space for the rest of my life. Mr. Flores was pointing out the space while his dog wound its leash around my suitcase. He wore a purple tracksuit. The dog wore a smaller purple tracksuit and was working with considerable determination to remove one of us from the pavement.

Shawna looked at my hands and pulled her purse closer to her side. “Don't put any of that in here.”

“We could buy another purse,” I said.

“I already like this one.” Shawna stepped over the leash, rescued my suitcase, and kissed the corner of my mouth. Then she took a proper look down the street. “Aiden. What did you do?”

Mr. Flores answered before I could make my adolescence sound reasonable. “He threw a refrigerator at my landlord.”

I lifted one finger from beneath the money. “The refrigerator was empty.”

“The landlord wasn't,” Mr. Flores said.

“He moved,” I reminded him.

“Both of us did!” Mr. Flores slapped his chest so hard the dog barked. “I got the downstairs unit.” He faced the barber shop and raised our joined hands, which I hadn't realized he'd taken. “He remembers!”

The people outside the shop applauded. A man under a barber's cape shuffled to the threshold to join them, discovered that his hands were trapped inside the cape, and nodded vigorously instead.

Shawna studied me. “You told me you were weird in high school.”

“I was very good at geometry,” I said.

She looked at Mr. Flores, then back at me. “Apparently you found a practical application.”

Bell Street ran six blocks downhill toward the port. Afternoon light lay across the shopfronts, and beyond the last intersection I could see the tops of cranes moving above the roofs. I'd been away six years. The butcher still occupied the same doorway. The pharmacy still had a sun-faded advertisement for a product that promised to improve your circulation without requiring you to circulate anywhere. The barber shop had acquired a second chair and apparently a foreign policy.

Two boys had tied a bedsheet between a bus stop and a palm tree. WELCOME BACK, GREAT CHIGGA spread across it in wet-looking black capitals. GREAT covered an earlier, longer attempt. One boy kept tugging the string to lift the sagging center; every time he succeeded, the boy at the other end rose onto his toes.

My cousin's nickname had survived three changes of bar ownership and at least one defeat by a dictionary.

“You didn't approve the sign?” Shawna asked.

“I would've asked for a picture,” I said.

She turned toward me. “Of yourself.”

“So people know which great one.” I shifted the cash into a pocket. The watch wouldn't fit, so I put it on. It slid down over my hand. An excellent beginning for a man advertised as larger than life.

Someone shouted my name upstairs. I looked up just as a paper bag came over a window ledge. I caught it against my chest. Heat came through the bottom, followed by a small round patch of grease.

“Auntie Loan says don't get skinny!” the woman at the window called.

Inside were two pork buns and five hundred dollars. I checked beneath the buns in case Loan had included the deed to something.

Shawna peered into the bag. “That's a lot of encouragement.”

I put a bun into her hand. She bit into it, closed her eyes briefly, and stopped objecting to the neighborhood for almost four seconds.

Then Darius came sprinting out of Bishop's with a wireless microphone.

“Ladies and gentlemen, the motherfucking prodigal nigga has arrived!” he shouted.

He didn't need amplification. He'd needed it at fourteen even less, when he tried to book me for a paid appearance at his own birthday party. We'd argued about the deposit until his mother cut the cake. Since then he'd gained a beard, a diamond tooth, and a cream tracksuit that made the walk from the restaurant seem like a formal procession he was late for.

“Saint,” I said, just before he hit me.

He wrapped both arms around my shoulders. I lifted the bun bag out of danger and breathed an entire department-store fragrance counter. Saint thumped my back, drew away, and inspected my face with the satisfaction of a man whose investment had returned from overseas.

He put the microphone under my mouth. “Say something for the people.”

“Your microphone isn't on,” I said.

Saint kept smiling at his audience while his thumb worked the switch. “Technical support. That's why the people love you.”

The speaker outside Bishop's screamed. Three pigeons came off a roof, the dog tried to flee its own tracksuit, and a bus stopped halfway through the intersection.

The driver opened his doors and leaned out. “Aiden?”

I waved with the bag.

“Look at this nigga!” the driver shouted. He clapped above the steering wheel. His passengers began clapping too, some because they recognized me and others, I suspected, because a stopped bus was more enjoyable when it appeared to be intentional. A woman raised a baby in the front window. I waved at the baby. The driver shut the doors and carried the applause downhill.

Saint followed it with his eyes. “I should have sold tickets.”

“To public transportation?” Shawna asked.

Saint pressed a hand over his heart. “I'm meeting my best friend's wife for the first time. Please don't attack my business model.”

She hugged him with her free arm. He addressed her as Mrs. Greatness. She told him she had an actual name. Within seconds the speaker was announcing Shawna Nguyễn to three generations of people who'd already heard every word without it.

The butcher used the announcement to cross the street. He carried a wrapped package broad enough to hide most of his shirt.

“For your wedding,” he told us.

“We got married last year,” I said.

The butcher stopped smiling. “I wasn't invited.”

“It was very small,” I said.

He looked down at the package. “So was the cow when I started saving it.”

Shawna opened the trunk before I could offer another excuse. The butcher maneuvered the meat between our suitcases, gave her a little bow, and then drew Saint aside. They whispered. Saint nodded. The butcher immediately produced a business card and held it beneath my chin while Saint took a photograph.

“What was that?” I asked as the butcher withdrew.

“Nothing,” Saint said, inspecting the photograph.

“Darius.” I put my hand over his screen.

Saint looked up. “He can say you eat there.”

“I haven't,” I said, still covering his screen.

“You have a whole animal in your trunk,” Saint said. “Eventually the claim becomes true.”

I tucked him under one arm and steered him toward Bishop's. Shawna walked on my other side, answering a message from the driver bringing her sewing machines and dress forms. The new atelier was two doors below the restaurant. We'd rented the apartment over it, and she had spent most of the flight drawing a jacket on the back of a customs form.

When I'd asked whether the jacket was for me, she'd said it was for someone who knew when to stop talking.

I still had hopes.

Denise Bishop met us at her restaurant door. The red chairs stood where I remembered them. Steam clouded the glass above the tiled counter, and the smell of broth made the years I'd spent elsewhere feel unnecessarily complicated.

“Don't bring that suitcase in,” Denise said, pointing past my knee. “I just mopped.”

I left it by the door. “Auntie, it's good to see you too.”

She took my face in both hands and kissed my forehead. Then she gave Shawna the same greeting, with an extra squeeze. They'd met on video calls. Apparently that had been enough for Denise to establish her preference.

“Look what she did to you,” Denise said, turning me toward the light. “Collar. Shoes match. Teeth clean.”

“I had teeth before,” I said.

“You also had a rat tail.” Denise let go of my face.

“For eleven days,” I reminded her.

“I counted,” Denise said, and sent us to the window booth.

Saint slid in beside me. Denise pointed at the counter. He began to explain that he was welcoming me home, saw her expression, and slid out again. She put a bowl in front of him at the counter. He accepted the demotion with both hands around the bowl.

Above our booth hung the familiar photographs: a baseball team, a graduation, Mom and Denise holding a cake shaped like a car. In the next frame, Dad stood beside an actual car with one hand under its rear bumper while Denise changed the tire. The photograph was from 2008. He was wearing the blue work shirt he still wore on Sundays.

Denise followed my eyes as she set down our bowls. “He charges everybody else. Tell your daddy that.”

“For changing tires?” I asked.

“For acting like he doesn't want his picture taken.” She pushed the chopsticks toward me. “Eat.”

I separated them. Outside, Shawna's moving truck pulled up behind a black van. Four men got out of the van before the driver could reach his rear doors. One carried a clipboard, one a baseball bat. The other two didn't appear to need assistance making themselves unpleasant.

The clipboard man stopped the driver. The driver pointed toward Bishop's.

Shawna stood.

“Sit down,” I said. “I'll see what they want.”

She looked at the chopsticks in my hand. “Those are my sewing machines.”

She was through the door before I'd put the chopsticks down. Saint left his bowl too. Denise caught mine beneath a lid, trapping the steam I'd come six years to breathe.

“You just got home,” Denise said.

“I know, Auntie.”

She pressed the lid flat. “And I used the good broth.”

Outside, the clipboard man had positioned himself between Shawna and the truck. His white shirt looked newly pressed. He held the board against his chest as though the authority on it might escape if exposed to daylight.

“Six hundred to unload,” he told her.

“I've paid for delivery,” Shawna said.

He tapped the board. “To the street. Unloading is separate.”

Shawna pointed at her shop. “The building is on the street.”

“That's convenient,” the man said.

The driver held out his phone. “Man, I work for the moving company.”

“Then you should know the rules,” the clipboard man replied, without looking at the screen.

Shawna reached for the board. He jerked it out of reach.

“You don't touch this,” he said.

“Then you don't get paid.” Shawna held out her hand and waited.

I came down the steps. The bat man's gaze caught on my face, traveled up to the bedsheet, and returned considerably less hopeful. He leaned toward the man in white and whispered.

“Who?” the clipboard man asked.

The bat man repeated himself, a little desperately.

I stopped beside Shawna. “You've got about six seconds to make this a welcome-home joke.”

The clipboard man looked past me at Saint, who was cupping both hands over the microphone. I could hear a low shuffling noise from the speaker behind us. He'd remembered the switch this time.

“We're with the Wharf Circle,” the man said.

“Four seconds,” I told him.

His eyes moved down to my slippers. I'd changed out of my plane shoes in the car. Shawna said I looked like I was escaping a hotel fire. The clipboard man appeared to have reached the same conclusion and found it encouraging.

“You somebody important?” he asked.

Saint uncovered the microphone. “That's a very expensive question, nigga.”

The clipboard man bumped the board against my chest. Behind him, one of the big men smiled and reached for the moving truck's latch.

I took the clipboard. He held on until I pulled it gently downward; then he released it to keep his shoulder where he'd been storing it. I braced the board across my knee, folded it in half, and put the two pieces against his shirt.

“Separate invoices,” I said.

The bat hit the pavement. Its owner stepped away from it.

At the truck, the big man abandoned the latch and seized Shawna's wrist. She drove her free palm into his nose. His head snapped back against the side panel, leaving a dark smear on the moving company's cheerful lettering. He let her go. She stepped backward toward Bishop's, opening the space between us without looking for me.

I was already in it.

I caught his jacket at the shoulder and his belt at the hip. The black van's sliding door stood open on the opposite side of the narrow gap. I turned him broadside and threw him through it. He crossed the van, hit the far sliding door, and took it off its track. The bottom corner struck the road with a clang. He landed across it, trying to inhale.

The second big man came around my left shoulder. I stepped toward him as his fist went past my ear, caught behind his elbow with my left hand, and drove my right fist beneath his ribs. The air came out of him in one long flat note.

I kept his elbow until his knees reached the pavement. Shawna had moved clear, but he'd been falling toward her shoes, and she'd only just stopped discussing mine.

The clipboard man was at the van's front door. He pulled a pistol from under his shirt. Its front sight caught in the cloth. He yanked again, staring at the snag instead of at me.

I crossed the gap and trapped his gun hand against the van's roof.

He fired.

The shot punched upward through the metal. The street dropped: heads, shoulders, Mr. Flores with both arms around the dog. Saint crouched behind a flowerpot narrower than his chest, still holding the microphone above the rim like a small surrender flag.

I bent the gunman's wrist backward over the roof edge. Something gave with a click I felt through his hand. His fingers opened. I caught the falling pistol by the barrel, set it farther along the roof, and drove his forehead against the side window.

The first impact cracked it. The second put his face through.

I pulled him back and let him slide down the door. He sat among the fragments with his ruined wrist in his lap. I stepped away before he could bleed on my slippers. My knuckles were already doing enough of that.

Behind me, the bat man had both empty hands over his head.

“I told him,” he said.

“You did.” I looked at the bat lying beside his feet.

“I'm from Alder Street,” he added. “I know your dad.”

“Then why are you holding your hands like that?” I asked.

He lowered them halfway. Apparently he knew Dad well enough to retain some uncertainty.

Shawna came beside me. “You shot?”

“No,” I said.

She checked anyway, opening my shirt with quick fingers. Her hand shook once against my chest. I covered it with mine, and for a moment we stood in the broken glass listening to each other breathe.

The man I'd thrown through the van crawled out over the fallen door. He looked around the vehicle at me, abandoned the attempt to stand, and sat on the curb.

The moving driver was still holding his phone. He'd been recording; the camera now pointed straight up.

Shawna took her hand from under mine and turned to him. “Get the machines inside. I'll show you where.”

She found the shop keys in her purse and walked him downhill. He followed so promptly he almost forgot to unlock his truck.

For a few seconds the block stayed quiet. Then Mr. Flores began clapping. The dog barked from under his arm. The barber shop joined in, followed by the butcher, who raised both hands as though he'd personally selected the winner.

From an upstairs window, someone shouted that I should have used the man's elbow on the glass.

“He's rusty!” Saint shouted back into the microphone. “Let the nigga warm up!”

A woman gave me a tissue. While I pressed it to my knuckles, another woman slipped twenty dollars into my shirt pocket.

“Auntie, you don't have to—” I began.

“Take it,” she said, patting the pocket closed.

I took it.

Denise stood at Bishop's door with the bowl lid in her hand. “Your food,” she said, “is getting disrespectful.”

The bat man was helping his boss upright. I pointed to the pistol on the roof.

“Leave the gun.”

“Absolutely,” the bat man said.

“And the bat,” I added, before he could bend to pick it up.

“Wasn't mine anyway,” he assured me.

They gathered their people. The detached door wouldn't go back into its track; two of them propped it against the opening and held it there as the van pulled away. At the first bump it banged against the body. At the second, somebody inside began swearing.

I carried the bat into Bishop's. Denise pointed at the umbrella stand without interrupting her conversation with a customer. I put it there, washed my hands, and returned to the booth.

When Shawna came back, Saint was opposite me drawing columns on a napkin.

“What's that?” I asked.

“Welcome-home expenses,” Saint said.

“You owe me money?” I asked.

He kept writing. “I'm working out how much more we could have charged.”

Shawna took his pen and put it in her purse. I finally got a mouthful of noodles.

A white sedan stopped beside the glass outside. The woman who got out wore a dark green suit. She spoke to Mr. Flores, who pointed at Bishop's with both hands, nearly dropping his dog. She entered, nodded to Denise, and laid a cream envelope beside my bowl.

“Mr. Lâm would like to see you tonight,” she said.

“I'm eating.” I kept the chopsticks in my hand.

“I can see that,” the woman said.

“Then you're already doing better than the last guy,” I told her.

Her eyes traveled to the bat in the umbrella stand. “Mr. Lâm would like to apologize for the misunderstanding.”

I opened the envelope. An address, a time, and a check for a hundred thousand dollars. A drop of broth fell from my chopsticks onto one corner.

Saint laid his napkin over it. Denise lifted the napkin, read the number, and returned with another bowl.

“On the house,” she told me.

Shawna put down her chopsticks. “For what?”

Denise pointed at the check. “Whatever the hell he's doing, I want him to remember who fed him.”

## Chapter 2: Shoes Off

At five that afternoon, my mother opened her front door, looked at the meat in my arms, and shut it again.

I stood on the porch with the butcher's wedding present blocking my view of my own feet. Beside me, Shawna had begun smiling. She'd seen Mom use that door on delivery people during video calls. Apparently family qualified for the same service.

“Mom,” I said.

The mail slot lifted. One of her eyes appeared behind it. “Did you call first?”

“You told me to come at five.” I shifted the beef before its greasy wrapping could slide against my shirt.

“Then why do you have a cow?” Mom asked.

“Wedding present,” I said.

Mom kept the slot open. “You got married last year.”

“Nobody's happy about that, apparently,” I said.

The slot snapped shut. Then the door opened and she pulled Shawna inside, embracing her around the shoulders. Mom wore a black shirt and gray trousers, the same sensible combination in which she'd once made a bride sit down and identify her intended husband before ordering invitations. Beyond her, the house smelled of fried garlic and the laundry powder she'd been buying since I was little.

She took the meat from me with a grunt, braced it on the hall table, and pointed at my feet.

“Shoes,” Mom said.

“Slippers,” I corrected her.

Mom tilted her head. “Outside slippers. Now they're making outside in my house.”

I stepped out of them. Saint came up behind me carrying the suitcase he'd insisted on collecting from Bishop's. He bumped it over the threshold, saw Mom look down, and lifted it so sharply the handle extended another notch.

“Auntie Mai! Looking magnificent,” Saint said, holding the case clear of the floor.

“You look expensive, Darius. Who owes you money?” Mom asked.

“Nobody. That's the problem.” He smiled widely enough for his diamond tooth to catch the light.

Mom put down the beef, caught his chin, and turned his face toward the window. “That's crazy, nigga. What happens when you eat corn?”

“With respect, Auntie, not everything is an engineering problem,” Saint told her.

“Until something gets stuck.” Mom released him, took a bag of recycling from beside the hall cupboard, and looped its handles over the suitcase handle. “Outside.”

Saint backed onto the porch carrying both. Shawna watched him through the screen.

“Does everyone on this street work for your mother?” she asked me.

“Usually only when they come inside,” I said.

Mom came back for me. She kissed my cheek hard enough to hurt, wrapped both arms around my middle, and held on. I bent over her shoulder. For a second I let the house be exactly the size I'd remembered.

Then she slapped the back of my head.

“I saw the video,” Mom said.

“Already?” I straightened.

“Denise sent it before you finished. Hotel slippers, Aiden?” She pointed toward the pair I'd left at the door. “For fighting?”

“Grip,” I explained.

“One came off,” Mom said.

“The weak one,” I explained.

Mom made a small noise deep in her throat and took the meat toward the kitchen. Shawna squeezed my arm. I couldn't tell whether the squeeze meant she was glad to be here or delighted to see somebody else administering me. Both seemed possible.

Dad was in the kitchen holding the refrigerator.

It wasn't a figure of speech. The refrigerator lay sideways across his arms, doors facing the ceiling, its lower corner just clear of the table. A bowl of marinating pork rested on the upper door. Beneath it, a puddle spread from a loose water-supply hose. The rice cooker continued steaming on the counter as though none of this required an opinion.

Dad looked over the appliance. “Come help.”

I went around the table and reached for the far end.

“With the mop,” Dad said.

I collected it from the closet. He lifted the refrigerator a little higher so I could get beneath it, and I began cleaning the water around his shoes. Shawna paused in the kitchen doorway. Dad shifted the weight onto one arm and extended the other hand to her.

“Good flight?” he asked.

“Long.” Shawna shook his hand, then looked at the refrigerator against his chest. “Is that comfortable?”

Dad glanced down. “It was in the way.”

“I can see that,” she said.

“Aiden, corner,” Dad told me, nodding past the mop.

I reached farther beneath him. The mop handle tapped the appliance. The pork quivered in its bowl. Dad corrected the tilt with a movement so small I felt briefly excessive for owning two arms.

Saint returned with the suitcase, saw the arrangement, and took out his phone. Dad hadn't turned around.

“Darius,” he said.

Saint lowered the phone. “For the warranty, Uncle.”

“Good. You can call them.” Dad waited for me to wring out the mop, then turned the refrigerator upright. The bowl began sliding. He caught it by the rim and passed it to Mom as she entered.

Mom took it without pausing her conversation with Shawna about the new sewing machines. She set the beef beside the sink, pork beside the cooker, and sent Saint to put the suitcase somewhere that didn't prevent people from having a kitchen.

I returned the mop. When I came back, Dad was waiting by the table.

I bowed from the waist. We'd done this since I was six and announced myself head of the household because I could finally reach the thermostat. Dad had explained that the title came with bills. I'd bowed and returned it immediately.

Now he put a hand behind my neck and drew me into him.

“Welcome home, son,” Dad said.

His shirt smelled of clean cotton over the old faint smell of his shop. I held him around the shoulders. For a moment I could have been small enough to duck under his arm again.

He stepped back, took my right hand, and tilted my knuckles toward the window.

“Glass,” he said.

“A little,” I admitted.

Dad examined the cuts. “Should've used his elbow.”

Saint slapped the counter. “I knew that was you upstairs!”

Dad looked at him. “I was at work.”

“Then why did—” Saint began.

“Because it was stupid,” Mom said, opening the cupboard for plates. “Several people can notice the same stupid thing.”

Shawna covered a laugh with her hand. I pointed my injured fingers at her.

“You're supposed to be on my side,” I told her.

“I'm in your mother's house,” Shawna said. “I haven't established voting rights yet.”

The back door opened behind her. Granddad Hòa came in carrying oranges and his folding chair, holding the chair sideways to get its bent leg through the doorway.

“Who's stupid?” he asked.

“Your grandson,” Mom said.

Hòa looked at me, then around the kitchen. “Which one?”

“You only have one here,” Mom told him.

“Then why ask me?” He set down the oranges and hugged Shawna and me in turn.

Granddad still smelled faintly of chalk, though he'd retired from teaching years before. Two pens stood in his shirt pocket beside a grocery list and a metal ruler with one corner missing. He unfolded his chair at the end of the table. Bright blue cord held part of the seat together. One leg leaned inward at an angle that made sitting down look like a negotiation.

“We have chairs,” Mom told him.

“This one knows me,” Hòa said, settling into it.

Saint put his hand on its back. “That's a historical chair, Uncle.”

“It will be if you stop touching it.” Hòa waited until Saint withdrew his hand, then took an orange from the bag.

Dad brought the rice over. Mom arranged vegetables around the pork and asked Shawna to help with the serving dishes. Everyone else was told to sit. Shawna got an actual job, which told me more about Mom's opinion than a speech would have.

I took the seat beside my wife. Dad caught its back before I could lower myself.

“Left,” he said.

I shifted it left.

Dad kept his hand on it. “More.”

The table leg was now between my knees. I looked up at him. “Dad.”

Dad nodded at the seat. “Your great-grandfather sits there.”

“I thought he was coming tomorrow,” I said.

Mom put a dish in front of me. “I thought you were arriving without punching a van. People surprise you.”

A high, furious horn sounded beyond the front window. Great-Grandpa's electric cart had reached the gate.

Saint stood immediately. “I'll get him.”

“Don't offer to carry his bag,” Hòa said, working his thumbnail beneath the orange skin.

Saint stopped in the hall. “Why?”

“Find out,” Granddad said.

We heard the gate click. Saint delivered a greeting worthy of a visiting president. Great-Grandpa answered too quietly for me to hear, followed by a prolonged grunt from Saint and the scrape of something heavy across the porch.

Tín entered in a white linen shirt, dark trousers, and sunglasses bigger than his face. Saint followed sideways, both arms wrapped around a canvas bag. Its bottom dragged along the floor despite his efforts to keep it clear.

“Put that down, nigga,” Great-Grandpa told him. “You're making it tired.”

Saint lowered the bag. The spoons jumped in their drawer.

“What's in there?” he asked, carefully straightening his back.

“My things,” Tín said.

“Your things weigh more than me.” Saint tried to lift one corner with his shoe, failed, and pretended he'd been adjusting the bag.

“Get some better things,” Great-Grandpa advised him.

He removed his sunglasses and inspected me. Fine lines crowded around his eyes as his smile spread.

“You got handsome,” Tín said.

“Thank you,” I replied.

He turned to Dad. “Where'd he get it?”

Mom made a delighted noise. Dad pulled out the chair I'd vacated.

“Please sit,” Dad said.

“Don't say please like you're closing a door.” Great-Grandpa went to Shawna first, hugged her, and accepted her compliment on his shirt. “Bought it to annoy my doctor,” he told her. Then he sat down and reached for the nearest dish.

Mom caught his wrist between her chopsticks.

“We haven't started,” she said.

“I've started,” Tín replied.

Hòa quietly moved the dish within his father's reach. Mom turned her chopsticks on Hòa. He became absorbed in removing a last thread of white pith from his orange.

We made it through half the meal before Saint mentioned the check. He'd been struggling for twelve minutes. I could see it in the way he kept swallowing perfectly good opportunities to speak.

“Uncle Minh,” he finally said, “how well do you know Bảo Lâm?”

Dad lifted another mouthful of rice. “Enough.”

“He gave Aiden a hundred thousand dollars,” Saint said.

Mom's chopsticks stopped above the vegetables. “For what?”

“Coming to a party,” I said.

Shawna looked at me. I put down my bowl and supplied the omitted part: the Wharf Circle, the machines, the van, the invitation.

Dad set his bowl down too. “Are you going?”

“Yes,” I told him.

“Don't take their car home.” Dad reached for the serving spoon.

I waited. “That's it?”

“You wanted directions?” he asked.

Mom added vegetables to my plate. “He wanted you to tell him not to go so he could explain his independence.”

“I did not,” I said.

“You took a breath for it,” Mom replied.

“I breathe regularly.” I picked up my bowl again.

Great-Grandpa snorted. Hòa placed an orange segment on his father's plate without looking away from Dad.

“Very calm, you two,” Tín told my parents. “Twenty-six years ago one of you came home with blood on his wedding shoes. The other wanted to know whether he'd saved the receipt.”

Mom pointed her chopsticks at him. “That wasn't the same.”

“No,” Tín agreed. “Your dress cost more.”

Dad passed the pork toward me. “He'll make his own decisions.”

Great-Grandpa turned to Shawna. “Listen to this nigga. When he was twenty-seven, his decisions arrived at my house on a tow truck.”

Shawna rested her chin on her hand. “I'd like to hear that story.”

“After he eats,” Mom said quickly.

I grinned at Dad over the pork. He studied me long enough to suggest that I might eventually want mercy from somebody in this room.

“Eat,” he told me.

The front bell rang. Saint began to stand, but Great-Grandpa touched one finger to his sleeve.

“You already met the bag,” Tín said. “Let somebody else have a turn.”

Dad went down the hall. Through the open kitchen doorway I heard a man announce himself in a bright professional voice.

“Mr. Lâm sent a courtesy vehicle,” the visitor said.

“He has a car,” Dad answered.

“This one's bulletproof,” the driver said.

Dad let a beat pass. “He has a car.”

I got up and joined him. The visitor wore a black suit and a purple pocket square. He'd put one foot across the threshold to keep the conversation open. Dad looked at the shoe.

“Take that out,” he said.

“Sir, I'm just here to—” the driver began.

Dad caught the front of his jacket and lifted him until both shoes cleared the floor. The man's sentence stopped. Dad turned him toward the porch, reached past him to open the screen, and held it clear with one elbow while carrying him through.

The driver hung stiffly, hands against his trouser seams, as if immaculate posture might persuade Dad to finish whatever this was. Dad set him on the top step, smoothed his lapel, and nodded toward the driveway.

“Tell Bảo thank you,” Dad said.

The driver went to the car with very little movement in his knees. I watched him get behind the wheel.

“You could've let me answer,” I told Dad.

“My house.” He began closing the front door.

I put my hand against it before it latched. Dad glanced down at my fingers.

“Son,” he said.

I pushed. He kept one finger on the brass knob. The door stayed where it was.

I planted both feet and pushed harder. The wood pressed into my palm. Dad looked past my shoulder toward the kitchen.

“Mai, did you put ginger in this?” he called.

“Yes,” Mom answered.

“Good.” Dad returned his attention to me as I put my shoulder against the door.

The frame creaked. He removed his finger.

I stumbled forward. Dad caught my shirt, brought me back upright, and put me where I'd started.

“Don't break the house,” he said.

From the kitchen, Great-Grandpa called, “Nigga, you broke that same frame in 2003!”

Dad shut his eyes for half a second. I went back to dinner smiling.

Granddad's folding chair now occupied my old position. Its bent leg looked straight. I paused behind him, trying to decide when he'd repaired it.

Hòa held up an orange segment. “Want one?”

Beneath the table, Tín nudged the chair leg with his shoe. Hòa nudged back. Neither looked down. The thick oak table slid an inch toward the wall, carrying everyone's bowls with it.

Mom slapped the tabletop. “Both of you. I have hot soup.”

The movement stopped. Hòa gave me the orange. Great-Grandpa winked at Shawna. Dad resumed eating as if this were an ordinary structural adjustment made during dinner.

Saint leaned toward me, one hand pressed to the small of his back.

“I need you to ask who's paying to fix this,” he whispered.

I glanced at the canvas bag beside the door. “What did he put in it?”

Saint lowered his voice further. “I opened it while he was arguing.”

“And?” I asked.

He looked across the table to make sure Tín was occupied.

“A hat,” Saint said.

## Chapter 3: Spring

The address on Bảo's invitation belonged to a boxing gym called Honest Work. Two men guarded its door beneath a yellow light. Through the high windows came music, the clink of glasses, and none of the sounds I'd generally associated with honest work.

Saint parked across the street. He'd changed into a black suit and replaced his diamond stud with a larger one. A second microphone lay between our seats. He caught me looking at it and slid it into his inside pocket.

“You're staying in the car,” I told him.

“Nigga, I've been waiting twenty years to say we're on the list.” Saint checked his reflection in the dark side window.

“Different kind of list,” I said.

“They all are when you haven't been on them.” He got out when I did.

I looked across the roof. “My mother will kill you.”

“Your mother likes initiative,” Saint said, straightening his cuffs.

“She gave you recycling,” I reminded him.

“And trusted me to complete it.” He locked the car before I could suggest another useful task.

The older guard recognized me and opened the gym door. His younger colleague put a hand on Saint's chest.

“Just Mr. Trần,” the younger man said.

Saint began pointing at me. “I'm his—”

“Publicist,” I supplied.

Saint paused just long enough to appreciate the promotion. “Publicist. Thank you.”

The younger guard looked at the older one. The older one shrugged, and we went inside.

“You invented my job,” Saint whispered.

“Keep it for ten minutes,” I told him.

The gym smelled of leather and bleach, with a sweeter smell from the drinks being carried between tables. A boxing ring occupied the center. Exercise machines had been pushed against the walls to make room for men in suits. Above them, photographs showed fighters holding belts. An upstairs balcony overlooked the ring from one side, with more guests leaning over its rail and plates balanced in their hands.

Conversations thinned as we passed. People looked at me, then leaned close to their neighbors. Saint responded as though this were the reception he'd personally arranged, greeting a man he didn't know and accepting a glass from a waiter who'd been trying to pass him.

The woman in the green suit met us before we reached the ring.

“Trinh Lê,” she said, offering her hand. “We didn't get to introductions earlier.”

“You were putting a check in my lunch,” I said.

“You were putting lunch on the check.” Trinh shook my hand and turned us toward the far end of the room.

Bảo Lâm sat in a red barber chair beyond the ring. A white towel covered his shoulders. Behind him, a woman was trimming the hair around his ears while he selected peeled grapes from a bowl on the armrest.

He put down a grape and offered me his hand. Four rings had been arranged to keep their stones out of the handshake.

“Aiden. My apologies for today,” Bảo said.

“Put them in writing next time,” I told him. “Keeps the van intact.”

His mouth twitched. The woman behind him hid a smile by checking her scissors.

Bảo indicated an empty chair. I remained standing. Saint began to sit, saw that I hadn't, and used the movement to adjust his trousers.

“You wanted to see me,” I said.

“I did.” Bảo looked toward Trinh, who laid a slim folder on the table beside him.

“I'm here,” I said, resting my fingers on the back of the unused chair.

Bảo took another grape. “You know what the Circle does.”

“Takes money from delivery drivers,” I answered.

“A misunderstanding.” He rolled the grape between his fingers before eating it.

“Popular business lately,” I said.

Bảo watched me chew over nothing while he finished. Then he described clubs, games, private events, disputes settled before they became public. He made it sound like a hospitality business with an unusually thorough complaints department.

“Naturally we have enemies,” he said. “Occasionally we meet somebody we'd rather invite than inconvenience. You have a remarkable family.”

“They're busy tonight,” I told him.

The scissors stopped behind his ear. Bảo looked up at me, and then he laughed.

“Your father said something like that once,” he said.

“How much did it cost you?” I asked.

Bảo set the bowl aside. “Would you like to make money, Aiden?”

I tapped the pocket holding his check. “I'm having a good day already.”

Trinh opened the folder to a photograph of green felt tables, a small stage, and a stained-glass window above a bar.

“Upstairs at the Pearl,” she said. “Games three nights a week. Staff in place. Twenty percent of the house.”

I pulled the photograph closer. “Forty.”

Saint took a long, silent drink.

“Confident,” Bảo said.

“You keep paying me for conversations.” I turned the photograph to see the room's entrance.

“Everyone here went through an introduction,” Bảo said.

I looked over my shoulder at the ring. The nearest table had become very quiet.

“There it is,” I said.

Bảo lifted one shoulder beneath his towel. “A little entertainment. People have heard stories. They'd like to meet the man.”

Saint put his empty glass on the table. “The man brought a publicist. Appearances are separate.”

Trinh regarded him with new interest. “What does he charge?”

“Depends what he has to take off,” Saint said.

I slid the photograph back toward her. “Forty percent. If I win, those four men leave my wife's deliveries alone. All her deliveries. All the drivers. Every imaginary fee.”

Bảo's smile narrowed. I kept my fingers on the photograph until he nodded.

Trinh took out a pen and changed the agreement. I read the page. Saint read over my shoulder, moving his lips at the numbers.

“You can read,” I reminded him.

“I like hearing good news twice,” Saint whispered.

Word traveled across the room faster than the waiter collecting glasses. Chairs turned toward the ring. Cash appeared on the tables. A man in a sleeveless black shirt climbed between the ropes and began rolling his shoulders. His scalp was shaved, both eyebrows ridged with scar tissue. His hands were wrapped but bare of gloves.

“Milo Voss,” Trinh said when she saw me watching him.

“What does he do?” I asked.

Voss leaned out through the ropes toward a hanging heavy bag and drove a short right hand into it. The top link of its chain broke. The bag hit the floor flat, jolting a nearby table hard enough to jump the drinks.

“This,” Trinh said.

At the betting sheet, a man discreetly drew his cash back toward his chest.

Saint inspected the broken chain from where we stood. “Old equipment, probably.”

A broad man beside the ring snorted. He held bottled water on a tray, with a hot dog balanced among them. “Put it up yesterday.”

“You work here, nigga?” Saint asked him.

“Sometimes,” the man said.

“Show a little customer service,” Saint suggested.

The man offered me a bottle. “Hậu Phạm.”

“Aiden.” I shook his free hand.

“I know.” Hậu leaned closer as I took the water. “Watch his left elbow. Broke a man's jaw with it last month. Said he slipped.”

“Did he?” I asked.

“Afterward,” Hậu said. “Blood everywhere.”

I passed Saint my jacket and the unopened bottle, stepped through the ropes, and tested the canvas beneath my shoes. This time I was wearing actual shoes. Voss noticed too. He stood in the far corner with his hands loose and his chin lowered.

“No slippers?” Voss asked.

“My mother saw the video,” I said.

He grinned, showing a gold tooth near the back and a gap beside it. “Ever fought somebody who can hit back?”

“My wife plays tennis,” I told him.

The bell rang while he was still deciding what to do with that.

Voss crossed the ring in three steps and sent his right fist at my head. I leaned left. His knuckles brushed my ear. He turned with the miss, bringing the left elbow around toward my jaw exactly as Hậu had warned.

I ducked beneath it and put two punches into his ribs. His body tightened against my fists. He grunted, shoved me back with his forearm, and drove a knee up the middle.

I caught it on both palms. My feet slid across the canvas.

He smiled at the distance he'd moved me. I kicked the inside of his standing ankle. That removed the smile long enough for him to put his other foot down. He recovered and came after me with both hands.

I slipped the first punch. The second struck my forearm with the weight of a thrown brick. Pain ran to my elbow. He followed me toward the ropes, keeping his shoulders between me and the center of the ring.

Beyond him, Bảo had stopped eating.

Voss stepped onto my right shoe and hit my cheek with his left fist. My head snapped sideways. I tasted blood. Saint shouted something about the referee, which would have been useful if anyone had bothered to become one.

Voss drew back his right hand. I bent my left knee and jumped to get my trapped foot out.

I overdid it.

The rope dropped below my hips. Voss's fist went beneath my ankle. I rose past the gym clock and found myself looking at a man on the balcony with a shrimp halfway to his mouth.

“Excuse me,” I told him.

Then gravity recovered its interest.

I landed behind Voss, knees bent, and felt a board crack under the canvas. Before he could turn, I caught the back of his shirt, pulled him toward me, and drove my knee between his shoulder blades. He hit the mat on his hands and knees.

The room came apart in noise. Someone shouted about a wire. The man on the balcony dropped his shrimp. It landed near Voss's hand, and for an instant the two of us stared at it.

Voss got up before I could enjoy the moment. He caught me around the waist and drove me into the corner. The turnbuckle struck my spine. Then my feet left the mat again, this time because he was lifting me against his chest and trying to fold me over the top rope.

I hooked my right foot behind the corner post. The rope bit into my lower back. He pushed. The post leaned toward a table outside the ring, and its occupants scrambled away with their money pressed against their shirts.

I caught the back of his head with both hands and pulled his face into my forehead.

His nose broke against it. His arms loosened. I dropped low, drove my shoulder beneath his ribs, and sent him sideways into the tilting post. Metal rang against his back.

He swung blindly as I stepped out. I took his left wrist and pulled him away from the corner. He turned to follow the pull, bringing his feet across each other.

I let him finish turning.

Then I hit his jaw.

Both feet left the canvas. His back struck the middle rope and carried it outward toward the abandoned table. The rope sprang him forward again. He landed on his knees, blinking, mouth open.

“Stay there,” I told Voss.

He reached for my leg. I hit him once more. His hand fell away and he lay down.

Hậu came through the ropes with a towel. He knelt, checked Voss, and rolled him onto his side. I backed into a corner, breathing through my mouth. My cheek had begun swelling. The people who'd bet against me were having loud disagreements about what they'd actually meant when they handed over the money.

Saint climbed onto the apron holding my jacket. “We need to discuss the flying.”

I took the towel when Hậu offered it and spat blood into the cloth. “Spring,” I said.

Saint looked up toward the clock. “What?”

“Bent my knee. Used the spring.” I wiped my mouth and gave Hậu back the towel.

“Nigga,” Saint said, “knees aren't supposed to come with a second floor.”

Before I could get out of the ring, a man in a white dinner jacket pressed cash into my hand. Another added a watch. A third wanted to know whether I could attend his daughter's graduation.

“Is she graduating from a casino?” Shawna asked from Saint's phone.

I turned toward him. He held the screen out from behind my jacket.

“She called,” Saint explained. “Couldn't ignore family.”

Shawna leaned toward her camera. “You got hit.”

“A little,” I said.

“Move the camera,” she told Saint.

He turned it toward Voss. Two men were helping him onto a stool. Voss pressed the towel against his face and waved off a third man trying to explain the fight to him.

Shawna considered the view. “Fine. Ice before you come home. And don't let anybody give you livestock.”

Bảo waited beside the ring, clapping slowly. His towel was gone and his hair lay smooth against his head. Trinh stood next to him with the amended agreement.

“Welcome to the Wharf Circle,” Bảo said.

I took the pen. My hands had stopped shaking enough to sign, though I kept the paper flat with my other palm. Forty percent. The deliveries protected. I signed and returned the pen to Trinh.

Across the room a lean man in maroon wasn't applauding. His arms were folded. Beside him sat the clipboard man from the afternoon, holding ice against his face.

“Who's the unhappy one?” I asked Bảo.

“Vinh,” Bảo said. “Collections.”

“He should collect better employees,” I told him.

Bảo smiled without showing his teeth. Vinh unfolded his arms and came toward us, eyes on the agreement in Trinh's hand. He stopped close enough for the peppermint on his breath to reach me.

“You made a mess of my van,” Vinh said.

“Your van made a mess of my afternoon,” I replied.

He looked once more at the paper. “Enjoy your room.”

“I intend to.” I moved my shoulder out of his path as he leaned closer.

“Things catch fire in this neighborhood,” Vinh said softly.

I took the new watch and slipped it into his shirt pocket. He froze rather than let the room see him flinch.

“There,” I said. “Now you can tell me what time.”

The man who'd given me the watch applauded again. I kept smiling at Vinh until he stepped away. The watch dragged one side of his shirt down as he walked.

Behind me, Hậu emerged from the ring. He picked up the hot dog he'd left among the water bottles and examined it. The napkin had absorbed most of what had once made it appetizing.

“Hungry?” I asked him.

“Always.” Hậu looked between the food and the bin.

“Come with us,” I said.

He glanced at Bảo. I opened my jacket and showed him the agreement I'd just been handed back.

“Forty percent of upstairs,” I told him. “Should cover fries.”

Hậu dropped the hot dog into the trash and followed us out.

## Chapter 4: The King Has a Receipt

By nine the next morning, somebody had painted wings on my welcome-home banner. By ten, Saint had put them on a shirt and brought one upstairs to sell to the woman married to the face beneath them.

I heard the pitch from our bathroom. Shawna had ordered twenty minutes with the cold pack against my cheek. The apartment still smelled of cardboard and fresh paint. Below us, workmen were fitting the atelier's cutting table; every hammer blow came up through the floor with more determination than consideration.

“Forty dollars,” Saint told her.

“That's my husband's face,” Shawna said.

“I know,” Saint said. “Bulk discount.”

“You owe him money,” she told Saint.

“He gets one free,” Saint said.

“Fifty percent,” I called through the bathroom door.

There was a pause while Saint considered an unwelcome new business expense. “Of what?”

“The shirts.” I came out holding the cold pack in place. A cartoon version of me soared over a terrified boxer on the fabric in Shawna's hands. BELL STREET AIRLINES curved above my head. Saint had given me wings on the shirt as well as on the banner. Apparently jumping unaided hadn't been impressive enough for marketing.

“You didn't design the wings,” Saint protested.

“I provided the flight,” I said.

He took the shirt back, folding my face inward. “I'll have to raise prices.”

Shawna lifted one finger beneath my chin and turned my cheek toward the window. “People will understand,” she told him. “Difficult being exploited by somebody's actual face.”

Hậu arrived with breakfast a few minutes later, already wearing one of the shirts. Saint began explaining that he'd paid a wholesale price downstairs and now owed the difference.

Hậu stopped unpacking the food. “You sold it to me personally.”

“Versatile company,” Saint said.

Hậu looked toward me. I shook my head. He resumed unpacking, picked up a sandwich, and ate it in two bites while Shawna poured coffee.

She paused with the pot above his cup. “Do you have somewhere else to be?”

“No,” Hậu said.

“Then the sandwich can stay a minute.” She filled his cup and pushed a plate closer.

He looked embarrassed. He put the next sandwich down before taking a bite, as though giving it time to become acquainted with the apartment.

I liked him more for that than for his warning at the ring. Last night, over fries, he'd told us about four years doing collections for Bảo. Before that he'd unloaded trucks, repaired restaurant equipment, and tried professional wrestling. The last career had lasted six months. His shoulders suggested it hadn't failed for lack of material.

“Hank the Thank,” I told Shawna when she asked what name he'd wrestled under.

Hậu closed his eyes briefly. “Poster was supposed to say Tank.”

“You kept it?” Shawna asked.

“People remembered,” he said, cutting his sandwich into two quite deliberate pieces.

Saint sat beside him. “Branding, nigga.”

Hậu pointed at him with half the sandwich. “You don't get a percentage of that too.”

We left Shawna downstairs arguing with the installers about whether level meant level everywhere or only where they'd put their tool. She had the tool in her hand when I kissed her goodbye. The men were watching its little bubble with increasing concern.

The Pearl stood between the jewelry shop and the pharmacy in a narrow brick building. Its downstairs restaurant hadn't opened for lunch. We took the stairs to the gaming room while the smell of hot oil rose from the kitchen below.

Trinh stood behind the upstairs bar counting glasses. Her aunt was attacking the carpet with a vacuum cleaner. To reach Trinh, Saint tried to step over the cord; her aunt moved the vacuum, caught his shoe in the loop, and waited while he freed himself.

“Early,” Trinh said to me.

“I own part of it,” I said.

She set two more glasses on the shelf. “Doesn't usually make people early.”

She showed me the room: six tables, twelve cameras, a bar with a cracked mirror, and a stage wide enough for a singer who didn't intend to pace. The stained-glass window spread blue wings above the bar. Morning light carried the heron's shape across the floor, where her aunt vacuumed straight through it.

Saint stood beneath the bird. “We could put your face there.”

“Could put yours outside,” Trinh said.

He touched his lapel. “I'm the publicist.”

“Advertise quietly,” she told him, and opened a drawer for me.

Bundles of cash filled it. Each had a paper band, and Trinh had lined them up with the care other people gave clean cutlery.

“Your welcome,” she said.

I looked at her. “Another one?”

“Men who watched last night booked tables for tonight. Paid in advance.” Trinh rested her hand on the drawer. “Your presence is worth more than our liquor.”

“How much more?” I asked.

“Fifty-eight thousand,” she said.

Saint coughed into his fist. “I need to stop selling cotton.”

I lifted a bundle and put it in Trinh's hand. “Fix the mirror. And pay the woman with the vacuum whatever you were considering paying me to stop talking.”

Trinh's mouth curved slightly. “She's my aunt.”

“Double it, then,” I said.

The vacuum stopped.

“I heard that,” her aunt called.

“Double the work,” Trinh called back.

Her aunt put one hand on the vacuum handle. “Nigga, I speak English.”

Saint laughed until he had to lean against the bar. I left him there and went onto the little balcony overlooking the street.

Below me lay the atelier, Bishop's, the barber shop, and a flatbed truck carrying something tall beneath blue plastic. Saint joined me just as the truck turned into the block. He folded his hands on the rail. That expression on him usually meant a surprise had exceeded its budget.

“What did you do?” I asked.

“The neighborhood wanted to honor you,” Saint said.

“It's been generous already.” I watched the covered shape sway as the truck passed a pothole.

“Needed a central place to express it,” Saint explained.

Hậu came outside and squinted down at the load. “Is that Bảo's statue from the banquet hall?”

Saint turned toward him. “You know too many things.”

The driver stopped outside Bishop's. Four workers climbed down. One wore ROYAL OCCASIONS over his shirt pocket. He waved at Saint, then pointed to the space beside the restaurant steps.

“You bought Bảo's statue?” I asked.

“Borrowed the concept,” Saint said.

“Sounds like stealing the object,” I said.

“Legally complicated,” Saint assured me as we went back inside.

By the time we reached the pavement, the workers had peeled the plastic away. Seven feet of bronze stood on a plinth. Someone had painted its hair black, put sunglasses over its eyes, and draped a new jacket around its shoulders. Its right hand held an enormous fish.

I walked around the truck to see whether another angle improved it.

It didn't.

“Why am I holding a fish?” I asked Saint.

He inspected it with the surprise of a man betrayed by his own purchase. “Prosperity.”

“Bảo won a fishing tournament,” Hậu said.

Saint spread his hands. “We all have our little journeys.”

People were collecting on both pavements. A boy unfolded a table and began laying out more shirts. Denise emerged from Bishop's, looked up at the bronze face blocking her window, and turned straight back toward her door.

“No,” she said.

Saint hurried after her. “Auntie, we discussed a neighborhood installation.”

Denise stopped on the top step. “You said a bench.”

“This encourages standing,” Saint explained.

“Not in front of my window.” She stayed where she was until he retreated.

The Royal Occasions worker laid his clipboard on the flatbed and asked about the balance. Shawna came out of the atelier with her tape measure around her neck. She saw the clipboard and slowed.

“Not again,” she said.

“Legitimate invoice,” the worker assured her. “Saw the video. We're insured.”

I paid him. That seemed the smallest available problem.

Two workers rolled the statue onto the lift platform. The driver pressed the switch. The motor whined, lowering the bronze fish and its owner toward me a few inches at a time. Then it slowed. The platform stopped halfway down.

The driver tried the switch again. The lift jerked. A worker stood at its left edge, palms against the plinth to steady it.

The left lifting arm snapped.

The platform tipped toward the worker. He tried to step back, but the edge caught his shoe. The statue leaned after him, its bronze face coming down with an expression of prosperous indifference.

I got both hands beneath the plinth.

Its weight drove my right knee into the road. Steel grated against bronze. The worker pulled his foot free of the shoe and scrambled backward on his hands.

“Away from the truck!” I shouted.

The crowd scattered. I braced my good foot, straightened enough to lift the base clear of the broken ramp, and felt its edge bite through the skin of my palms. Hậu ducked in beside me, dragged the loose steel out, and kicked it clear.

The driver stood near his cab with both hands on his head. “What do you want me to do?”

“Move the truck,” I told him.

He climbed in. The engine started. I kept the plinth level while the bed rolled out from under it, shifting the weight fully onto my arms. The fish hovered over my shoulder. From this close I could see that somebody had painted only the front half of the statue's hair.

My right shoe split along the sole.

I took one short step. Then another. On the third I reached the gutter and lowered the plinth onto the concrete. The impact jolted my teeth together. The fish now pointed across the road at the pharmacy, as though it intended to file a complaint.

Saint began clapping before I'd straightened. The rest of the street followed.

The rescued worker limped over in one shoe and wrapped both arms around my waist. He tried to thank me. Nothing clear came out. I rested my hands on his shoulders while he cried against my shirt.

“You're good,” I told him. “You're good. It's down.”

He nodded without letting go. Hậu found his shoe under the broken ramp and brought it over. Denise came down with water. The driver sat on the truck's step, then got up again when he saw his cousin standing.

Shawna crouched by my feet. “Your shoe's dead.”

“Served bravely,” I said.

“I bought those.” She lifted the loose flap of sole with one finger.

“Beautiful childhood, then,” I told her.

She stood and took my wrist to check the hand above it. In the brief interval while everybody's attention was elsewhere, Saint moved the shirt table closer to the statue.

The driver approached with the delivery money. “Take it back,” he said.

“Fix the lift,” I told him.

He looked over at the worker holding his recovered shoe. “My cousin was under there.”

“Still your cousin,” I said.

“Exactly.” He put the delivery payment away, extracted two hundred dollars from a different fold in his wallet, and pushed it into my hand. Then he went back to the truck before I could give him another opinion.

Shawna watched him go. “We've barely unpacked. This city is going to need a budget.”

A silver car stopped farther along the block. A tall man got out, followed by two men carrying boxes. His pale blue shirt had no wrinkles I could see. He stopped to greet a woman at the pharmacy and admired her glasses, keeping his attention on her while his companions waited.

Hậu moved beside me. “Leon Mercer,” he said quietly.

“Sunday,” Saint murmured.

The name traveled through the crowd. Some people came forward to shake his hand. Others put their children behind their legs. Mercer made his way toward us without appearing to notice either response more than the other.

“Aiden Trần,” he said.

“Mr. Mercer,” I replied.

“Sunday. Everybody calls me Sunday.” He looked at the bronze fish. “Strong entrance.”

“Some assembly required,” I told him.

He laughed, introduced himself to Shawna, and kept his hands where we could see them. “I hear you're opening an atelier. My sister needs a dress. I'll send her over.”

“I'll give her my rates,” Shawna said.

“I'm sure she'll deserve them.” Sunday signaled to his companions. They opened the boxes on the hood of his car: handmade shoes, one pair black, the other dark brown.

He glanced at my split sole. “Saw yours go. Lucky coincidence.”

I looked at the shoes, then at the men who'd brought them. “You keep these in your car?”

“I keep gifts,” Sunday said.

Saint leaned toward me. “Rich-nigga sentence.”

Sunday heard him and laughed again. He gave me a card with a number embossed in silver.

“Call if you need anything,” he said. “Bell Street's always been good to my family.”

He left without mentioning the Circle. His car went uphill, away from the water. Hậu waited until it turned the corner before touching the edge of one box.

“Don't wear them,” he said.

“Why?” I asked.

“You'll start owing him places to walk,” Hậu said.

I closed the lids. “Get a good price,” I told Saint.

He already had his phone out.

By lunchtime we'd found a place for the statue beside the barber shop. Denise refused it. Shawna refused it. Loan accepted on the condition that anyone stopping to buy a shirt should also consider a haircut. We used straps, a properly rated moving dolly, and a clear path. I'd done enough of the delivery personally.

Then we went back to the Pearl. Shawna wanted to inspect the stage for her first private showing. She measured the width and watched how the heron's colored light fell across the boards. When Saint stepped into the beam, she told him to stand elsewhere if he intended to keep wearing that much cologne.

I held the other end of her tape. For a few minutes, my job involved staying still while the woman I loved made something happen.

Hậu's phone rang behind us.

He listened, turned toward the window, and stopped chewing the mint he'd taken from the bar.

“Statue's gone,” he said.

Saint looked up from his own phone. “Gone where?”

“Two men. Truck. Said Bảo wanted his face back,” Hậu reported.

Trinh lowered the glass she was drying. “Bảo's on a plane to San Diego.”

My phone vibrated. I kept hold of the tape with one hand and checked it with the other.

An unknown number had sent a photograph. The statue lay in a truck beneath bunched blue plastic. Its sunglasses were gone. Someone had painted a red X across its chest.

Underneath was a message: Bring the thing from underneath, or we start with your wife's window.

I handed Shawna the tape and showed her the screen. Then I turned toward Saint.

He raised both hands.

“I have a receipt,” Saint said.

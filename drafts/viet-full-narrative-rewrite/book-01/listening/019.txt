Chapter 18: Granddad's Errand

Granddad called at nine to ask whether I could drive him to the library. I was in Orchard House's kitchen, listening to Valerie explain what the pool filter had been built to handle. A silk pocket square, three cocktail napkins, and a plastic crown were apparently outside its capabilities.

“Yes,” I told Hòa immediately.

“You sound relieved,” he said.

“I like books,” I told him.

“Then we should have done this sooner,” Hòa replied.

He waited outside his house with a canvas bag and his folding chair. I put the chair in the trunk. He kept the bag on his lap as I pulled away.

“Which library?” I asked.

“Harbor branch,” Hòa said.

“There are closer ones,” I told him.

“This one has my book,” he replied.

I glanced over. “A book you wrote?”

He looked at me. “A book I haven't returned.”

We drove through the neighborhood with the windows down. People waved when they recognized me. Granddad waved back first, confusing some of them into making a more respectful second attempt.

At a light, a man leaned out of a delivery van beside us.

“Aiden! My nephew bought the shirt!” he called.

“Tell him thanks,” I said.

He saw Granddad in the passenger seat and sat straighter.

“Mr. Hòa,” the driver said.

Granddad nodded toward him. “Still speeding, Paul?”

“Not now, sir,” Paul replied.

The light turned green. He moved off with such care that the driver behind him immediately became impatient.

“Student?” I asked Granddad.

“Two years,” Hòa said.

“You remember all of them?” I asked.

“The ones who think traffic lights are suggestions,” he told me.

The harbor branch occupied an old customs building. Its high windows looked toward the water, and the stone stairs had worn smooth through the middle. Granddad went up with his canvas bag. I followed him to the desk.

The librarian smiled when she saw him.

“Mr. Lê,” she said.

“I brought it back,” Hòa told her.

“I was beginning to wonder,” the librarian replied.

He placed a slim blue book on the counter. She opened its cover and checked the date slip. Her eyebrows rose.

“Twenty-seven years,” she said.

I turned toward him. “Granddad.”

He looked at the book. “I wanted to finish.”

The librarian laughed and carried it to a shelf behind the desk. When she returned, she asked whether he'd like to renew his card.

“I brought identification,” Hòa told her.

He took an envelope from his pocket. Inside were two old photographs, a receipt, and a folded letter. He sorted through them slowly, checking both sides of the receipt.

“Maybe I didn't,” he said.

The librarian waved away the problem. “I know who you are.”

Granddad put the papers back. One photograph slipped from the envelope before he closed it. I picked it up from the counter's edge.

A much younger Hòa stood beside a collapsed railway bridge. Tín was a few feet behind him with his shirt open at the neck. Both were muddy. A locomotive lay across the broken span at an angle I couldn't understand until I looked closely at the rails beneath its front half.

The rails had bent upward into a cradle. The train rested on them.

On the back, somebody had written two words: Ask Hòa.

I turned the picture toward him. “Who took this?”

“A woman with a camera,” Hòa said, taking it from me.

“What happened?” I asked.

“The train stopped,” he told me.

“I can see that part,” I said.

He smiled faintly and slipped the photograph back into the envelope. The librarian handed him his renewed card.

“Come on,” Hòa told me. “I have another stop.”

The next stop was a repair shed on the edge of the commercial harbor. A woman in oil-stained overalls came out when she saw the car. Her hair was tied beneath a blue cap. She held a wrench long enough to be useful in an argument.

“Mr. Hòa,” she said.

“Imani,” Granddad replied.

She hugged him with one arm, keeping the wrench away from his shirt with the other. He turned toward me as she stepped back.

“Captain Imani Cole,” Hòa said. “She knows boats.”

“And he knows when I've forgotten my homework,” Imani added.

She looked me over. “You're the grandson.”

“One of them,” I told her.

“The expensive one,” she said.

Granddad smiled. I went back to the car for his folding chair while Imani opened the shed door wider.

Half a boat engine stood near the entrance. Photographs of ferries, tugboats, and their crews covered the back wall. In some, the people looked proud to be standing in front of the water. In others, they looked tired of seeing it.

Granddad unfolded his chair beside the workbench.

“You wanted a ferry captain,” Hòa told me.

I looked at him. “I didn't ask you for one.”

“You showed your mother the invitation,” he said. “She showed your grandmother. By then everybody was having an opinion.”

Imani sat on a stool opposite us.

“I used to command the boat Mercer bought,” she told me. “Before he put gold stripes on it.”

I took the brochure from my jacket and laid it on the bench. She looked at the name across its cover.

“He calls it the Sunday Best?” Imani asked.

“Yes,” I said.

She closed her eyes briefly. “Of course he does.”

I gave her the short version of the cinema, mall, and garage. She listened without interrupting until I mentioned King.

“The big man in the green shirt?” Imani asked.

“You know him?” I said.

“He came here asking for the boat's old keys,” she told me. “I said I'd returned them.”

“Had you?” I asked.

“No,” Imani said.

She gave Granddad an orange from a bowl on the bench. He began peeling it while she opened a drawer and took out photographs. In them, the ferry wore older paint and another name. Crew members smiled from a sunlit deck.

“He keeps changing things,” Imani said, placing two pictures beside the brochure. “Walls where people used to move. Furniture where crew used to work. He thinks money improves a boat the way it improves a room.”

“Does it?” I asked her.

“Depends whether the room floats,” she said.

She tapped a photograph with one blunt fingernail.

“If he fills it the way he's advertising, somebody sober needs to be aboard,” Imani told me.

“Would you?” I asked.

She looked toward Granddad. He had arranged the orange segments on a paper towel. He offered her one.

“He can pay,” Hòa said.

Imani took the segment and looked back at me. “Then yes. And I choose my crew.”

“Done,” I told her.

We were looking at the lower vehicle deck when a van stopped outside. Imani turned toward the door and put down the photograph.

“That's not a customer,” she said.

Three men entered. The first wore a blue jacket with a marina logo. The second kept a hand beneath his shirt. The third remained near the doorway, looking over the engine stand and the tools beside it.

The man in blue saw me and hesitated. Granddad continued removing the last strip of orange skin.

“We're closed,” Imani told the visitors.

“Mr. Mercer needs the old crew list,” the man in blue said.

“He can call me,” Imani replied.

“He sent us,” the man told her.

I stood. The second man drew a pistol.

Granddad flicked the orange peel at his face. The man blinked, and Hòa was beside him before the peel reached the floor.

He caught the gun by the slide and turned his wrist. Metal squealed softly. The pistol came free of the man's hand, and Granddad placed it on the bench. Its barrel pointed upward at an angle the rest of it hadn't agreed to.

The man stared at his empty fingers. Hòa adjusted his shirt collar with two fingers.

“You should ask before bringing things into a workplace,” Granddad told him.

At the door, the third man took a length of chain from a hook and swung it toward Hòa's head. I started forward.

Granddad reached back without turning. The chain stopped in his hand.

He pulled once. The man at the other end flew across the shed and struck the engine stand, folding over its side. The stand skidded six feet across the concrete. Imani caught a mug that had begun sliding off the workbench.

Hòa let the chain fall. The man in blue raised both hands.

“We didn't know you were here,” he said.

“You should have been polite anyway,” Hòa told him.

Granddad returned to his folding chair and sat down. The visitors left, with one supporting the man who'd struck the stand. They made no attempt to collect the pistol.

Imani watched the van pull away. Then she turned toward Hòa.

“You still do that,” she said.

He looked at the orange segments waiting on the towel. “Only when people interrupt.”

I picked up the bent gun with a cloth and moved it aside. Granddad had moved faster than I had. I tried to recall the first step, but couldn't find the moment when his weight left the chair. One instant he was peeling an orange; the next, the man had lost his gun.

Hòa looked up at me watching him.

“What?” he asked.

“Could you do that to Great-Grandpa?” I asked.

Imani became interested in her mug. Hòa held out the last orange segment.

“He would complain about the peel,” Granddad said.

“That's not what I asked,” I told him.

He smiled. “I know, nigga.”

On the way home, he sat with the empty canvas bag folded across his lap. Imani had agreed to bring two crew members she trusted. She'd also given me the former chief engineer's number. According to her, he owed her money and would answer carefully.

Granddad watched the harbor pass outside his window.

“You could tell me,” I said.

“About the train?” Hòa asked.

“About any of it,” I told him.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he took out the envelope and offered me the photograph again.

“Keep that one,” he said.

I stopped at a light and looked at the words on the back.

“Who wrote this?” I asked.

“Your great-grandfather,” Hòa told me.

I turned toward him. He pointed through the windshield.

“Light's green,” he said.

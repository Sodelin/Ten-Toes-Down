Chapter 4: The King Has a Receipt

By nine the next morning, somebody had painted wings on my welcome-home banner. By ten, Saint had put them on a shirt and brought one upstairs to sell to the woman married to the face beneath them.

I heard the pitch from our bathroom. Shawna had ordered twenty minutes with the cold pack against my cheek. The apartment still smelled of cardboard and fresh paint. Below us, workmen were fitting the atelier's cutting table; every hammer blow came up through the floor with more determination than consideration.

“Forty dollars,” Saint told her.

“That's my husband's face,” Shawna said.

“I know,” Saint said. “Bulk discount.”

“You owe him money,” she told Saint.

“He gets one free,” Saint said.

“Fifty percent,” I called through the bathroom door.

There was a pause while Saint considered an unwelcome new business expense. “Of what?”

“The shirts.” I came out holding the cold pack in place. A cartoon version of me soared over a terrified boxer on the fabric in Shawna's hands. BELL STREET AIRLINES curved above my head. Saint had given me wings on the shirt as well as on the banner. Apparently jumping unaided hadn't been impressive enough for marketing.

“You didn't design the wings,” Saint protested.

“I provided the flight,” I said.

He took the shirt back, folding my face inward. “I'll have to raise prices.”

Shawna lifted one finger beneath my chin and turned my cheek toward the window. “People will understand,” she told him. “Difficult being exploited by somebody's actual face.”

Hậu arrived with breakfast a few minutes later, already wearing one of the shirts. Saint began explaining that he'd paid a wholesale price downstairs and now owed the difference.

Hậu stopped unpacking the food. “You sold it to me personally.”

“Versatile company,” Saint said.

Hậu looked toward me. I shook my head. He resumed unpacking, picked up a sandwich, and ate it in two bites while Shawna poured coffee.

She paused with the pot above his cup. “Do you have somewhere else to be?”

“No,” Hậu said.

“Then the sandwich can stay a minute.” She filled his cup and pushed a plate closer.

He looked embarrassed. He put the next sandwich down before taking a bite, as though giving it time to become acquainted with the apartment.

I liked him more for that than for his warning at the ring. Last night, over fries, he'd told us about four years doing collections for Bảo. Before that he'd unloaded trucks, repaired restaurant equipment, and tried professional wrestling. The last career had lasted six months. His shoulders suggested it hadn't failed for lack of material.

“Hank the Thank,” I told Shawna when she asked what name he'd wrestled under.

Hậu closed his eyes briefly. “Poster was supposed to say Tank.”

“You kept it?” Shawna asked.

“People remembered,” he said, cutting his sandwich into two quite deliberate pieces.

Saint sat beside him. “Branding, nigga.”

Hậu pointed at him with half the sandwich. “You don't get a percentage of that too.”

We left Shawna downstairs arguing with the installers about whether level meant level everywhere or only where they'd put their tool. She had the tool in her hand when I kissed her goodbye. The men were watching its little bubble with increasing concern.

The Pearl stood between the jewelry shop and the pharmacy in a narrow brick building. Its downstairs restaurant hadn't opened for lunch. We took the stairs to the gaming room while the smell of hot oil rose from the kitchen below.

Trinh stood behind the upstairs bar counting glasses. Her aunt was attacking the carpet with a vacuum cleaner. To reach Trinh, Saint tried to step over the cord; her aunt moved the vacuum, caught his shoe in the loop, and waited while he freed himself.

“Early,” Trinh said to me.

“I own part of it,” I said.

She set two more glasses on the shelf. “Doesn't usually make people early.”

She showed me the room: six tables, twelve cameras, a bar with a cracked mirror, and a stage wide enough for a singer who didn't intend to pace. The stained-glass window spread blue wings above the bar. Morning light carried the heron's shape across the floor, where her aunt vacuumed straight through it.

Saint stood beneath the bird. “We could put your face there.”

“Could put yours outside,” Trinh said.

He touched his lapel. “I'm the publicist.”

“Advertise quietly,” she told him, and opened a drawer for me.

Bundles of cash filled it. Each had a paper band, and Trinh had lined them up with the care other people gave clean cutlery.

“Your welcome,” she said.

I looked at her. “Another one?”

“Men who watched last night booked tables for tonight. Paid in advance.” Trinh rested her hand on the drawer. “Your presence is worth more than our liquor.”

“How much more?” I asked.

“Fifty-eight thousand,” she said.

Saint coughed into his fist. “I need to stop selling cotton.”

I lifted a bundle and put it in Trinh's hand. “Fix the mirror. And pay the woman with the vacuum whatever you were considering paying me to stop talking.”

Trinh's mouth curved slightly. “She's my aunt.”

“Double it, then,” I said.

The vacuum stopped.

“I heard that,” her aunt called.

“Double the work,” Trinh called back.

Her aunt put one hand on the vacuum handle. “Nigga, I speak English.”

Saint laughed until he had to lean against the bar. I left him there and went onto the little balcony overlooking the street.

Below me lay the atelier, Bishop's, the barber shop, and a flatbed truck carrying something tall beneath blue plastic. Saint joined me just as the truck turned into the block. He folded his hands on the rail. That expression on him usually meant a surprise had exceeded its budget.

“What did you do?” I asked.

“The neighborhood wanted to honor you,” Saint said.

“It's been generous already.” I watched the covered shape sway as the truck passed a pothole.

“Needed a central place to express it,” Saint explained.

Hậu came outside and squinted down at the load. “Is that Bảo's statue from the banquet hall?”

Saint turned toward him. “You know too many things.”

The driver stopped outside Bishop's. Four workers climbed down. One wore ROYAL OCCASIONS over his shirt pocket. He waved at Saint, then pointed to the space beside the restaurant steps.

“You bought Bảo's statue?” I asked.

“Borrowed the concept,” Saint said.

“Sounds like stealing the object,” I said.

“Legally complicated,” Saint assured me as we went back inside.

By the time we reached the pavement, the workers had peeled the plastic away. Seven feet of bronze stood on a plinth. Someone had painted its hair black, put sunglasses over its eyes, and draped a new jacket around its shoulders. Its right hand held an enormous fish.

I walked around the truck to see whether another angle improved it.

It didn't.

“Why am I holding a fish?” I asked Saint.

He inspected it with the surprise of a man betrayed by his own purchase. “Prosperity.”

“Bảo won a fishing tournament,” Hậu said.

Saint spread his hands. “We all have our little journeys.”

People were collecting on both pavements. A boy unfolded a table and began laying out more shirts. Denise emerged from Bishop's, looked up at the bronze face blocking her window, and turned straight back toward her door.

“No,” she said.

Saint hurried after her. “Auntie, we discussed a neighborhood installation.”

Denise stopped on the top step. “You said a bench.”

“This encourages standing,” Saint explained.

“Not in front of my window.” She stayed where she was until he retreated.

The Royal Occasions worker laid his clipboard on the flatbed and asked about the balance. Shawna came out of the atelier with her tape measure around her neck. She saw the clipboard and slowed.

“Not again,” she said.

“Legitimate invoice,” the worker assured her. “Saw the video. We're insured.”

I paid him. That seemed the smallest available problem.

Two workers rolled the statue onto the lift platform. The driver pressed the switch. The motor whined, lowering the bronze fish and its owner toward me a few inches at a time. Then it slowed. The platform stopped halfway down.

The driver tried the switch again. The lift jerked. A worker stood at its left edge, palms against the plinth to steady it.

The left lifting arm snapped.

The platform tipped toward the worker. He tried to step back, but the edge caught his shoe. The statue leaned after him, its bronze face coming down with an expression of prosperous indifference.

I got both hands beneath the plinth.

Its weight drove my right knee into the road. Steel grated against bronze. The worker pulled his foot free of the shoe and scrambled backward on his hands.

“Away from the truck!” I shouted.

The crowd scattered. I braced my good foot, straightened enough to lift the base clear of the broken ramp, and felt its edge bite through the skin of my palms. Hậu ducked in beside me, dragged the loose steel out, and kicked it clear.

The driver stood near his cab with both hands on his head. “What do you want me to do?”

“Move the truck,” I told him.

He climbed in. The engine started. I kept the plinth level while the bed rolled out from under it, shifting the weight fully onto my arms. The fish hovered over my shoulder. From this close I could see that somebody had painted only the front half of the statue's hair.

My right shoe split along the sole.

I took one short step. Then another. On the third I reached the gutter and lowered the plinth onto the concrete. The impact jolted my teeth together. The fish now pointed across the road at the pharmacy, as though it intended to file a complaint.

Saint began clapping before I'd straightened. The rest of the street followed.

The rescued worker limped over in one shoe and wrapped both arms around my waist. He tried to thank me. Nothing clear came out. I rested my hands on his shoulders while he cried against my shirt.

“You're good,” I told him. “You're good. It's down.”

He nodded without letting go. Hậu found his shoe under the broken ramp and brought it over. Denise came down with water. The driver sat on the truck's step, then got up again when he saw his cousin standing.

Shawna crouched by my feet. “Your shoe's dead.”

“Served bravely,” I said.

“I bought those.” She lifted the loose flap of sole with one finger.

“Beautiful childhood, then,” I told her.

She stood and took my wrist to check the hand above it. In the brief interval while everybody's attention was elsewhere, Saint moved the shirt table closer to the statue.

The driver approached with the delivery money. “Take it back,” he said.

“Fix the lift,” I told him.

He looked over at the worker holding his recovered shoe. “My cousin was under there.”

“Still your cousin,” I said.

“Exactly.” He put the delivery payment away, extracted two hundred dollars from a different fold in his wallet, and pushed it into my hand. Then he went back to the truck before I could give him another opinion.

Shawna watched him go. “We've barely unpacked. This city is going to need a budget.”

A silver car stopped farther along the block. A tall man got out, followed by two men carrying boxes. His pale blue shirt had no wrinkles I could see. He stopped to greet a woman at the pharmacy and admired her glasses, keeping his attention on her while his companions waited.

Hậu moved beside me. “Leon Mercer,” he said quietly.

“Sunday,” Saint murmured.

The name traveled through the crowd. Some people came forward to shake his hand. Others put their children behind their legs. Mercer made his way toward us without appearing to notice either response more than the other.

“Aiden Trần,” he said.

“Mr. Mercer,” I replied.

“Sunday. Everybody calls me Sunday.” He looked at the bronze fish. “Strong entrance.”

“Some assembly required,” I told him.

He laughed, introduced himself to Shawna, and kept his hands where we could see them. “I hear you're opening an atelier. My sister needs a dress. I'll send her over.”

“I'll give her my rates,” Shawna said.

“I'm sure she'll deserve them.” Sunday signaled to his companions. They opened the boxes on the hood of his car: handmade shoes, one pair black, the other dark brown.

He glanced at my split sole. “Saw yours go. Lucky coincidence.”

I looked at the shoes, then at the men who'd brought them. “You keep these in your car?”

“I keep gifts,” Sunday said.

Saint leaned toward me. “Rich-nigga sentence.”

Sunday heard him and laughed again. He gave me a card with a number embossed in silver.

“Call if you need anything,” he said. “Bell Street's always been good to my family.”

He left without mentioning the Circle. His car went uphill, away from the water. Hậu waited until it turned the corner before touching the edge of one box.

“Don't wear them,” he said.

“Why?” I asked.

“You'll start owing him places to walk,” Hậu said.

I closed the lids. “Get a good price,” I told Saint.

He already had his phone out.

By lunchtime we'd found a place for the statue beside the barber shop. Denise refused it. Shawna refused it. Loan accepted on the condition that anyone stopping to buy a shirt should also consider a haircut. We used straps, a properly rated moving dolly, and a clear path. I'd done enough of the delivery personally.

Then we went back to the Pearl. Shawna wanted to inspect the stage for her first private showing. She measured the width and watched how the heron's colored light fell across the boards. When Saint stepped into the beam, she told him to stand elsewhere if he intended to keep wearing that much cologne.

I held the other end of her tape. For a few minutes, my job involved staying still while the woman I loved made something happen.

Hậu's phone rang behind us.

He listened, turned toward the window, and stopped chewing the mint he'd taken from the bar.

“Statue's gone,” he said.

Saint looked up from his own phone. “Gone where?”

“Two men. Truck. Said Bảo wanted his face back,” Hậu reported.

Trinh lowered the glass she was drying. “Bảo's on a plane to San Diego.”

My phone vibrated. I kept hold of the tape with one hand and checked it with the other.

An unknown number had sent a photograph. The statue lay in a truck beneath bunched blue plastic. Its sunglasses were gone. Someone had painted a red X across its chest.

Underneath was a message: Bring the thing from underneath, or we start with your wife's window.

I handed Shawna the tape and showed her the screen. Then I turned toward Saint.

He raised both hands.

“I have a receipt,” Saint said.

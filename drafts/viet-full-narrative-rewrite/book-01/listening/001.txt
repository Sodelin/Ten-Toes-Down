Chapter 1: Money at the Curb

The first man who handed me money had mistaken me for my father. I tried to explain this while he pushed a roll of hundreds into my palm, but he was already backing into the barber shop, bowing with the frantic courtesy of somebody reversing out of a narrow parking space.

“I'm Aiden,” I called after him. “Minh's son.”

He caught the door before it shut, considered the difference, and came back for a photograph.

The second man knew exactly who I was. He apologized for giving me less than the first, unclasped his watch, and dropped it on top of the money. I stood beside our car with my suitcase between my ankles and a gold bracelet slowly disappearing into the roll of bills. We'd been on Bell Street for perhaps ninety seconds. Shawna was still looking for her sunglasses.

We had come for noodles.

By the time my wife got out, I had nine thousand dollars, a gold watch, and a parking space for the rest of my life. Mr. Flores was pointing out the space while his dog wound its leash around my suitcase. He wore a purple tracksuit. The dog wore a smaller purple tracksuit and was working with considerable determination to remove one of us from the pavement.

Shawna looked at my hands and pulled her purse closer to her side. “Don't put any of that in here.”

“We could buy another purse,” I said.

“I already like this one.” Shawna stepped over the leash, rescued my suitcase, and kissed the corner of my mouth. Then she took a proper look down the street. “Aiden. What did you do?”

Mr. Flores answered before I could make my adolescence sound reasonable. “He threw a refrigerator at my landlord.”

I lifted one finger from beneath the money. “The refrigerator was empty.”

“The landlord wasn't,” Mr. Flores said.

“He moved,” I reminded him.

“Both of us did!” Mr. Flores slapped his chest so hard the dog barked. “I got the downstairs unit.” He faced the barber shop and raised our joined hands, which I hadn't realized he'd taken. “He remembers!”

The people outside the shop applauded. A man under a barber's cape shuffled to the threshold to join them, discovered that his hands were trapped inside the cape, and nodded vigorously instead.

Shawna studied me. “You told me you were weird in high school.”

“I was very good at geometry,” I said.

She looked at Mr. Flores, then back at me. “Apparently you found a practical application.”

Bell Street ran six blocks downhill toward the port. Afternoon light lay across the shopfronts, and beyond the last intersection I could see the tops of cranes moving above the roofs. I'd been away six years. The butcher still occupied the same doorway. The pharmacy still had a sun-faded advertisement for a product that promised to improve your circulation without requiring you to circulate anywhere. The barber shop had acquired a second chair and apparently a foreign policy.

Two boys had tied a bedsheet between a bus stop and a palm tree. WELCOME BACK, GREAT CHIGGA spread across it in wet-looking black capitals. GREAT covered an earlier, longer attempt. One boy kept tugging the string to lift the sagging center; every time he succeeded, the boy at the other end rose onto his toes.

My cousin's nickname had survived three changes of bar ownership and at least one defeat by a dictionary.

“You didn't approve the sign?” Shawna asked.

“I would've asked for a picture,” I said.

She turned toward me. “Of yourself.”

“So people know which great one.” I shifted the cash into a pocket. The watch wouldn't fit, so I put it on. It slid down over my hand. An excellent beginning for a man advertised as larger than life.

Someone shouted my name upstairs. I looked up just as a paper bag came over a window ledge. I caught it against my chest. Heat came through the bottom, followed by a small round patch of grease.

“Auntie Loan says don't get skinny!” the woman at the window called.

Inside were two pork buns and five hundred dollars. I checked beneath the buns in case Loan had included the deed to something.

Shawna peered into the bag. “That's a lot of encouragement.”

I put a bun into her hand. She bit into it, closed her eyes briefly, and stopped objecting to the neighborhood for almost four seconds.

Then Darius came sprinting out of Bishop's with a wireless microphone.

“Ladies and gentlemen, the motherfucking prodigal nigga has arrived!” he shouted.

He didn't need amplification. He'd needed it at fourteen even less, when he tried to book me for a paid appearance at his own birthday party. We'd argued about the deposit until his mother cut the cake. Since then he'd gained a beard, a diamond tooth, and a cream tracksuit that made the walk from the restaurant seem like a formal procession he was late for.

“Saint,” I said, just before he hit me.

He wrapped both arms around my shoulders. I lifted the bun bag out of danger and breathed an entire department-store fragrance counter. Saint thumped my back, drew away, and inspected my face with the satisfaction of a man whose investment had returned from overseas.

He put the microphone under my mouth. “Say something for the people.”

“Your microphone isn't on,” I said.

Saint kept smiling at his audience while his thumb worked the switch. “Technical support. That's why the people love you.”

The speaker outside Bishop's screamed. Three pigeons came off a roof, the dog tried to flee its own tracksuit, and a bus stopped halfway through the intersection.

The driver opened his doors and leaned out. “Aiden?”

I waved with the bag.

“Look at this nigga!” the driver shouted. He clapped above the steering wheel. His passengers began clapping too, some because they recognized me and others, I suspected, because a stopped bus was more enjoyable when it appeared to be intentional. A woman raised a baby in the front window. I waved at the baby. The driver shut the doors and carried the applause downhill.

Saint followed it with his eyes. “I should have sold tickets.”

“To public transportation?” Shawna asked.

Saint pressed a hand over his heart. “I'm meeting my best friend's wife for the first time. Please don't attack my business model.”

She hugged him with her free arm. He addressed her as Mrs. Greatness. She told him she had an actual name. Within seconds the speaker was announcing Shawna Nguyễn to three generations of people who'd already heard every word without it.

The butcher used the announcement to cross the street. He carried a wrapped package broad enough to hide most of his shirt.

“For your wedding,” he told us.

“We got married last year,” I said.

The butcher stopped smiling. “I wasn't invited.”

“It was very small,” I said.

He looked down at the package. “So was the cow when I started saving it.”

Shawna opened the trunk before I could offer another excuse. The butcher maneuvered the meat between our suitcases, gave her a little bow, and then drew Saint aside. They whispered. Saint nodded. The butcher immediately produced a business card and held it beneath my chin while Saint took a photograph.

“What was that?” I asked as the butcher withdrew.

“Nothing,” Saint said, inspecting the photograph.

“Darius.” I put my hand over his screen.

Saint looked up. “He can say you eat there.”

“I haven't,” I said, still covering his screen.

“You have a whole animal in your trunk,” Saint said. “Eventually the claim becomes true.”

I tucked him under one arm and steered him toward Bishop's. Shawna walked on my other side, answering a message from the driver bringing her sewing machines and dress forms. The new atelier was two doors below the restaurant. We'd rented the apartment over it, and she had spent most of the flight drawing a jacket on the back of a customs form.

When I'd asked whether the jacket was for me, she'd said it was for someone who knew when to stop talking.

I still had hopes.

Denise Bishop met us at her restaurant door. The red chairs stood where I remembered them. Steam clouded the glass above the tiled counter, and the smell of broth made the years I'd spent elsewhere feel unnecessarily complicated.

“Don't bring that suitcase in,” Denise said, pointing past my knee. “I just mopped.”

I left it by the door. “Auntie, it's good to see you too.”

She took my face in both hands and kissed my forehead. Then she gave Shawna the same greeting, with an extra squeeze. They'd met on video calls. Apparently that had been enough for Denise to establish her preference.

“Look what she did to you,” Denise said, turning me toward the light. “Collar. Shoes match. Teeth clean.”

“I had teeth before,” I said.

“You also had a rat tail.” Denise let go of my face.

“For eleven days,” I reminded her.

“I counted,” Denise said, and sent us to the window booth.

Saint slid in beside me. Denise pointed at the counter. He began to explain that he was welcoming me home, saw her expression, and slid out again. She put a bowl in front of him at the counter. He accepted the demotion with both hands around the bowl.

Above our booth hung the familiar photographs: a baseball team, a graduation, Mom and Denise holding a cake shaped like a car. In the next frame, Dad stood beside an actual car with one hand under its rear bumper while Denise changed the tire. The photograph was from 2008. He was wearing the blue work shirt he still wore on Sundays.

Denise followed my eyes as she set down our bowls. “He charges everybody else. Tell your daddy that.”

“For changing tires?” I asked.

“For acting like he doesn't want his picture taken.” She pushed the chopsticks toward me. “Eat.”

I separated them. Outside, Shawna's moving truck pulled up behind a black van. Four men got out of the van before the driver could reach his rear doors. One carried a clipboard, one a baseball bat. The other two didn't appear to need assistance making themselves unpleasant.

The clipboard man stopped the driver. The driver pointed toward Bishop's.

Shawna stood.

“Sit down,” I said. “I'll see what they want.”

She looked at the chopsticks in my hand. “Those are my sewing machines.”

She was through the door before I'd put the chopsticks down. Saint left his bowl too. Denise caught mine beneath a lid, trapping the steam I'd come six years to breathe.

“You just got home,” Denise said.

“I know, Auntie,” I told her.

She pressed the lid flat. “And I used the good broth.”

## Chapter 22: No One Leaves the Party

Sunday caught my sleeve as I passed his chair.

“The program hasn't begun,” he told me.

I looked at his hand. He removed it.

“Where are you going?” Sunday asked.

“To see the part of the boat you didn't put in the brochure,” I said.

King stopped at the foot of the upper stairs, close to the doors leading down. He rolled his shoulders once. White linen had replaced the green shirt from the bowling alley. His collar was open, and his hands hung loose at his sides.

“We can do this after dinner,” King said.

“You get tired after meals?” I asked.

He smiled. “Only when they're small.”

Hậu came beside me. Voss moved to my other side. Saint remained at the table, looking between the stairs, the guests, and the room's other doors.

The singer finished her song. A few people applauded, uncertainly. Most were watching the men forming a line at the far end of the lounge. Sunday stood and raised his glass.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” he announced. “A small private matter. Please enjoy yourselves.”

I took the microphone from the singer's stand.

“The crew downstairs need the exits opened,” I told the room. “If you work on this boat, find Captain Cole. If you're a guest, keep away from the stairs until we clear them.”

Sunday's glass stopped halfway to his mouth. The room went quiet. Saint came over and took the microphone from me.

“And put down the champagne, niggas,” Saint added. “You can carry your own feet.”

King drove his right hand toward my chest. I stepped left and caught his forearm with both hands. The force carried me across the polished floor before I could turn his punch past my body.

Hậu hit him low around the waist. Voss struck the side of his left knee. King staggered one step, then seized the back of Hậu's jacket and threw him onto the nearest empty table.

The table broke. Voss punched King's jaw. King turned with the blow, caught Voss's shoulder, and shoved him against the stair rail. The metal bent around his back, but Voss kept his feet.

I took a champagne bottle from a tray and smashed it across King's temple. Glass burst. He blinked as blood ran down the side of his face. I drove the broken neck toward his eye.

He caught my wrist. I dropped the glass and punched his throat with my other hand. His grip loosened enough for me to pull free. I kicked his knee and shouted Hậu's name.

Hậu rolled clear of the broken table, took the edge of another, and shoved it into King's legs. I jumped onto that table and over King's shoulder, landing beside the stair doors. Voss followed me through them. Hậu stayed in the lounge to keep King from coming after us.

Behind us, Saint told the guests where to move. Chairs scraped across the floor. A woman shouted for her husband. The band continued for three confused bars before somebody told them to stop.

I went down the stairs two at a time. The lower passage smelled of hot metal and cooking oil. A steel door blocked access to the vehicle deck. Two men stood in front of it, one holding a pistol, the other a heavy ring of keys.

The gunman began to raise his arm. I dropped low, slid the last few feet along the smooth floor, and kicked his legs out. He fired as he fell. The shot struck the ceiling behind me.

Voss stepped past my shoulder, caught the other man's wrist, and drove a fist into his stomach. The keys fell. Voss caught them before they reached the floor.

I took the pistol from the gunman and shoved him face-first against the wall.

“Stay there,” I told him.

He stayed. I slid the gun well beneath a fixed service cabinet and got up while Voss tried the keys. The third turned. We pulled the door open together.

Thirty people were crowded on the other side, including Imani and Edwin. Cooks in white jackets stood with servers, deckhands, and two musicians. Edwin sat on an overturned crate pressing a cloth to his forehead. Imani knelt beside him.

She stood when she saw us.

“Upper lounge?” Imani asked.

“Saint is moving people,” I told her.

“Then we get these people to the open deck,” she said.

She took the keys from Voss and began giving orders. The deckhands moved at once. Frightened servers watched them, then followed. Voss held the door while I crouched beside Edwin.

“Can you walk?” I asked.

“I can complain standing up,” Edwin told me.

I helped him rise. He swayed, found his balance, and pointed toward the far end of the deck.

“They've got somebody who thinks throttle is a personality,” Edwin said.

The floor shifted beneath us as the ferry entered a sharp turn. Overhead lights swung. Imani looked up, then toward the line at the door.

“Get them out now,” she ordered.

I left Edwin with her and went up the stairs. At the lounge entrance, Hậu was on one knee. Blood ran from a split eyebrow. King stood over him, gripping the back of his collar.

I jumped and struck King from behind with both feet. He let go of Hậu as the impact drove him across the lounge and into the bar. Bottles fell around his shoulders.

I landed badly on my left foot. Pain shot up the ankle. I caught a table edge and stayed upright while Hậu tried to stand.

“Help Imani,” I told him.

He shook his head. “I can—”

“She needs someone who can carry Ed if he stops complaining,” I said.

Hậu looked at me, then toward the stairs.

“Don't let that nigga keep another jacket,” he told me.

He went down. Saint had cleared most of the guests from the lounge. A few remained by the windows, frozen or still filming. He moved from group to group with the microphone, pointing toward the open deck doors.

“You in the purple tie,” Saint called. “Put the phone down. Your wife is already outside. Follow your good fortune.”

The man lowered his phone and went.

Sunday stood near the upper stairs between two guards. He looked around the emptying room and its broken furniture.

“You could have sat down,” Sunday told me.

I picked up a chair and threw it at him. He ducked behind the rail. The chair burst against the wall above his head, and his guards pulled him up the stairs.

King rose behind the bar. He took a broken bottle out of his shoulder and dropped it among the others.

“Just us,” he said.

My phone rang. Shawna's name filled the screen. I answered without looking away from King.

“Are you safe?” I asked her.

“Your father is making a parking decision,” Shawna replied.

An engine roared through the phone, followed by a heavy metallic crash.

“Shawna?” I said.

“I'm inside,” she told me. “We're all inside. Two trucks came to the printshop.”

King took a step toward me. I moved around the nearest table, keeping it between us.

“Put Dad on,” I said.

I heard a scrape and a grunt as the phone changed hands.

“Busy,” Dad said.

“Mercer's men?” I asked.

“They haven't introduced themselves,” he told me.

A man shouted in the background. Metal tore with a long, shrill noise. Dad spoke away from the phone.

“Take your foot off the pedal, nigga,” Dad told someone. “You're wasting fuel.”

I heard Great-Grandpa laugh. Then Mom's voice came through, sharp and furious.

“Minh! Not against my window!” she shouted.

Dad came back on the line. “Handle your boat.”

He hung up. King overturned the table between us before I could pocket the phone. I jumped backward as it hit the floor, shoved the phone away, and brought my hands up.

He came through the gap with both fists moving. I slipped the first punch. The second hit my guard hard enough to drive my forearms into my chest. I stumbled back two steps.

Behind him, Saint had reached the last two guests.

“Out!” I shouted.

Saint looked at me. I nodded toward the deck doors, and he took the guests through them.

The lounge was empty. King rolled his neck until something popped. I backed toward an unbroken chair, took off the white jacket, folded it once, and laid it across the back.

King watched me.

“You think you're coming back for that?” he asked.

I loosened my collar. “My wife made it.”

He grinned. “Then I'll try not to bleed on it.”

The ferry hit a wake. The chandelier swung overhead, and the floor tilted beneath us. I planted my feet.

King came forward.

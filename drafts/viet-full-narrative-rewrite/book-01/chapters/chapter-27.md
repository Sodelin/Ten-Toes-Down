## Chapter 27: Places We Could Go

By autumn of 2027, I could eat for free in six places on Bell Street. At Denise's, I paid an irritation charge.

It appeared beneath the coffee on my receipt that afternoon. Saint leaned over my shoulder to inspect it. Outside the window, Hậu stood beside the man he'd hired to advertise the gym. The man wore a foam boxing glove on each hand. He couldn't pick up his coffee or get the gloves off, so Hậu held the cup to his mouth while delivering a speech I couldn't hear through the glass.

“That one's employed,” I told Denise, pointing outside.

“Employment isn't the same as an improvement,” Denise said. She put another plate beside mine anyway.

Saint tapped my receipt. “She charge you for breathing now?”

Denise heard him from the register. “He can stop. I'll take it off.”

I paid while Hậu tilted the cup a little farther and the man in the gloves tried to signal that it was enough.

Shawna arrived seven minutes late with a coat over one arm and a folder hugged against her chest. I knew the look on her face before she spoke. I'd seen it when a difficult seam finally sat right, and when she'd found a fabric she'd spent three weeks being told didn't exist.

“I got the gallery,” she told me.

I stood so quickly that my chair fell over. Two men by the window rose too, looking to me for the emergency.

“My wife got the gallery,” I told them.

One started clapping. The other joined him, and Saint supplied enough applause for the rest of our table.

Shawna caught my sleeve. “Sit down. You're making them think somebody died.”

“Let them clap,” Denise said from the counter. “Somebody in here worked.”

I picked up my chair. Shawna kissed my cheek before sitting beside me, then placed the folder between our cups. The cover had a little dent where she'd been gripping it.

The space was a former electrical showroom downtown, with pale brick, high windows, and enough room for her winter collection to move instead of standing still on a rack. The owner had asked about her brand, the deposit, and whether the ceiling was going to survive her husband.

“What'd you tell her about the ceiling?” I asked.

“I showed her the clothes from the cinema.” Shawna opened the folder. “She liked the clothes.”

Across the table, Saint shook his head. “Lady asking about structural damage, she selling coats. That's focus.”

Shawna turned the folder toward me. Drawings filled the first pages. Fabric samples were fastened beside names and dates; farther in, a floor plan marked where people would enter, where they would sit, and the path the models would take. The lights had their own page. So did the photographer.

I bent over a sketch of a coat I recognized from our bedroom door. In pencil it looked finished. At home I'd watched her take its sleeve off three times.

“He's going to photograph the fabric properly this time,” Shawna said, touching the lighting plan. “I don't want six beautiful black pieces that turn into one dark hole.”

“How many seats?” I asked.

Her finger stopped on the paper. “Aiden. Don't buy every seat.”

“Wasn't going to buy every seat,” I told her.

“You asked the same way you asked about the airplane,” Shawna said.

Saint leaned forward. “What airplane?”

“He tried to buy the seats on a flight because he didn't like a man's suitcase,” Shawna explained.

“The suitcase was leaking,” I reminded her.

“It was a plant,” Shawna told me.

“Plants have responsibilities,” I said.

She slid the seating plan free of the other pages. Names already occupied some of the little rectangles. I recognized a few; most were strangers.

“I want people to buy the clothes because they want to wear them,” Shawna said. “I want them to take them somewhere I haven't been. I want to see a photograph one day and recognize something I made before I recognize the person in it.”

I looked again at the unknown names. There was a place for me near the front.

“Two seats?” I asked her.

“Two,” Shawna told me. “And you can carry boxes on Friday.”

Hậu came inside carrying the empty coffee cup. Behind him, the man in the foam gloves waved at passing traffic with both hands, back at work.

“If you need security,” Hậu said, “I own my own equipment.”

On Friday, a woman with a clipboard met me inside the showroom before I'd finished saying hello. A man followed me through the door, trying to hand me a bottle of expensive liquor.

“Liquor in the kitchen,” she told him. “Boxes at the back.”

The man held out the bottle. “It's for Mr. Trần.”

“Then he can carry it after the boxes,” the woman told him.

Her name was Marisol. Shawna had hired her after an interview that had somehow involved fixing a cuff and reorganizing a delivery schedule. From the doorway, I could see the cuff repair on the jacket she wore. The delivery schedule occupied three pages of her clipboard.

Shawna came out from behind a clothes rack, saw where I was standing, and pointed upstairs. “Six cartons, please. The steamer's coming in a minute.”

I left the bottle with its owner and carried the cartons upstairs. When I came back down, a builder was adjusting a pedestal in the showroom. He asked me to move it three feet to the left. We moved it. He looked from the pedestal to the window, then asked for another foot.

The builder and I moved it again. Shawna came from behind a rack and stopped where the photographer would stand. She looked at the pedestal, then at the builder.

“It was right before you moved it,” Shawna told him. “Put it back.”

The builder looked at me across the pedestal. “You afraid of her?”

“I'm married to her,” I told him.

He considered that while we carried it back.

By the time the lights were arranged, I had moved the pedestal twice more. Marisol had a mark for it on the floor. I asked whether the mark was its permanent address, and she told me to stop standing on the delivery route.

The steamer arrived through the loading entrance beneath a security shutter stuck halfway up its runners. The driver crouched under it with the carton on his shoulder. Above him, the motor made a grinding noise.

I heard a hard little snap inside the housing. The shutter dropped.

I ducked beneath the bottom rail and caught it across my back. The driver shouted and let go of his carton. It hit the floor beside his shoes. For a moment he stayed bent there, looking from the box to the steel hanging over my shoulders.

“Move,” I told the driver.

He scrambled inside. The builder ran to the power switch while the motor continued pulling against me. When the grinding stopped, I shifted my palms beneath the rail and straightened enough to lift it into the upper frame. One corner kept trying to come down. I folded the damaged lip of its runner inward until it held the rail above the opening.

Marisol knelt beside the carton. “Can you check whether the steamer's damaged?” she asked the driver.

He stared at her. “The door almost killed me.”

“I saw,” Marisol said. “Are you hurt?”

He looked down at himself, patted his arms, and shook his head. Then he bent over the carton with her.

Shawna took my hands. A stripe of dirt crossed each palm. She turned them over, checked the backs, and kissed one hand.

“You're fixing that,” she told me, nodding at the shutter.

“I know somebody,” I told her.

The driver had begun to clap. Shawna looked toward him with my hand still in hers.

“After we open,” she told him. “Four hours.”

Dad was two streets away, attending to a refrigerator that, according to its owner, stopped working whenever the rent came due. He reached the showroom while I was still describing the shutter on the phone. I ended the call when I heard my own voice coming from his pocket.

He studied the bent metal above my head, then looked at the builder.

“You install this?” Dad asked him.

The builder shook his head quickly. “I'm doing the interior.”

“Good.” Dad set down his tools. “You still can.”

He disconnected the drive and had me take the weight while he worked the folded corner free. He pressed the bent guide straight with the heel of his hand, inspected the channel, and guided the shutter down. The builder watched Dad's hand for a while before remembering to bring over the ladder.

Once Dad had raised and lowered it manually, he tied it up out of the way and pointed at the damaged parts.

“New runner. New limit switch,” Dad told the builder. “Nobody uses it until both are here.”

Shawna offered to pay him. Dad wiped his hands, looked at the chairs beginning to fill the room, and asked for a seat.

“Of course,” Shawna said.

Dad nodded toward me. “He can stand.”

By the show, somebody had found enough chairs for both of us. Mine was in front, well beyond Saint's reach. That didn't stop him from trying to bring visitors across the room to greet me. Loan intercepted him with her folded program.

“She's working,” Loan told Saint. “You sit.”

“I'm helping,” Saint told her.

Loan struck his wrist with the program. He sat.

There were buyers I didn't know and stylists carrying bags that probably cost more than some of the cars I'd stolen. A woman beside the aisle inspected the seam of her own sleeve while waiting, running her thumb along it with the concentration I usually associated with concealed weapons. Estelle arrived in a suit with two friends. She found Shawna before she found her seat.

Our family occupied most of the second row. Mom sat beside Denise. Hậu had put his shoulders into the jacket Shawna had made him and held his arms away from his sides, afraid to damage it. Voss's sister helped him sit without catching the hem. Rafa held his clean hands away from his shirt, as though concerned that either might contaminate the other.

Great-Grandpa Tín, now a hundred and five, had brought peppermints and reading glasses. He unfolded the schedule, adjusted the glasses, and looked through it twice.

“Where's the food?” Tín asked Mom.

She pointed to the schedule. “Reception afterward.”

Tín lowered his glasses. “Ambitious plan.”

Hòa looked up from his own program toward the secured shutter. Dad, sitting nearby, followed the glance and nodded. Neither of them explained anything. I looked away before they could make me responsible for a third door.

The lights changed. People put down their conversations.

I'd seen the clothes at breakfast, on the backs of chairs, over Shawna's arm while she hunted for a missing pin. Now the first coat came toward me on someone I'd never met, moving beneath the lights she'd chosen. The fabric changed as the model turned. A seam I'd heard Shawna swear at for an entire evening lay exactly where she'd wanted it.

I looked toward the far doorway. Shawna stood there with Marisol, watching the garments. Marisol said something too quietly for me to hear. Shawna nodded, looked down at her list, and returned her attention to the next model.

I stayed in my chair. If anything went wrong, it was small enough that I didn't recognize it. Marisol crossed behind a rack once. A different model appeared in the doorway. The clothes kept coming.

At the end, Shawna stepped out as though she had only come to collect something. The applause caught her halfway into the room. She stopped, looked at the people on either side of the aisle, and then smiled.

I stood. This time no one needed to wonder why.

Shawna bowed. Mom was crying. Denise passed her a tissue and announced that the room had dust, which several people nearby politely accepted. Hậu clapped with his whole hands. Saint whistled until Loan touched his sleeve.

Behind me, Tín leaned toward Hòa. “That's a good one,” Tín said.

“All hers,” Hòa answered.

“I meant his wife,” Tín told him.

Afterward, people wanted photographs with Shawna. She stood beneath her own lights, holding flowers someone had brought from outside. I took a phone when somebody offered it, backed up until everyone fit, and waited for her to laugh.

Then I took the photograph. I took another for the next person. For once, being the man outside the picture felt exactly right.

The next morning, I found a prospectus on our kitchen table. A pale house with tall windows occupied the cover. The name beneath it was Aster House.

Shawna set two cups beside it. “The gallery owner gave me that and a list of people who want appointments. There's an exhibition coming up at Aster House.”

I opened it. The old part of the house looked as though every window remembered a grudge. Inside were photographs of a restored staircase, a chandelier, and rooms whose emptiness seemed expensive. The new wing was credited to An Vân and the architect Đặng Minh Khải.

“They've spent money on this,” I said.

Shawna leaned over my shoulder. “Quiet money. That's usually worse.”

On the plan, one room had a label but no photograph. I turned the page, then turned it back.

“Ninth Room,” I read aloud. “What's in there?”

“If I knew,” Shawna said, “I wouldn't be reading the brochure with you.”

I studied its little rectangle. Everything around it had a picture, a description, or a carefully chosen word about light. The Ninth Room had its name. I closed the prospectus when Shawna sat beside me.

“Thank you for yesterday,” she said.

“Moved that pedestal five times,” I reminded her.

“And stayed in your chair.” Shawna took my hand beneath the table. “One of your greatest feats.”

“Where am I on the list?” I asked her.

She leaned against me. “It's one of my favorites.”

We had no appointments for another hour. There was no present waiting outside. Valerie had the morning off. Saint's phone was silent because Loan had confiscated it after discovering that he'd tried to sell an unauthorized recording of the show to the photographer Shawna had already paid.

I kissed my wife. She tasted of coffee and the jam she'd eaten straight from the spoon while telling me she wasn't hungry. We took our cups to the sofa. She tucked her feet beneath her, leaned on my shoulder, and fell asleep before the coffee cooled.

I opened the photographs from the show. In one, Mom and Shawna were laughing over a sleeve. Their hands touched the same piece of fabric. Behind them, the rest of us had blurred into shapes and light.

I sent that one to the printer. Then I put my phone away and stayed until she woke.

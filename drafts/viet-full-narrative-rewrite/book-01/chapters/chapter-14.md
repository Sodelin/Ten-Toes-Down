## Chapter 14: Sunday Bowling

Sunday had rented every lane at Crown Bowl except lane seven.

An elderly couple occupied lane seven. The woman wore a purple bowling shirt with her name stitched across the back. Her husband waited beside her holding a ball, staring at the pins with disappointment that seemed older than the game.

“Why are they here?” Saint whispered as we came in.

Sunday heard him. “Mrs. Whitcomb has had Tuesdays at noon for thirty-one years. I offered to pay for another time.”

“And?” Saint asked.

“She offered to outlive me,” Sunday said.

Mrs. Whitcomb released her ball. It ran straight down the lane and knocked over nine pins. Her husband watched the survivor settle.

“Again,” he said.

“I can count, Frank,” Mrs. Whitcomb told him.

Sunday smiled and extended a hand. I looked at it without taking it.

“You sent men to my father's shop,” I said.

“Did I?” Sunday asked.

“One of them said your name while holding an engine,” I told him.

Sunday lowered his hand. “People use my name when they want to get out of things.”

“Then they should choose lighter things,” I said.

Two lanes away, Rhett King stood beside a ball return in a green polo shirt and dark trousers. His rented bowling shoes were red and blue. The shoes looked so small beneath the rest of him that Saint stopped walking.

King turned toward him. Saint became interested in the ceiling.

Sunday gestured to a table set behind the approaches. It held fried chicken, sandwiches, and a glass pitcher of lemonade. Plates had been arranged around it with more care than the occasion deserved.

“I said I'd bring plates,” Sunday reminded me.

“You also sent somebody a picture of my wife,” I said.

His expression stayed pleasant. “A beautiful house should be admired.”

I stepped closer. “Look somewhere else.”

The smile disappeared. For a moment he stood still enough for me to hear the return mechanism working behind him. Then Mrs. Whitcomb shouted at Frank, and Sunday glanced toward lane seven.

“Let's bowl,” he said.

I took off my jacket and laid it over a chair. The bandage showed beneath my rolled sleeve. Sunday looked at it and said nothing. King looked at it and smiled.

Hậu stayed near the food table with the bottle of water he'd brought. Saint accepted a sandwich, opened it just far enough to inspect the filling, and ate it anyway. Sunday selected a black ball from the rack.

“My uncle knew your great-grandfather,” Sunday told me.

“A lot of people did,” I said.

He fitted his fingers into the holes. “Duke Mercer.”

Hậu glanced toward me. I kept watching Sunday.

“I've heard the name,” I said.

“I'm sure the stories are flattering,” Sunday replied.

“Depends who's telling them,” I told him.

Sunday stepped onto the approach. His ball curved into the pocket, and all ten pins went down. He turned with both hands open.

“Your turn,” he said.

I chose a ball and sent it down the same lane. It struck a little high. Pins scattered, but the last one rocked twice and remained upright. King chuckled from beside the return.

Saint pointed at the standing pin. “That's defective.”

I picked up a second ball and knocked it down. Sunday had already seated himself beside the chicken.

“Your family has always understood spectacle,” he said as I came back. “Your great-grandfather put half this city on the river for a party and made the other half pay to look at it.”

“You weren't born,” I said.

“I was four,” Sunday told me.

“Then you had a very early business interest,” I replied.

He smiled. “I grew into it.”

From his jacket he took a folded brochure. On its cover, a white ferry with dark windows and a gold stripe crossed impossibly clean water. THE SUNDAY BEST appeared above the picture.

Saint leaned over the table. “You named a boat after yourself?”

“People remember,” Sunday said.

Saint nodded. “I respect that part.”

Sunday opened the brochure between the plates. Restaurants, private rooms, a performance deck, an upper lounge overlooking the water. The photographs made the ferry look like a hotel somebody had pushed into the harbor.

“The city likes tourism,” Sunday said. “Visitors like feeling they've discovered something. My guests like games the city doesn't need to know about.”

“And you need the Circle's guests,” I told him.

“I need you,” Sunday said.

I looked up from the pictures. “That's an expensive sentence.”

“I can afford it,” he replied.

He put an envelope beside the brochure. Saint made a quiet sound before catching himself. I left the envelope where it was.

“Appearances?” I asked.

“An opening night. A few private dinners,” Sunday said. “Your name attached to the upper room. You sit, people come, everybody gets richer.”

“And the men with guns?” I asked him.

“Security,” Sunday said.

“At my wife's show?” I asked.

He held my gaze. “Vinh was ambitious.”

“Vinh was paid,” I said.

Sunday turned the envelope slightly with one finger. “So are most people.”

King picked up a bowling ball in his right hand. He didn't use the holes. His fingers spread over the surface, and he squeezed until a crack opened through the shell.

Hậu stopped chewing. King squeezed again. A piece broke free and struck the floor beside his rented shoe.

Sunday sighed. “Rhett. They charge for those.”

King looked at me. “Just checking the equipment.”

I took what remained of the ball from his hand. The broken shell felt rough against my fingers. I turned it once, set it at the start of the lane, and nudged it forward with my foot.

It wobbled away crookedly. Halfway down, it caught the gutter edge and bounced inward. It struck the far-right pin, which hit the next. The line collapsed across the rack until the head pin fell last.

Ten.

Mrs. Whitcomb put her own ball down and looked at me.

“Do that again,” she said.

I watched the broken ball disappear into the mechanism. “I'd rather not test my luck.”

She pointed at Frank. “See? Some men know when to stop.”

Saint applauded loudly enough to fill the building. King stepped toward me, and Sunday rose just far enough to put a hand on his chest.

“We're having lunch,” Sunday told him.

I sat opposite Sunday and pushed the envelope back across the table.

“No,” I said.

He looked down at it. “You haven't counted.”

“I'm very good at counting,” I told him.

“Then name a number,” Sunday said.

“One,” I replied. “You get one more chance to stop sending people near my family.”

Sunday leaned back. For a while he watched the lane rather than me. Behind him, Frank tried to explain something to his wife. She kept her eyes on the pins.

“Bảo is still alive,” Sunday said eventually.

“Yes,” I told him.

“His son has friends. His managers have habits,” Sunday continued. “A room can applaud you and still call somebody else afterward.”

“Then I should stay busy,” I said.

“You should stay employed,” Sunday told me.

I stood. King moved between me and the exit, close enough that his shoulder blocked my view of the doors. Hậu put his water on the table and rose. Saint stopped chewing.

Mrs. Whitcomb looked over from lane seven.

“Move away from the approach,” she told King. “People are playing.”

He ignored her. She picked up her ball. Sunday looked at her face and stood too.

“Rhett,” Sunday said. “Let him pass.”

King moved half a step. There was enough space to squeeze past him if I turned sideways and accepted what the movement was for.

I walked straight ahead.

My shoulder met his chest. He planted his feet. I kept moving, and the rubber soles of his rented shoes squealed across the polished floor. One inch, then another. His right hand closed around my upper arm, exactly where the bandage covered the stitches.

Pain flashed white behind my eyes. I caught his thumb with my good hand and bent it back until his grip loosened. Then I drove my forehead into his face.

His nose flattened with a blunt crack. He staggered backward into the ball rack, tipping it. Six balls spilled across the floor. Sunday pushed his chair away as King roared and came off the rack toward me.

I kicked one of the loose balls beneath King's leading foot. His shoe rolled over it. He caught himself against the lunch table with his right hand, which went through the top.

Plates jumped. The lemonade pitcher fell sideways and emptied into his lap. Hậu caught Saint by the back of his collar and pulled him clear of the food.

I stepped away and collected my jacket. On lane seven, Mrs. Whitcomb bowled her spare. The last pin fell.

“Finally,” she said.

We left Sunday standing beside his broken lunch. Outside, Saint bent forward with his hands on his knees and laughed until he couldn't breathe. Hậu held half a sandwich out to him.

“You dropped this,” Hậu said.

Saint took it gratefully. I looked down at my bandage. A thin red line was spreading through the cloth.

“Fuck,” I said.

Hậu saw it. “We should get that checked.”

“I know,” I told him.

He looked back toward the bowling alley. “He didn't use the other hand.”

“Who?” Saint asked around his sandwich.

“King,” Hậu said.

I'd noticed too. King had squeezed the ball, grabbed my arm, and caught the table with his right hand. His left had stayed near his body. At the truck, when the chain failed, I'd seen that side strike the broken ramp. Whatever he'd hurt there hadn't healed.

I put my jacket on carefully. “We'll remember,” I told Hậu.

Back on Bell Street, Trinh waited outside the Pearl with a man who owned three card rooms. He wanted to put all three beneath my name by the weekend. He'd brought his keys in a jewelry box. I sent him to Trinh and went upstairs to call Shawna.

She listened without interrupting. When I finished, she returned to the detail I'd hoped to pass over quickly.

“You reopened the stitches,” Shawna said.

“A little,” I told her.

“The doctor said a week,” she reminded me.

“It felt longer at the bowling alley,” I said.

“Come home,” Shawna told me.

I promised I would. Hậu went to collect food from Bishop's, with Saint beside him still explaining how we could turn the broken-ball strike into a commercial. I began packing my black machine into its case.

Tires shrieked outside. Then Saint shouted my name.

I reached the window in two strides. A gray van stood sideways in the street with its sliding door open. Three men were dragging Hậu toward it. He'd planted one foot against the curb and locked both hands around the doorframe.

King stood behind him holding a short black club in his right hand. He struck Hậu in the back of the head. Hậu's hands opened, and the men pulled him inside.

I went through the stained-glass window.

Blue glass broke around my shoulders. I landed on a parked car, crushing its roof beneath both feet, and jumped down into the road. The van's side door slammed. Its tires caught as I reached the rear.

I seized the handle. For a moment the van pulled against my grip. Then the mounting tore free, leaving the metal in my hand. The van accelerated around the corner before I could catch the body.

Saint staggered toward me with blood on his lip and his sleeve torn at the shoulder.

“They took him,” he said.

I looked down at the broken handle. Then I turned and threw it through the empty space where the Pearl's window had been.

## Chapter 12: Membership Fees

The doctor put nine stitches in my arm. Halfway through, he asked what had happened.

“A fashion event,” I said.

He looked at Shawna. She nodded from the chair beside the examination table.

“Very competitive industry,” she told him.

He didn't laugh. When he'd finished, he wrapped the arm and told me to avoid heavy lifting for at least a week. Shawna looked at me before I could offer an opinion.

“He heard you,” she said.

The doctor handed the instructions to her.

We got home after two. Shawna helped me out of the shirt Rafa had brought. It had lasted less than one evening. She put it in a bag for repairs, looking at the cut fabric as though she intended to have a separate argument with it later, and made me drink water before I could ask for anything else.

“Did the orders come through?” I asked.

She sat on the edge of the bed and checked her phone. “Twenty-three confirmed. Four deposits already paid. Estelle ordered two more dresses.”

“Good,” I said.

Shawna scrolled a little farther. “One woman wants the coat you used in the fight.”

“The actual coat?” I asked.

“She said it has provenance now,” Shawna told me.

I laughed and stopped when the movement pulled at my arm. Shawna put the phone down, leaned over me carefully, and kissed the unbruised side of my mouth.

“You looked good before they shot at us,” she said.

“I looked good after,” I told her.

“You looked expensive after,” Shawna replied.

She switched off the lamp. I reached for her with my good arm, and she settled against me, warm and tired. For a few hours, nobody summoned me anywhere or brought me anything made of bronze.

Sunday morning brought twelve flower arrangements, cufflinks, and tickets to a private aquarium. Saint carried the last envelope upstairs while we were still in bed.

“The fish community is reaching out,” he announced from the doorway.

Shawna pulled the blanket over her head. “Make him leave.”

I pointed back the way he'd come. Saint left the tickets on the counter and departed with two pastries he claimed had been addressed to him.

By afternoon, the Pearl belonged to me on paper. Trinh brought the completed documents to the apartment. Bảo had signed them. Along with the building and rooms, the papers covered the games and her continuing share in the operation, which I'd insisted she keep.

She read that section twice before looking up.

“You didn't have to do that,” Trinh said.

“You run the room,” I told her.

“Bảo paid me to run it,” she said.

“I'm paying you to care whether it burns down,” I replied.

Trinh lowered the folder onto her lap. For a moment she looked at the signature on the last page.

“He's going to ask you to kneel tonight,” she told me.

I looked up. “Vinh mentioned it.”

“He's making it a ceremony,” Trinh said. “Cameras. Witnesses. He gives you the keys in front of everyone. You thank him. You accept the Circle's authority.”

“On my knees,” I said.

She nodded. Shawna came out of the bedroom carrying a jacket she'd begun altering to fit over the bandage.

“Is this a thing people actually do?” Shawna asked.

“It's a thing Bảo saw in a film,” Trinh told her. “He liked the furniture.”

I leaned back in the chair. “Does he have a throne?”

Trinh paused too long. I closed my eyes.

“Of course he has a throne,” I said.

We ate at my parents' house before going to Honest Work. Dad looked at my bandage, then at my clean shirt, and let me sit down without asking. Mom waited until I'd taken the vegetables before demanding the story. I put them back down and explained what had happened at the cinema.

Great-Grandpa Tín listened through the gunman, the knife, and the falling screen. When I finished, he leaned toward Shawna.

“The woman in the silver dress kept both shoes?” he asked.

“One came off,” Shawna told him.

Tín nodded. “Then she had a real evening.”

Granddad Hòa sat beside the window, peeling an apple with the little knife he carried everywhere. The skin came away in one long curl. When I mentioned Bảo's ceremony, he cut the strip neatly onto his plate and looked up.

“Take a handkerchief,” Hòa said.

“Why?” I asked him.

He glanced at my bandage. “Something always spills.”

Dad passed the vegetables back to me.

“Car has fuel?” he asked.

“Full tank,” I said.

“Good,” Dad told me.

Nobody told me not to go. Shawna stayed with Mom to work through her orders. Before I left, she checked that my sleeve wasn't pulling against the bandage.

“If you come home wearing a crown, I'm charging you for storing it,” she said.

I promised to remember and went to collect the others.

Honest Work had hired a red carpet. It ran from the curb through the gym doors to a wooden arch covered with gold flowers. Saint stopped beneath the first row of petals.

“He stole my concept,” Saint said.

“You were selling shirts,” I told him.

Saint straightened a flower that had turned sideways. “It's a developing concept.”

Hậu wore a dark suit that strained across his shoulders. Voss followed him, moving carefully but refusing the chair I'd offered to bring from the car. Trinh walked on my right.

Inside, the boxing ring had become a stage. The ropes were gone, leaving the platform open except for the short handrail beside its access steps. A large carved chair stood at the far end among white lilies. Bảo occupied it with his injured arm concealed beneath a dark jacket. Baojun stood behind him. Beside the platform, Phương had chosen an ordinary chair and looked far more comfortable.

People rose as we entered. Applause spread through the gym. Men called my name. Near the bar, a woman held up her phone and asked me to turn so she could photograph the good side of my jacket.

Bảo clapped slowly from his chair, smiling through it.

Saint leaned toward my ear. “This feels like a wedding where both men hate the cake.”

Trinh took a place near the front. Hậu and Voss stayed by the steps while I went up alone. The platform still had enough give to remind me of the ring beneath all the flowers.

Bảo stood to meet me.

“Aiden Trần,” he announced to the room. “A son of a great family. A brave man. A generous man.”

“Depends what you're asking for,” I said.

The guests laughed. Bảo waited, then took a ring of oversized keys from Baojun. They were gold-plated and large enough to hurt somebody. I'd already put the useful set in my pocket.

“The Pearl,” Bảo said, lifting the ceremonial keys. “A reward for loyalty.”

I held out my hand. He kept them beyond it.

“First, respect,” Bảo told me.

The room quieted. He pointed at a red velvet cushion laid before the chair, gold fringe spread neatly along its edge.

I looked at the cushion and then at him. “Your knees hurt?”

A few people laughed. Bảo didn't.

“You understand,” he said.

“I understand the building's already transferred,” I told him.

He stepped closer and lowered his voice. “And I can make that very difficult.”

I reached into my inside pocket and took out the original hundred-thousand-dollar check. A faint broth stain remained at one corner. Bảo watched me fold it, tear it in half, and place both pieces on the arm of his chair.

“Refund,” I said.

Baojun moved first. He came around the chair with a pistol pointed at my stomach. I caught his wrist in my right hand, turned the muzzle toward the ceiling, and squeezed until his fingers opened. My left hand caught the falling gun. I set it beside the torn check before its weight could pull at the stitches.

Baojun dropped to his knees, clutching his wrist. The red cushion caught him neatly.

“There,” I said, looking down. “Somebody's using it.”

Laughter burst through the room. Several guests clapped hands over their mouths after the sound had already escaped. Bảo's face darkened.

“Enough,” he said.

Three men moved toward the steps behind me. Hậu met the first as he reached the top. He wrapped both arms around the man's waist and lifted him backward off the platform. They landed on a table, which collapsed beneath them. Hậu got up holding the man's belt and deposited him beside the broken legs.

The second man swung at Voss. Voss caught his punching arm, ducked beneath it, and drove him shoulder-first into the short rail beside the steps. Pain tightened Voss's swollen jaw. He kept hold until the man stopped struggling.

The third reached the platform with a knife held low against his thigh. I stepped on his leading foot and punched him in the nose. He jerked backward, but the trapped foot stayed under mine. I caught his knife wrist and slammed it against the carved chair arm.

Wood split. The knife fell between the cushions. I drove my good elbow into his ribs. As he doubled over, I turned him toward the steps and sent him down them face-first.

My stitched arm burned. I held it close to my side and turned back in time to see Bảo reaching for his son's pistol.

I put my right hand over his before he could lift it. We stood almost shoulder to shoulder, with his good hand trapped against the chair and Baojun still kneeling below us.

“You could have handed me the keys,” I told Bảo.

He strained against my grip. I pressed down harder. One of his rings bent against the carved wood, and he stopped pulling.

“Let go,” Bảo said.

“Drop the gun,” I told him.

His fingers opened. I took the pistol and slid it across the platform toward Trinh. She stepped up to collect it, keeping the muzzle pointed at the floor.

The room was standing now. Some people held drinks. Others held phones. Most looked at Bảo before looking back at me.

“Anyone else?” I asked.

Nobody moved. Then the jeweler raised a cautious hand near the bar. I turned toward him.

“Photograph?” he asked.

The room broke into laughter again. Bảo lowered himself heavily into the damaged chair. Baojun crawled toward him, still holding his wrist.

Phương crossed the platform and helped her son stand. She looked at her husband.

“We're leaving,” Phương said.

“Phương—” Bảo began.

“You paid for flowers,” she told him. “Use the evening.”

She guided Baojun down the steps. Bảo watched them go, then looked around the room. Phones remained raised. The table lay broken beside the platform. His men were still where Hậu, Voss, and I had left them.

He picked up the ceremonial keys and put them in my hand.

Applause began at the back and spread forward. It grew louder as Bảo walked down the steps. Trinh came to stand beside me. Hậu climbed up brushing splinters from his suit, with Voss following more slowly.

Saint appeared holding a microphone.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” he said, “please give the previous management a respectful amount of room to leave.”

Bảo stopped at the door and looked back. I held his gaze until he turned away. Then he was gone.

Money began arriving before the applause ended. Men came up with envelopes, checks, and business cards folded around cash. Someone offered a yacht for my use. Another offered a racehorse, which I refused on my wife's behalf. I set the enormous gold keys among the flowers and kept accepting envelopes.

Hậu looked at the growing pile on the chair.

“How much is that?” he asked.

Trinh glanced at it. “Enough to replace the table.”

Saint put the plastic crown from the apartment on my head. I took it off. He put it back before I could lower my hand.

“For the picture, nigga,” Saint said.

I let the jeweler take one photograph. Then I handed the crown to Hậu, checked that the actual keys were still in my pocket, and looked toward the doors through which Bảo had left.

My phone rang with an unknown number. I answered.

“Congratulations,” Sunday Mercer said, warm and amused. “I hear you found a chair.”

The crowd behind me was still cheering.

“It's a little damaged,” I told him.

“They always are by the time you get them,” Sunday said.

“What do you want, Sunday?” I asked.

“Breakfast,” he replied. “Just you and me.”

Beside me, Hậu had put on the plastic crown. One point was already bent against his hair.

“Pick somewhere with good plates,” I told Sunday.

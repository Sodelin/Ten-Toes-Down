## Chapter 8: Everybody Wants a Picture

Bảo held his car door open for me. For a moment I heard my father more clearly than the music coming down the Pearl's stairs: do not get into Bảo's car.

“We can talk here,” I said.

We stood under the striped awning, with Bảo nearest the curb and Vinh beside the entrance. Upstairs, somebody had found the little piano. He was attacking a song enthusiastically, although the tune kept escaping him. People laughed through the open window. From where they stood, nothing much had changed.

Vinh had replaced his maroon shirt with a black one. When he moved his hand, light caught the watch I'd put in his pocket.

“You got it fitted,” I told him, pointing.

He looked down and tucked the wrist behind his back.

Bảo tapped the top of the open door. “Private conversation.”

“Your man threatened my wife,” I said. “We've gone public.”

Vinh took a step toward me. Bảo extended an arm across his chest without turning his head, and Vinh stopped against it.

“I'm told you stole a truck,” Bảo said.

“It was carrying my statue,” I replied.

Bảo studied my face. “Your statue.”

“Mine,” I said.

“The one with my face,” he said, still studying me.

“We're making changes,” I told him.

He let out one short laugh through his nose. Upstairs the piano player tried the beginning again. Bảo waited for him to get past the same difficult bar.

“Come to my house tomorrow,” Bảo said. “We can resolve this.”

I took out my phone. “You should look at something tonight.”

I'd queued the recording upstairs. Now I held the screen between us and let Vinh speak for himself. In the video, he sat comfortably across from Kenny, with the red box on the table. Neither man had to move out of anyone's way.

“She's the designer,” the recorded Vinh said. “Useful until the doors open.”

Bảo's smile disappeared.

Beside him, Vinh struck my phone with an open hand. I caught his wrist as the phone hit the paving and skidded beneath the car. Its speaker scraped through another piece of the conversation before falling silent.

“You should've watched the rest,” I told him.

He pulled backward. I kept his wrist at chest height and tightened my grip until his fingers opened. His free hand twitched near his belt.

Bảo looked down at the trapped hand, then up at him. “Vinh.”

“He made it,” Vinh said quickly. “He's the computer boy. He makes pictures talk.”

“That's a very nervous sentence,” I said.

Vinh drove his other fist toward my throat. I turned outside the punch, drew the wrist I held across his chest, and pushed him backward. His shoulder hit the open car door. The hinges groaned as his weight forced it wider.

Bảo stepped around the door and backward into the road.

An engine revved downhill. I looked past his shoulder. A dark sedan was coming toward us without lights, too fast for the parked cars crowding the street. Its passenger window was open. Inside the black rectangle, a gun barrel rose.

I let go of Vinh, seized Bảo's jacket, and threw him toward the concrete planter beside the Pearl's entrance. His heels left the curb. He landed hard behind it as the first shots struck his car.

Vinh dropped flat beneath the open door. I jumped over his legs and came down beside Bảo, catching the back of his head to pull it below the planter's rim. More shots broke the lights along the awning. Glass pattered on my shoulders. Upstairs, the piano stopped in the middle of a chord.

The sedan passed us. I saw its rear window above the rim and stood. A heavy clay pot sat inside the concrete surround, packed tight with soil and an unfortunate shrub. I tore it free with both hands and threw it after the car.

It struck the rear window squarely. Glass collapsed inward. The pot burst through, filling the back seat with soil, clay, and most of the shrub. The sedan swerved into a parked pickup, bounced off its rear corner, and kept going. One back wheel wobbled under the damaged fender.

I turned toward the car door. Vinh was already on his feet.

He ran past the next storefront and into the alley beside the pharmacy. Something bright skipped from his arm into the gutter. By the time I reached the curb, he'd disappeared between the buildings. The watch lay faceup in dirty water, its strap broken.

Bảo was still seated behind the planter. Blood ran between the rings on the hand he pressed against his upper arm.

“He shot you,” I said.

Bảo looked at his hand. “I noticed.”

Hậu came through the door at a run, with Trinh close behind him. In the upstairs window, Saint's face appeared between two pieces of broken glass. He looked at the awning, looked at the road, and vanished.

“Ambulance,” Trinh said into her phone.

Bảo caught her sleeve with his bloody hand. “No.”

She lowered the phone just far enough to stare at him. He pointed toward his car.

“My doctor,” Bảo said.

“Your car is full of holes,” Trinh told him.

Hậu opened the rear door on the side away from the Pearl. He leaned in, checked the seat, and brushed glass off the upholstery.

“This side's mostly okay,” he reported.

I helped Bảo up. With dirt in his hair and one arm held tight to his ribs, he seemed suddenly smaller than the man who'd opened the door for me. He straightened before we reached the car and pulled his jacket into place with his good hand. Blood darkened the white cuff.

“Come with me,” Bảo said.

“No,” I replied.

He stopped beside the rear seat. “Aiden.”

“You need a doctor,” I told him. “I need Vinh.”

Anger tightened his face. Then he looked past me at the gutted planter and the bullet marks stippling his door. He got into the car.

“Tomorrow,” Bảo said.

I crouched by the curb before Hậu started the engine. My phone had come to rest just inside the front tire, protected by the wheel from everything after its first fall. I slid it out by the cracked corner of its case. The screen lit when I pressed the button. Behind me, Trinh climbed in beside Bảo with a folded bar towel for his arm.

Hậu drove them away. The damaged door rattled all the way to the corner.

I called Shawna. She answered before the second ring.

“I heard,” she said.

“Stay at Mom's,” I told her.

“I am at your mother's,” Shawna said. “She's looking for her car keys.”

“Don't let her leave,” I said.

“You tell her that.” Shawna handed over the phone before I could propose somebody with more authority.

“Are you bleeding?” Mom asked.

“No,” I said.

“Is Darius?” she asked.

I looked up. Saint had returned to the window in a reflective vest. He seemed to be organizing people on the far side of it, where the glass hadn't broken.

“No,” I told Mom.

“Good. Stop standing in the road,” she said.

Half a block away, she stood in the printshop doorway with Shawna beside her. I stepped onto the sidewalk. Mom lowered her phone only after both my feet were over the curb.

A man from the card room came out behind me carrying a leather envelope.

“Mr. Trần,” he said.

“Not now,” I told him.

He laid the envelope on the planter anyway, taking care to find a place without loose soil.

“For getting Mr. Lâm out of the way,” the man explained.

I stared at him. “People were shooting.”

“Yes. I saw,” he said. “That's why it's a generous amount.”

He hurried back upstairs before I could find a reply. Saint came down with a dustpan and broom, keeping the broom sideways to avoid the man.

“Everybody's asking whether the party is over,” Saint told me.

“The party is over,” I said.

He set the dustpan against the curb. “I said that. They asked whether you're coming to the next one.”

“Darius,” I said.

Saint began sweeping glass toward the pan. “I'm just reporting demand, nigga.”

We closed the Pearl. Trinh's aunt thanked each guest on the way to the stairs and reminded several of them about their drinks. She collected what she could before allowing the line to move. Nobody was permitted to use the gunfire as a reason to lose track of a tab.

By ten the street had quieted. Police took statements from people whose eyesight had failed at almost precisely the same moment. I waited beside the planter while one officer examined the pickup. Across the road, clay fragments lay among the sedan's window glass. The shrub had stayed with the car.

One detective remained after the other officers left. She was in her forties, with short dark hair and a gray jacket. She introduced herself as Elena Pike. A black notebook rested closed in her hand.

“You threw that?” Pike asked, looking across the street.

“The car was leaving,” I said.

“Most people wave,” she told me.

“They'd been rude,” I said.

Her gaze moved to the tape on my ear. “You seem to be having a difficult week.”

“It's Wednesday,” I pointed out.

“Then pace yourself,” Pike said.

She gave me her card. Behind me, Saint climbed onto a chair and began cutting the ruined awning fabric loose with kitchen scissors. Pike watched a strip descend beside his reflective vest.

“You know Leon Mercer?” she asked.

“He gave me shoes,” I said.

“That's friendly,” Pike observed.

“We didn't exchange sizes,” I told her.

She held my gaze for a moment, then put the notebook away without opening it.

“Call if you remember anything useful,” Pike said.

Before getting into her car, she looked up at the blue heron in the Pearl's window. I watched her drive away, then went down the block to the printshop.

Shawna had silver fabric across her lap and the largest worktable to herself. Her portable sewing machine hummed beneath the overhead lamps. Mom had cleared a path around the cloth. Denise sat near the television with a packet of crackers, telling a host what was wrong with his presentation.

I stayed in the doorway until Shawna looked up.

“You're doing the sad face,” she said.

“I have several,” I told her.

She stopped the machine. “This is the one where you want me to be mad so you can stop waiting.”

Denise turned up the television. I came over and sat opposite my wife.

“We should move the show,” I said.

“I've already moved it,” Shawna told me.

“Where?” I asked.

She turned her notebook around. The old cinema on Alder Street filled the page, three blocks inland from the Pearl. She'd marked the aisle and backstage space and crossed out the rows of seats she wanted removed. Beside the drawing she'd begun a list of measurements.

“The owner owes your mother for menus,” Shawna said.

“It's a cinema,” I said.

“He had a restaurant before that,” she explained.

From the back room, Mom called, “And a terrible understanding of invoices.”

Shawna tapped the page. “Nobody gets the new address until Friday. The Pearl stays on the printed invitation. Your mother is handling the corrected cards.”

The fabric across her knees caught the light when she shifted. This morning it had belonged in her own window, on her own table. Now she had to keep one elbow clear of Mom's paper cutter.

“You still want to do it?” I asked.

“I have six clients, four models, a photographer, and a woman who has paid me enough to make her shoulder a landmark,” Shawna said. “Yes.”

I reached across the table for her hand. “I'll keep them away.”

She squeezed my fingers once. “Keep them away from everyone. I'm not the only person with a window.”

Then she let go, gathered the silver cloth, and fed its edge beneath the needle. I stayed in the chair while she worked.

Mom brought me rice and set it at the far end of the table, beyond any chance of a stray grain reaching the dress. Denise wanted to know whether I intended to pay for her flowerpot. When I said I would, she told me she hadn't decided which price she remembered paying for it. I ate carefully and waited for the memory to improve.

At eleven, Hậu returned with soap on his hands and a bloodstain drying on his sleeve. He reported that Bảo was alive: the bullet had passed through the fleshy part of his upper arm. The doctor had ordered rest. According to Hậu, Bảo had immediately tried to fire two people from the treatment chair.

“He wants you at his house at noon,” Hậu said.

“I know,” I told him.

“He wants Vinh before then,” Hậu added.

“So do I,” I said.

He sat beside me, rolling the stained cuff away from his hand. For a while he listened to the sewing machine.

“Vinh called me,” Hậu said.

I put down my bowl. “When?”

“At the doctor's,” Hậu replied. “Said I should remember who gave me work.”

“What did you say?” I asked.

Hậu looked at Shawna, who was guiding a seam past the needle. “I said I was busy.”

“Will he call again?” I asked him.

“Probably,” Hậu said.

I nodded. “Answer.”

He turned back toward me. “You want me to tell him where you are?”

“Tell him you want your job back,” I said.

Hậu held my eyes for a moment. Then he leaned back far enough for his chair to creak.

“I should ask for a raise,” he said.

“Absolutely,” I agreed.

Denise turned away from the television. “Get dental.”

Saint arrived carrying a framed photograph so large he had to angle it through the door. We watched him set it on the floor with its face against his shins.

“Before you get upset,” he said, “this was already paid for.”

He turned it around. In the photograph I hung in midair above Voss, both feet clear of the ring. On the balcony behind me, a man stared with a shrimp halfway to his mouth. BELL STREET'S OWN ran beneath us in gold letters.

Shawna studied the photograph. Her hand went to her mouth. A small laugh escaped between her fingers, and she tried to hold the next one in. Then she looked again at the shrimp and folded forward over the edge of the table, laughing too hard to speak.

Mom came out to see what had happened. Saint tilted the frame toward her. She started laughing too. Denise removed her glasses and wiped her eyes with the corner of her sleeve. The machine stood silent, its needle poised in Shawna's unfinished seam.

“How much?” I asked Saint.

“For you? Free,” he said.

He leaned toward me while my wife laughed behind her hand.

“For the shrimp nigga,” Saint added, “we're negotiating.”

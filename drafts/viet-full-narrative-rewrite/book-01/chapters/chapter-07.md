## Chapter 7: The Easy Problem

The man hung up before I could ask whether Sunday school offered refunds. I called back and got a disconnected-number message. Outside the atelier, his employee was still in the recycling cart, so I doubted the organization had entirely ceased trading.

Saint looked over my shoulder at the phone. “Fast service.”

I put it away. Hậu crouched beside the cutting table, lifted the unconscious man beneath the arms, and dragged him toward the door. Mom directed two men carrying plywood past him. They stopped, waited for the shoes to clear, and continued to the broken window.

Estelle finished her call in the corner and came over.

“My brother says he knows nothing about this,” she told me.

“Does he?” I asked.

“He knows I'm here,” Estelle said. “That should concern somebody.”

She gave Shawna a card and offered her driver. Shawna indicated Mom, now correcting the angle of the plywood with both hands.

“I'm going next door,” Shawna said.

“Then he'll carry things.” Estelle pressed the card into her hand. “He works for me.”

Nia put the damaged dress back into its bag. The two women left together, passing the recycling cart without slowing. The lid had risen far enough to expose a furious eye. Estelle looked at it. The lid settled.

I remembered that as I followed Hậu outside.

We took the red box and drive to the Pearl. Trinh locked the upstairs door behind us while her aunt continued counting bottles beneath the bar. I laid the broken lid on a card table and told Trinh what had happened.

“Shawna?” she asked at once.

“Unhurt,” I said.

Trinh put down the tray in her hands. “What do you need?”

“Table. Power outlet. Somewhere Saint can't touch anything,” I said.

“I heard that,” Saint said from behind me.

“System works,” I told him.

Rafa brought my equipment case over from the apartment. It fitted beneath an airline seat, although security people generally liked to discuss it first. I opened it on the felt, unfolded the screen, and connected the compact black machine beside it. There was no manufacturer's name. I'd marked the cables with tape because knowing mathematics didn't stop me plugging the wrong one in when I was tired.

Saint stood on the other side of the table, turning his head to look for a feature I'd failed to advertise.

“That's the famous thing?” he asked.

“One of them,” I said.

“Expected more lights.” Saint leaned forward as I connected the power.

“Built it for work.” I moved his drink off the felt.

“Always missing the merchandise,” he told me.

Trinh came beside the screen. “What does it do?”

“Finds answers,” I said.

“Google does that.” She watched me connect the drive.

“Google asks whether you meant another question,” I replied.

When I was twenty, I'd proved P equaled NP. The argument had occupied notebooks, then a paper, then several months of people assuring other people it must be wrong. Building a machine that could use it had taken another year. That year now sat on green felt between Saint's fingerprints and a sandwich Hậu had brought in a napkin.

“What's NP?” Hậu asked.

“Nondeterministic polynomial time,” I said, checking the screen connection.

Hậu considered that. “Could've said you didn't want to tell me.”

The screen brightened. A silver star rotated above a box requesting a passphrase.

“Try password,” Saint suggested.

I looked up at him.

“Works on my aunt's Wi-Fi,” he said, defensively.

I set the machine its problem. The fan rose to a thin, irritated whine. The loose corner of Hậu's napkin fluttered toward it. He put one finger on the paper while we waited.

Thirteen seconds later the star disappeared. A list of files took its place.

Saint looked from the screen to the machine. “That's it?”

“That's it,” I said.

“You solved a thing with letters in it to open a folder?” Saint asked.

“Organizes photographs too,” I told him.

He rested both hands on the table. “You need a publicist so badly.”

I opened the first image. Bảo sat at a table in a white suit, signing a document. Someone mostly outside the frame held the paper flat. In the next image the same man stood beside Vinh, facing the camera.

He was the tall man I'd left on Shawna's floor. Seeing him upright in clean clothes made the photograph seem older than it had any reason to be.

Trinh leaned close enough for her shoulder to touch mine.

“Curtis Ames,” she said. “Used to drive for Vinh.”

I opened a video. The camera looked past a bottle toward a restaurant booth. Vinh sat on the left, Kenny Vale on the right. Between them lay the red box, its lid still intact.

In the recording, Kenny tapped the box. “Bảo gets the stage. Mercer gets the water. What do I get?”

Vinh slid an envelope across the table. Kenny opened it, counted a few notes, and grinned.

“I like water,” the recorded Kenny said.

They went on to discuss dates, names, and a private show at the Pearl. I turned up the sound. Vinh described where Bảo would stand when the guests arrived: in front of the stained-glass window. Kenny wanted to know whether the wife would be there too.

“She's the designer,” Vinh said from the speaker. “Useful until the doors open.”

I stopped the recording.

The fan whined into the silence. Saint had taken his hands off the table. Trinh turned toward the blue heron spreading across the window above us.

“Saturday,” she said.

“Shawna's show.” I looked from the frozen image to the stage she'd measured earlier.

Trinh nodded.

Hậu gripped the back of a chair. Wood cracked beneath his fingers. He looked down, let go, and said, “Vinh wanted extra security here. Asked me who had keys.”

I copied the files onto my own storage before disconnecting the drive. Vinh remained on the screen, smiling above the money. I wanted him to finish the conversation to my face. First I needed Bảo to hear what his man was selling.

“Call him,” I told Trinh. “Before anybody else gets to him.”

“He'll be back tonight.” She took out her phone.

Her aunt came around the bar and studied the machine.

“Can it find my brother?” she asked.

All four of us turned toward her.

“Owes me two thousand,” she explained. “Nobody's dead. Don't make those faces.”

Saint pulled out a chair. “Auntie, we may have a premium service.”

I shut the machine down before he could name the price.

Trinh couldn't reach Bảo. She left a message and called the man responsible for his airport car. While they spoke, I packed the drive and its contents back into the case. The broken red lid would no longer close properly. I set it on top, keeping the card and heron inside.

“Flight's delayed,” Trinh told me, covering her phone. Then she uncovered it. “Why?”

She listened for almost a minute, shut her eyes, and pinched the bridge of her nose.

“No,” Trinh said into the phone. “Nobody can pay extra to touch him.”

Saint's head rose. I pointed at him until he lowered it again.

Trinh wanted to postpone the evening. I told her to open. If Vinh expected me to hide the box and wait for an older man to tell me what to do, I wanted him disappointed. I also wanted him to come where we could see him. I put on a fresh jacket and stood beneath the heron, knowing perfectly well which piece of glass I'd chosen.

The first guests arrived at seven.

Saint took a microphone from the stage. “Please respect the man's personal space. Photographs welcome. Flash photography, fifteen dollars extra.”

Trinh took it from his hand. “Flash photography is free.”

He followed her toward the bar. “In front of customers. You keep doing this in front of customers.”

The room filled in half an hour. People brought envelopes, introductions, and reasons I ought to remember their names. Several women admired my jacket and then asked about Shawna's. I gave out her cards until my pocket was empty. She'd spent the day holding her business together around gunfire. At least some of these people could pay her for the work she'd intended to do.

A jeweler approached with a ring whose blue stone was as large as my thumbnail. He held it between two fingers so the light would catch it.

“Good luck,” he said.

“What do you want?” I asked.

He looked hurt. “To be generous.”

I waited. He turned the stone toward the window once more.

“Perhaps one photograph,” the jeweler added.

I put it on and let him take the picture. Saint appeared at his elbow with a notebook. The jeweler's smile vanished while mine was still on his screen.

Near eight, Milo Voss entered between two friends supporting his elbows. He wore a gray tracksuit and white tape across his nose. His jaw had swollen overnight. When he saw me, he shook his friends off and crossed the room alone, concentrating on the walk.

People quieted as he passed. He stopped in front of me and offered a small velvet box.

“You people have a serious problem with gifts,” I said.

Voss opened it. His gold tooth lay inside.

“Found it in my bag,” he said.

“Collectible,” Saint breathed beside us.

Voss ignored him. “Heard you met King.”

“Briefly,” I said.

“Still got both arms?” Voss asked.

“Last I checked,” I said, showing him my hands.

He closed the box. His thumb stayed on its lid. “My sister worked at one of Mercer's clubs. Tried to leave with her tips. King broke her hand.”

I didn't answer. Voss looked past my shoulder toward the window, then back at me.

“I want to be there next time,” he said.

“Can you stand without your friends?” I asked.

He placed the box on the table, straightened, and took one complete step back. His mouth tightened with pain. He stayed upright.

“Yeah,” Voss said.

“Heal first,” I told him.

Hậu brought a chair. Voss lowered himself into it slowly. Saint set down a glass of water without asking anyone to pay for it.

At the far end of the room, a dealer rose abruptly. The customer opposite him had caught his wrist and was pulling him halfway across the table. Two companions stood behind the customer, pushing back their chairs.

“Dealt from the bottom!” the seated man shouted.

Trinh crossed the room. I followed. The dealer was young, with a neat mustache and a shirt loose at the collar. His trapped hand had turned red.

“Let him go,” Trinh told the customer.

“Lost twenty thousand,” the man snapped.

“Expensive evening,” Trinh said, stopping beside the dealer.

The customer turned toward me. “You own this place now?”

“Part of it,” I said.

“Fix it, then.” He tightened his grip to make the dealer lean farther over the felt.

I looked at the cards, the chips, and the man's chair. A white corner protruded beneath his right thigh, bright against his dark trousers.

“Stand up,” I told him.

He stayed seated. I put one hand on the back of his chair and lifted it, tilting him just enough for his feet to leave the floor. He grabbed the table edge with his free hand. The card slid out from under his thigh and fluttered onto the felt.

His companions sat down.

I held him there while everyone looked at the card. Then I lowered the chair carefully enough to leave his drink undisturbed. He released the dealer's wrist.

“Misunderstanding,” the customer said.

I looked at Trinh. “Do you sell those here?”

“Only to regulars,” she replied.

The man took an envelope from his jacket and laid it on the table. “For the inconvenience.”

I slid it toward the dealer. Around us, people began applauding. Saint announced that standing ovations were now included with the cover charge.

My phone vibrated before the dealer could thank me. Trinh had given the airport driver my number while she tried to reach Bảo. Now his message filled the screen: Mr. Lâm is back. He says come downstairs alone.

I looked toward the stairwell. Bảo stood at the bottom in a white suit. Vinh waited beside him.

Both were looking up at me.

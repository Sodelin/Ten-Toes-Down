## Chapter 15: The Brother in the Van

Saint tried to wipe the blood off his lip with the back of his wrist. His hand shook. He missed twice before I took the tissue from him and held it against the cut.

“Look at me,” I told him.

He did. Behind him, people were coming out of the shops. Someone began picking glass off the car roof where I'd landed.

“Which way?” I asked Saint.

“Alder. Then east,” he said.

“How many?” I asked.

Saint swallowed. “King. Three in the van. One driving.”

“You see a plate?” I asked him.

He shook his head hard. “I was trying to pull him out.”

“I know,” I said.

“I had his hand,” Saint told me.

I kept the tissue against his lip until he stopped trying to talk through it. His fingers closed around my wrist for a moment, then let go.

Rafa arrived in the tow truck with Voss beside him. Trinh came down carrying my equipment case and the red box. She put both into the cab and moved aside as my phone rang.

“Your friend is comfortable,” Sunday said.

“Put him on,” I told him.

“First—” Sunday began.

“Put him on,” I repeated.

Something scraped against the phone. Then I heard breathing.

“Aiden,” Hậu said.

“You hurt?” I asked him.

“They hit me in the head,” Hậu replied.

“I saw,” I said.

“Then why ask?” he said.

I closed my eyes for half a second. His voice was slow, but it was his voice.

“I'm coming,” I told him.

Sunday took the phone back. “The old Morrow Mall. East entrance. Bring the machine.”

“You want the computer now?” I asked.

“I want you to understand that we can still make an arrangement,” Sunday said.

“You've made one,” I told him.

He hung up. Saint reached for the truck door. I stopped him with a hand against his chest.

“You can stay,” I said.

His face tightened. “That's my friend too.”

I looked at his hands. He clenched them to stop the shaking and held my gaze until I moved. Then he climbed in.

Morrow Mall had been closed for three years. The anchor store's sign was gone, leaving pale letter shapes where the sun hadn't reached the wall. Weeds grew through cracks in the parking lot. Beside a locked service gate, a giant plastic rabbit from the final Easter promotion lay on its side.

The gray van stood near the east entrance. King leaned against it with fresh tape across his nose.

“You've had a busy afternoon,” I called as I got out.

He looked at the case in my right hand. “Leave your friends.”

“They don't like waiting,” I told him.

“Then they can learn,” King said.

Saint, Rafa, and Voss stayed beside the truck while I approached. King pushed the glass door open and let me pass ahead of him.

Inside, heat had collected beneath the skylight. Empty storefronts lined a long central walkway. A dry fountain stood in the middle, its low concrete wall surrounding dust and a few coins nobody had bothered to steal. Artificial palms leaned over benches with torn cushions.

Hậu sat in a wheelchair beyond the fountain. His wrists were tied to its arms, and dried blood ran down the back of his neck. A man stood behind him with a pistol in one hand and the chair handles in the other.

Sunday waited beside a shuttered jewelry store. He'd changed his shirt since bowling.

“You came quickly,” he said.

I looked at Hậu. “Why the wheelchair?”

“They couldn't find a chair with wheels that fit,” Hậu told me.

“That is a chair with wheels,” I said.

“I made that point,” Hậu replied.

Sunday extended a hand toward the equipment case.

“Set it down,” he told me.

I placed it on a bench near the fountain. “Untie him.”

“After we see it work,” Sunday said.

“You've heard what it does,” I told him.

“I've heard a man jumped over a boxer,” Sunday replied. “I still prefer demonstrations.”

He nodded toward the jewelry store. Behind the half-raised shutter, a small safe rested on a handcart, its electronic display glowing red.

“Open it,” Sunday said.

I looked at the safe. “With the machine?”

“That would be the point,” he told me.

I went to the cart and put my good hand beneath the safe's front edge. I tipped it forward. It fell off the cart with a crash that echoed the length of the empty mall. Sunday jerked back half a step.

I put a foot against the safe's side and pulled its handle until the latch tore through the door. Then I dropped the handle beside it.

“Open,” I said.

King laughed once behind me. Sunday looked at him until he stopped.

“The computer, Aiden,” Sunday said.

I returned to the bench and opened the case. “Then untie him. He's bleeding on your chair.”

Sunday watched my hands over the machine. After a moment, he nodded to the gunman.

The man lowered his pistol and bent toward the knot at Hậu's right wrist. Hậu drove both feet against the floor and threw his weight backward. The chair tipped into the man's knees. His gun hand flew up as he tried to recover his balance.

I slammed the case shut, caught its latches with my palm, and threw it into his chest.

The pistol went off into the skylight. Glass rained into the dry fountain. Hậu hit the tiles still tied to the overturned chair.

King lunged from the entrance. I jumped onto the bench and kicked off its back toward the nearest artificial palm. Its lower branch tore free when I caught it. My other hand found the rigid trunk beneath the plastic, and I swung my feet back onto the bench for purchase.

I pulled the palm sideways by its steel core. The heavy planter skidded over the tiles and struck King's shins. He stumbled. I let go, jumped to the fountain rim, and dropped beside Hậu.

Sunday drew a pistol. I seized the wheelchair frame and heaved it over the low fountain rim with Hậu still attached. He landed on his side inside the dry basin. The first bullet broke the tile outside where his head had been.

“You could've untied me first,” Hậu said.

“Busy,” I told him.

I snapped the cord at his right wrist, then the left. He rolled clear of the chair and drew one deep breath.

King hit the other side of the fountain. Concrete cracked inward. Hậu and I scrambled in opposite directions as King climbed over the broken wall. His foot crushed the wheelchair's wheel against the basin floor.

I pulled its bent metal rim free and swung it into the side of his head. King turned with the blow. When I swung again, he caught the rim in his right hand.

His left arm stayed tight to his ribs.

I released the rim and drove my right fist into that side. King grunted and turned away. I hit the same place again, and his left elbow dropped to cover it.

Behind him, Hậu had torn a length of pipe from the fountain fittings. He gripped both ends, drove it behind King's knees, and pulled. King fell backward into the basin. Hậu and I climbed over the low wall before he could catch either of us.

Sunday was running toward the escalators. Two more men appeared from a side corridor, raising guns.

A horn blasted inside the mall.

Rafa's tow truck came through the glass entrance in a glittering shower. Saint leaned out of the passenger window.

“Customer pickup!” he shouted.

The armed men jumped behind a bench as the truck bore down on them. Rafa braked beside the fountain, its rear wheels sliding on the polished tiles. Voss pushed the back door open from inside the crew cab.

“Get in!” Voss shouted.

Hậu climbed first. I grabbed the case where it lay beside the fallen gunman, threw it into the back, and jumped onto the running board.

King rose behind me and caught my jacket.

The truck accelerated. For a moment I hung between the moving cab and his grip, my feet dragging off the running board. Cloth tore across my shoulders. I pulled one arm out of its sleeve, twisted free of the other, and fell against the open rear door.

Voss caught my belt and hauled me inside. King was left holding the jacket.

“I liked that one too,” I said through my teeth.

Rafa drove along the central walkway. Storefronts flashed past on both sides. Saint shouted directions from his memory of buying sneakers here at sixteen.

“Left after the pretzel place!” Saint called.

“Which one's the pretzel place?” Rafa shouted back.

Saint craned toward the window. “The one with the—fuck, they took the sign!”

Ahead, Sunday appeared at the top of a short service ramp. He aimed down toward the windshield. I caught the back of Rafa's seat with my good hand.

“Right,” I told him.

Rafa turned hard. The truck struck the half-open shutter of an empty department store. Metal screamed along the cab roof. We burst into a wide room of dusty display stands and abandoned mannequins, the truck's rear swinging into a row of them.

Saint looked back at the falling bodies. “Shawna could use those.”

“Later,” I said.

Rafa drove through the stockroom doors and onto the loading dock. The truck dropped off its low edge, landed hard enough to bounce us against one another, and rolled into the service lane behind the mall.

For several seconds nobody spoke. Hậu sat against the back seat with his eyes closed. Then he opened them and leaned forward between the front seats.

“Who brought food?” he asked.

Saint started laughing. It came out too loud and shook in the middle, but he kept going. Hậu put a hand on the back of his neck and pulled him close.

“You came through a building for me,” Hậu said.

Saint wiped his face with his torn sleeve. “Rafa was driving.”

“You still came,” Hậu told him.

I opened the equipment case on my knees. The shell was dented. Inside, the machine remained seated in its supports, but a crack crossed one corner of the screen. I pressed the power button and waited for it to respond.

Hậu saw me looking at the damage. “Sorry.”

The screen lit. I closed the lid.

“It opens folders,” I told him. “You eat sandwiches. I made a choice.”

He looked toward the window, smiling despite the dried blood on his collar.

We took him to the clinic. The doctor who'd stitched my arm stopped when he found all five of us in the waiting room.

“Fashion?” he asked.

“Retail,” Shawna said from the doorway behind him.

I turned. She stood beside Mom carrying a clean shirt. Neither wore an expression I wanted to argue with.

“Saint called,” Shawna told me.

“He does that,” I said.

“Good,” she replied.

Hậu went in with the doctor. Mom followed close behind, asking her questions before they'd reached the examination room. Watching her put a hand between his shoulders, I knew he would have trouble convincing her he wasn't family now.

Saint sat beside Voss and let himself stop smiling. Shawna handed me the shirt.

“Your jacket?” she asked.

“King has it,” I told her.

She closed her eyes. “I'm billing the whole fucking city.”

I put my forehead against hers. “Start with me.”

Across the waiting room, my phone lit on the chair where I'd left it. Detective Pike had sent a message. She wanted to discuss the mall that evening, alone. She said she could keep it from becoming much worse.

I read it until the screen went dark.

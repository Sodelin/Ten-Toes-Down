## Chapter 5: Heavy Delivery

Saint asked for a discreet car. Rafa Ortiz brought an orange tow truck with a gorilla painted on each door and a loudspeaker announcing that breakdowns were opportunities.

The announcement repeated while Rafa parked outside the Pearl. People came out to see what had broken. Saint tried to wave them back indoors, which made more people come out.

“Turn that off,” I told Rafa as I climbed into the front seat.

He reached across the dashboard and pulled a wire loose. The voice stopped halfway through opportunity.

“My uncle made that recording in 1998,” Rafa said. “Dead four years. Still books more work than me.”

Hậu climbed into the back. The cab settled toward his side. Saint squeezed in beside him, straightened one lapel, and immediately began explaining the difference between discretion and arriving in a commercial for yourself.

Rafa handed him a socket wrench.

“Why?” Saint asked.

“So your hands have a job,” Rafa said, and pulled away.

I called Shawna. She'd gone back to the atelier after seeing the message about her window. I could still picture her face as she handed my phone back: one quick look at the glass downstairs, then a longer one at me.

“Loan and Denise are coming over,” I told her. “Keep the door locked until they get there.”

“They're here,” Shawna said.

Behind her, Loan asked whether the window was laminated. Denise wanted to know whether the men threatening it were. A chair scraped across the floor.

“Home before dinner,” I promised.

“You said that yesterday,” Shawna replied.

“I was.” I braced a hand on the dash as Rafa turned downhill.

“You came home with boxing money in a takeout bag,” Shawna said.

“Clean bag,” I said.

“Call me when you have a better argument,” Shawna told me, and hung up.

Saint leaned between the seats. “We should standardize the bags. Something with a handle.”

I held out my phone to Hậu instead of answering. The boy selling shirts had filmed the statue being loaded, mostly to document the thieves refusing to wait while he moved his merchandise. For three seconds the truck's rear plate filled the image. Hậu recognized the bent bumper before I'd finished reading the numbers.

“Kenny Vale,” he said. “Moved equipment for the Circle. Now it's whoever pays him.”

“Where would he take it?” I asked.

“Cold-storage place by the Blue Cut,” Hậu said.

Rafa took the next right. “They owe my uncle for a recovery.”

Saint kept hold of the seat as he leaned farther forward. “Your uncle's dead.”

“The invoice isn't,” Rafa replied.

The brick shops gave way to fenced yards, corrugated walls, and channels carrying brown river water toward the harbor. A red drawbridge stood raised ahead of us. Beyond it, cranes moved above the docks. Rafa turned down a service lane beside the channel and slowed before a cold-storage building with a faded smiling fish painted across its wall.

Kenny's truck was backed into the middle loading bay. Its engine was running. The right rear door stood open against the side of the cargo box; the other hung out over the ramp. Two men stood beside it.

The smaller one had KENNY embroidered on his jacket. The larger wore a gray shirt stretched across a chest wide enough to make the lettering on Kenny seem optimistic. His right hand rested on the open door. The aluminum had dented beneath his fingers.

Hậu stopped shifting in the back seat.

“Rhett King,” he said.

“I know the name,” I answered.

“You should know the rest of him.” Hậu watched the man's hand remain still against the metal.

Rafa parked around the corner. Hậu got out with me. Saint began to follow, still carrying the wrench.

“Stay with Rafa,” I told him.

“To coordinate?” Saint asked.

“To sit.” I shut the door on his next proposal.

Hậu and I walked through the open gate. Kenny saw us and caught King's arm. King looked up slowly. He was taller at this distance, six and a half feet at least, with a pale scar from his left ear to the corner of his mouth. A thick silver chain lay against his shirt. No rings, no watch, no visible weapon.

Kenny supplied the weapon for both of them, drawing a pistol before either of us spoke.

“Stay there,” Kenny said.

We stopped ten feet short of the loading bay. I could see the edge of blue plastic in the truck behind him.

“You took my fish,” I said.

Kenny blinked. “What?”

“Big bronze fish,” I said. “Ugly man attached.”

King looked into the cargo compartment and then back at me. “That's supposed to be you?”

“Paperwork says so,” I told him.

“Got robbed twice,” King said.

I laughed before I could stop myself. Kenny didn't. He moved the pistol between my chest and Hậu's, trying to cover both without standing any farther from the truck.

“Where's the box?” Kenny demanded.

“What box?” I asked.

“Don't do that.” He came forward two paces, the gun rising with him.

I kept my hands visible. “You stole a statue and sent me a picture. I'm not the one being cryptic.”

King took his hand off the door. Four fingerprints stayed behind in the panel.

“Small red box,” he said. “Under the base when it left the banquet hall. Gone now.”

I thought of the lift breaking, the plinth dragging over steel, Hậu pulling the ramp away from the worker. I hadn't seen anything red beneath it.

“Ask the moving company,” I told King.

Kenny took the final step toward us and shoved the pistol at my face.

Hậu caught his wrist from the side and forced it upward. The shot punched into the loading-bay roof. I stepped past Hậu and drove my fist into Kenny's stomach. He folded around it, pulling his gun hand down against Hậu's grip.

Hậu stripped the pistol away, twisted the wrist, and drove Kenny shoulder-first against the truck.

King hit me before Kenny reached the concrete.

His fist caught the side of my head. The yard disappeared in a white tilt. My shoulder struck the bay's pillar hard enough to put the view back where it belonged, though I would have preferred a gentler correction.

I ducked. King's next punch hit the pillar beside my ear. Concrete broke away and rattled down my collar.

I put three punches into his body, left, right, left. The third made him step back. I kicked the side of his knee.

His leg moved. The rest of him stayed put.

Before I could withdraw my foot, he caught my ankle. He turned his hips and swung me into the open rear door. My back struck its lower edge. The bottom hinge tore loose. I dropped onto the ramp on one hip and rolled beneath it as his boot came down.

The ramp bent across its middle, shrinking the space above my chest.

Hậu hit King across the back with a pipe. The sound rang through the hollow truck. King turned around. Hậu looked at the curve he'd just put in the pipe.

“That's some bullshit,” Hậu said.

He hit King again, lower this time, across the outside of the knee. King grunted and shifted his weight. I came out from beneath the ramp and drove my shoulder into the back of the same knee.

He dropped onto one leg. Hậu flung the pipe at his face and sprang clear. King raised his right arm to block it. I reached over his shoulder, caught the silver chain, and pulled a loop backward around the truck's door handle.

King grabbed for my hand. I let go and kicked the broken ramp out from under his planted foot.

He fell sideways. The chain pulled tight for an instant, turning him toward the door. Then it snapped. Links struck the truck and scattered across the concrete. King landed hard on his left side against the ramp's bent edge.

I caught Hậu's sleeve. “Truck.”

We ran around opposite sides of the cab. Kenny was crawling toward the gate. The pistol lay within a few feet of his uninjured hand; Hậu kicked it beyond the fence before climbing into the passenger seat.

The engine was still running. I got behind the wheel, found the gear, and pulled out.

Something struck the rear so hard it shoved the whole truck forward.

In the side mirror I saw King gripping the damaged door. He ran with us for three strides. I pushed the accelerator to the floor. His shoes began scraping over the yard, but his hand stayed closed.

The remaining hinge tore out.

We cleared the gate, leaving him in the yard holding the door. Hậu braced his palm against the dashboard as I turned onto the river road.

“That nigga's going to be upset,” Hậu said.

I checked the mirror. King drew the door back over one shoulder.

“Below the window,” I told Hậu.

The door spun out of the yard and struck the truck's rear corner. The impact shoved us left. I fought the wheel, clipped a bin outside a fish business, and sprayed crushed ice across the lane. In the mirror, King was still at the gate, watching us leave with an expression that made me want several more gears.

Rafa's tow truck pulled out behind us. Saint leaned from the passenger window, shouting something I couldn't hear. My phone rang. Hậu took it from the dash and put him on speaker.

“Why is there a door in the road?” Saint demanded.

“Change of management,” I said.

“Rafa says you can't go straight,” Saint told me.

Ahead, the drawbridge had begun lowering. Red lights flashed at its barrier. The raised roadway descended toward the opposite side of the channel while a tug cleared the opening below with a barge against its bow.

I looked at the bridge, then at the mirror. No King. Yet.

“We can,” I said.

Hậu looked from the dropping span to the speedometer. “We absolutely cannot.”

I pressed harder on the accelerator. The statue bounced in the cargo box. Its weight dragged at the truck every time the suspension rose, and my hands tightened on the wheel as though I could convince the rest of the vehicle through them.

“Aiden,” Hậu said.

“I can see it,” I told him.

He flattened a hand against the dash. “Can you see the river?”

“Also yes,” I told him.

The bumper caught the crossing barrier and snapped it at the hinge. We hit the bottom of the span while it was still angled upward. The nose rose. Road vanished beneath the windshield, replaced by pale sky.

Hậu's head struck the ceiling. Behind us, bronze hit the cargo box with a noise like somebody dropping a church bell down a stairwell.

For a moment the engine raced without resistance. We'd run out of bridge.

Then the far roadway came up under the front wheels.

The dashboard split beside Hậu's hand. My teeth clicked together. The rear of the truck landed, bounced, and slewed toward the rail. I corrected, felt the tires bite, and corrected back before the opposite side of the channel acquired our fish.

We rolled onto level road. I eased off the accelerator.

Hậu sat with both hands pressed against the ceiling. The phone remained wedged beside his thigh. Even Saint had stopped talking.

“Alive?” I asked Hậu.

“Deciding,” he said.

In the mirror, the bridge finished settling. Rafa drove across it without leaving the ground once.

Saint found his voice. “The bridge was coming down, nigga! You could have waited!”

I checked for King again. The road behind Rafa was empty.

“Traffic,” I said.

Hậu turned his head very slowly. “You're buying lunch.”

At Rafa's yard, I parked Kenny's truck between two vehicles that had already lost their arguments with the road. We left the statue in the cargo box and covered it with a tarp. I hadn't recovered enough affection for it to attempt another lift.

Rafa walked around the truck, stopping at each torn hinge and dent. He whistled softly.

“Uncle would've called this an opportunity,” he said.

Saint peered beneath the tarp. The fish's tail had bent upward in the landing. He tilted his head, viewing it as a design decision.

“More dynamic now,” Saint announced.

I sat on a stack of tires. Hậu stood beside me and lifted the hair behind my ear. His fingers came away red. He fetched a clean cloth from Rafa, folded it, and pressed it against the cut.

“Stitches,” Hậu said.

“Later,” I replied.

He pressed harder. “Now is usually before later.”

Saint paced in front of the tires, scrolling through his phone. He stopped so abruptly that I thought someone had sent another photograph of Shawna's window.

“Oh,” Saint said.

I looked up. “What?”

“There was a box.” He kept his eyes on the screen.

Hậu lifted the cloth from my ear. “Say that carefully.”

Saint turned the phone toward us. Auntie Loan stood in the barber shop holding a small red metal box. A brass star shone in the middle of its lid.

“Fell off when the lift broke,” Saint said. “Kid picked it up. Loan thought it was a donation box.”

“Where is it now?” I asked, getting off the tires.

Saint swallowed. “She's trying haircut scissors.”

Rafa came out of his office as I reached the truck. He carried an envelope.

“Before you go,” he said, offering it.

Inside were three hundred dollars and his uncle's unpaid invoice. I looked from the aging paper to Rafa.

“What is this?” I asked.

“Kenny owes us. You got him out of the truck. More progress than we've made in five years.” Rafa folded the flap down again.

“Rafa,” I began.

He closed my hand around it. “Take the money, nigga. Let my uncle rest.”

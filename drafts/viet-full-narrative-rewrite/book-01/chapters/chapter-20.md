## Chapter 20: A Brother's Name

Hậu bought the old firehouse gym with a handshake, a transfer from his phone, and a promise to keep the previous owner's photograph above the water fountain. Then he asked me to help move the photograph.

“You promised,” I reminded him.

“I promised above the fountain,” Hậu said. “I didn't say how far.”

We stood beneath a five-foot-wide frame. In it, a man with an enormous mustache held a championship belt upside down while a painted eagle spread its wings behind him.

“That's a lot of photograph,” I said.

“He had a lot of confidence,” Hậu told me.

Voss came out of the back room carrying old gloves in a cardboard box.

“That's Sal,” Voss said. “He never won that belt.”

Hậu looked at him. “What?”

“He rented it,” Voss explained.

I studied the eagle again. “Saint's going to love this place.”

Hậu had hired Voss to teach boxing and help run the gym. Voss accepted before Hậu finished explaining the hours. His sister came that afternoon to see the building. Her right hand still wouldn't close properly, so she offered me her left when we met.

“Milo's been talking about the gym in his sleep,” she told me.

“I haven't,” Voss said from behind his box.

She turned toward him. “You shouted about floor mats at two in the morning.”

“They were a good price,” Voss told her.

She hugged him and stayed to help clear the office.

The firehouse had thick brick walls, high ceilings, and a wide garage door opening directly onto the street. Hậu wanted weights at one end, mats at the other, and a wrestling ring in the middle. As we carried the ring posts into position, he described what he wanted people to find there.

“Somewhere you can learn without having to lose first to somebody who enjoys it too much,” Hậu said.

He said it once. Then Saint arrived with a banner reading HANK THE THANK'S HOUSE OF PAIN AND GRATITUDE, and we had to discuss something less dignified.

“No,” Hậu told him.

Saint held the banner higher. “You haven't seen it from the street.”

“I can read from here,” Hậu said.

“That's why the lettering is good,” Saint replied.

We settled on BELL STREET FIGHT CLUB, with Hậu's actual name beneath it. Saint put THE THANK in smaller print on the flyers when Hậu wasn't looking.

The opening fell on the Friday before the ferry's launch week. My arm had closed cleanly by then, though a hard pull still reminded me where Vinh's knife had been. King had apparently recovered too. A video from one of Sunday's rooms showed him raising a barbell overhead with both hands. Three men applauded from a safe distance.

I watched once and put my phone away. Voss saw my expression.

“You were hoping he stayed hurt,” Voss said.

“I was hoping he'd take up painting,” I told him.

“He'd break the brushes,” Voss replied.

The gym filled before six. Denise brought trays of food. Loan arrived with a cash box and announced she was handling donations, although Hậu hadn't requested any. Mom printed membership cards with his name in raised black letters.

Dad brought two heavy bags and hung them himself. After checking the bolts, he gave Hậu the remaining hardware.

“If anything moves now, it's the building's problem,” Dad told him.

Great-Grandpa sent a wooden bench with a note: For when you stop running around. Hậu read it twice.

“Is that a threat?” he asked me.

“He likes multipurpose gifts,” I said.

Shawna had made Hậu a jacket. It fitted across his shoulders and closed without asking anything unreasonable of the buttons. When he put it on, he stood in front of the mirror for a long time.

“You like it?” Shawna asked.

He nodded without looking away.

“Then stop looking surprised,” she told him. “You have shoulders. I measured them.”

Hậu laughed and touched the sleeve with one finger.

Saint climbed into the ring holding the microphone. I saw paper in his other hand and knew we were in trouble.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” Saint announced, “tonight we celebrate a man who has eaten at every restaurant on Bell Street and tipped with his whole heart.”

Hậu covered his face. “Please stop him.”

“He wrote notes,” I told Hậu. “That means it can get worse.”

Saint consulted the paper. “A man who once broke a folding chair by sitting down politely. A man who went into a hostile meeting and negotiated dental. A man who has finally bought a roof high enough for all his ambitions.”

The laughter softened into applause. Hậu lowered his hands. Saint held out the microphone.

“Come on, nigga,” Saint said. “Say your name.”

Hậu climbed between the ropes and accepted the microphone in both hands. He looked around the gym, swallowed, and brought it closer to his mouth.

“Hậu Phạm,” he said.

People cheered. He waited for them to finish, then tried again.

“Some of you know me from work,” Hậu said. “Some of you know me from buying too much food. Some of you know me because I owe you money.”

Loan raised a hand. Hậu pointed at her.

“That was a sandwich,” he told her.

“It was two,” Loan replied.

He smiled and looked down at the microphone.

“I want this place open early,” Hậu said. “I want people to come because they like coming here. If you need to learn something, we'll teach you. If you already know everything, there's a mirror in the back. Go argue with it.”

The room applauded again. Hậu returned the microphone before Saint could ask for more.

Voss joined him for a short exhibition. They put on gloves and circled while Saint supplied commentary neither had requested. Voss tapped Hậu's guard twice. On his next attempt, Hậu caught the arm, stepped across his body, and threw him onto the mat with enough care to make it seem friendly.

Voss rolled, got up, and pointed a glove at him.

“You're paying for the next floor,” Voss said.

“I own this one,” Hậu told him.

They touched gloves. Near the entrance, a man in a white tracksuit began clapping slowly.

He had a shaved head and a narrow mustache. Three friends stood behind him with their hands in their pockets. Hậu saw them and stopped smiling.

“Tucker,” Hậu said.

The man raised both hands. “Just supporting local business.”

Voss moved toward the ropes. “Support it from outside.”

Tucker looked at me, then back at Hậu.

“I heard the Thank got a place,” Tucker said. “Thought I'd see whether he still falls down when people mean it.”

The room quieted. Hậu began taking off his gloves.

“You can pay for a class,” Hậu told him.

“I'm offering one,” Tucker said.

I moved toward the ring. Hậu looked at me and shook his head once. I stopped.

Tucker climbed through the ropes. His friends spread out along the edge of the room. Rafa put down his plate. Dad stood beside the heavy bags with his hands in his pockets, watching. Mom caught Shawna's sleeve and drew her a little farther from the ring.

Tucker bounced on his toes.

“No gloves?” he asked.

Hậu handed his pair to Voss. “You didn't bring any.”

Tucker threw the first punch before Hậu finished speaking. Hậu took it on his left forearm and stepped toward him. Tucker twisted away, kicked the inside of Hậu's knee, and drove another punch into his cheek.

Hậu's head moved. His feet stayed planted.

Tucker hit him again and tried to retreat. Hậu caught the back of his tracksuit. He pulled Tucker toward him, wrapped both arms around his waist, and lifted him straight off the mat.

Tucker kicked backward. One heel struck Hậu's shin. Hậu adjusted his grip, carried him three steps toward the middle of the ring, and turned.

He drove Tucker down onto the canvas. The ring shook. Tucker rolled onto an elbow, gasping for air.

Hậu stepped back and waited.

“You done?” Hậu asked him.

Tucker reached into his waistband. I saw metal flash beneath the white cloth. Hậu saw it too. He stamped on Tucker's wrist before the knife cleared his clothes, and the blade dropped onto the mat.

Hậu kicked it beneath the bottom rope, where it slid clear of the spectators, then caught Tucker by the front of his shirt and hauled him upright.

Tucker swung with his free hand. Hậu ducked underneath, lifted him again, and threw him over the top rope.

I caught Tucker before he reached the first row of chairs. He hung upside down in my arms, staring at me.

“Wrong address,” I told him.

I carried him to the door. His friends had already begun moving that way, and I put him outside beside them.

Behind me, the gym erupted. Saint shouted Hậu's name into the microphone. Loan raised both hands. Voss climbed into the ring and lifted Hậu's arm.

Hậu looked around as if he hadn't expected the sound to be for him. Then he grinned so widely he had to look down. Voss kept his arm raised. I stood in the doorway and watched him accept it.

Tucker spat onto the pavement near my shoe.

“Mercer says your boat ride's still booked,” he told me.

I looked down at him. “Tell him to keep a seat warm.”

His friends helped him away.

When I went back inside, someone had given Hậu a bouquet. Three envelopes and expensive boxing gloves had followed. He looked at the pile, then at me.

“Is this how it starts?” Hậu asked.

“Wait until they bring the horse,” I told him.

He laughed hard enough to lean against the ropes.

Later, after the guests had gone, we sat on Great-Grandpa's bench eating the last of Denise's food. Saint slept in a chair with the microphone in his lap. Voss's sister helped Shawna fold unused tablecloths. Dad had found something else to fix.

Hậu turned one of the membership cards over in his hands. His thumb moved across the raised letters.

“My name,” he said quietly.

“It's been yours the whole time,” I told him.

He nodded, still looking at it. Then he put the card inside his jacket and handed me another sandwich.

“Eat,” Hậu said. “You look skinny.”

## Chapter 26: How They Spent It

The first person to ask me for money after I left the hospital sold pictures of me accepting money from other people. His table stood outside the Pearl.

A picture cost twenty dollars. For thirty, he'd add an inspirational quotation. His best seller showed me coming down the ferry's stairs beneath NEVER LEAVE THE GRIND. In the photograph, I was escaping a man who'd just lifted a piano.

“You got a permit for that table?” I asked.

The man looked up. My face was printed on his shirt, complicating his attempt not to recognize me.

“Saint said the pavement was a common resource,” he told me.

“Saint charges people to use common resources,” I said.

“He charged me,” the man replied.

I held out a hand. He sighed and reached for his cash box.

“For a picture,” I told him.

“Oh,” he said.

“The one with the stairs,” I added.

He handed it over. I wrote on the back: PLEASE LEAVE THE GRIND IF THE GRIND HAS A PIANO. Then I signed it and gave it back.

“That's the accurate one,” I said.

He looked between the inscription and my face. “Can I sell this?”

“You can sell anything Saint hasn't already sold,” I told him.

Before I reached the door, he'd put it on an easel and changed the price to eighty dollars.

Everyone stood when I entered the gaming room.

“Sit down,” I said.

They sat. It remained an excellent trick.

Trinh had put my chair at the far end, where I could watch the entrance without turning my sore body. Beside it stood a table with water, a clean ashtray, and a little brass bell.

“What does that do?” I asked her.

“You ring it,” Trinh said, “and Saint stops whatever he's doing until somebody explains why you're unhappy.”

I rang the bell. Somewhere above us, Saint became abruptly quiet.

Trinh folded her arms. “Worth every penny.”

The Pearl had acquired curtains, two dealers, and a pianist specifically instructed to stay near his instrument. People continued bringing gifts. A cigar importer offered a cabinet. A jeweler sent cufflinks. Three brothers from a wrecking yard brought a black truck with reinforced doors.

“Against what?” I asked the oldest brother.

He looked at my bandaged fingers. “We were hoping you could tell us.”

I accepted the cufflinks, sent the cabinet to the lounge, and let Rafa inspect the truck. He went over it with the solemn attention other people gave newborn children.

“Suspension's good,” Rafa said after crawling out from underneath. “Engine's clean. They put an alarm on it.”

“Useful,” I said.

“For other people, yeah,” Rafa told me.

He caught the keys I threw him. Then he stopped smiling.

“What?” Rafa asked.

“Your tow truck's fucked,” I said.

“It's still a truck,” he told me.

“It currently has a shopping-mall directory in the radiator,” I said.

“That's information,” Rafa replied.

“Keep this one,” I told him.

He turned the keys over, looked at the brothers, then at the truck, then back at me.

“You sure?” he asked.

“You drove through a wall for me,” I said.

“It was mostly glass,” Rafa replied.

“You want a smaller truck?” I asked him.

He closed his fist around the keys. “Nigga, I love this truck.”

Hậu came from the kitchen with a plate of small sandwiches. When Rafa climbed into the cab, Hậu leaned through the other window and sounded the horn for him.

“Do I get a truck?” Hậu asked me.

“You got a gym,” I said.

“Gym doesn't have a horn,” Hậu pointed out.

“We'll work on that,” I told him.

Upstairs, Saint had arranged chairs around a table with the black bag in the middle. Sunday's last attempt to leave Eastbridge smelled faintly of river water and hotel soap.

We'd dried the damp notes on spare towels at Orchard House. Valerie found them while coming to discuss a leaking tap. She examined the drying racks and moved a bundle away from a dripping windowsill.

“I prefer advance notice when the laundry becomes a bank,” she'd told us.

Now the money was ready to divide. There was enough for the staff's promised bonuses, repairs to what we'd wrecked, and shares large enough to make everyone around the table unusually quiet.

I named the amounts. Saint began explaining the spiritual value of finding the bag. Hậu reached over and covered his mouth with a sandwich.

“You found us on the boat too,” Hậu told him. “You getting paid for everybody?”

Saint ate before answering. “Depends if we're counting King.”

Voss held his cup between both hands. He'd come from the gym, where his sister was painting the office with her good hand and insulting his choice of colors. He said they'd settled on blue because every other color had become personal.

I slid his share across. He left it there for a moment.

“He asked to see me,” Voss said.

None of us needed to ask who he meant.

“You going?” I asked.

“Already went,” Voss told me.

Hậu put down his plate. “And?”

“He looked like shit,” Voss said.

“That part was Aiden,” Hậu replied.

Voss smiled briefly. “They've got him watched. Lawyer outside. Cop by the door. He asked if my sister was working again.”

“What did you say?” I asked.

“That he didn't get to ask her anything,” Voss told me.

He pulled the envelope toward him.

“Then I left,” Voss said.

We let that stand. Saint counted his share twice. Hậu counted his once, lost his place, and asked Trinh to help. Rafa looked at the number on his envelope until I threw a paper ball at his head.

“Buy something,” I told him.

“I just got a truck,” Rafa said.

“Something that doesn't need oil,” I suggested.

He turned the envelope over. “You are closing a lot of doors.”

He bought a boat that afternoon. It needed oil.

A week later, a partner from the lawyer's firm asked to meet at Shawna's atelier. She said the Pearl's waiting area made people feel as though they were about to lose either money or a finger.

“Usually money,” I told her.

“I'm delighted by your restraint,” the lawyer replied.

Shawna put us at the cutting table after making us wipe our shoes. She fitted a customer's jacket in the next room while we went over Sunday again. There would be more questions about him. For that morning, the partner worked through where I'd stood, what he'd held, and where the ferry struck the terminal. She stopped whenever I tried to describe a location as right fucking there.

The crew had given accounts, as had Imani. The lawyer said Pike's own recordings and the garage video made her explanation of Vinh's death harder to maintain. Kenny Vale had supplied dates and names through his own lawyer before leaving Eastbridge.

“He asked whether your mechanic still wanted payment for the damaged truck,” the partner told me.

“Which damaged truck?” I asked.

She checked a note. “That was his exact question.”

Rafa sent three photographs and an invoice. Kenny paid before dinner.

Shawna came into the room carrying an inside-out sleeve.

“Are you two finished?” she asked.

“For today,” the lawyer said.

“Good,” Shawna replied. “He's booked.”

“I am?” I asked.

She pointed toward the fitting platform. I climbed onto it carefully. My ankle was better. My ribs still objected to sharp turns, laughing, and the way Hậu greeted me as if I'd been missing for seven years.

Shawna laid dark cloth across my shoulder.

“Arms out,” she said.

“What am I modeling?” I asked.

“A husband who stays where he's put,” Shawna told me.

“Limited edition,” I said.

“Extremely,” she replied.

She pinned the cloth. In the mirror I could see the new window display and a woman collecting a finished dress. Proper shelves lined the wall. Shawna's assistant had labeled the garment bags. Beside the doorway hung a newspaper photograph from the Alder Cinema show, with Shawna at its center and the models behind her.

My picture wasn't on that wall. I liked hers better.

“Estelle paid the balance?” I asked.

“And ordered another dress,” Shawna told me.

“How is she?” I asked.

Shawna smoothed the cloth beneath my collar. “Tired. Angry. She wants a dress with pockets.”

“Reasonable,” I said.

Shawna paused with the pin above my shoulder. “She said her brother spent his whole life making entrances and left her to find all the exits.”

I looked at my wife in the mirror.

“What did you say?” I asked her.

“That she could pick the lining herself,” Shawna replied.

She put the pin where she wanted it and told me to lower my shoulder. I stood while she worked. Her phone rang twice. She took one call, rejected a delivery substitution on the other, and gave me the measuring tape to hold against my chest while she found a spool.

The woman collecting the dress finally recognized me. Her eyes widened.

“Oh my God,” she said.

I prepared a modest smile.

“You're blocking the good mirror,” the woman told me.

Shawna turned me six inches to the left.

The statue returned to the Pearl on a Monday. Rafa brought it upright in his new truck, secured with straps chosen to withstand my father's judgment. Saint hired two men to unveil it. Loan arrived with a chair, explaining that anyone reopening a business with a statue of himself required supervision.

Under the repainting, the bronze face still resembled Bảo if you knew where to look. Saint had corrected the hair. It now looked as though a bird had won a fight with a helmet. The fish at its feet had survived everything with its mouth open.

“We can replace the head,” Rafa offered.

“Leave it,” I told him.

“Why?” he asked.

“Reminds me what happens when you pay somebody before checking the work,” I said.

Loan leaned forward in her chair. “Finally. A statue teaches something.”

We put it beside the stairs. Saint removed the old plaque and uncovered its replacement. THE MAN WHO CAME BACK appeared in large letters. Beneath it, in smaller print: PLEASE DO NOT RUB THE FISH.

“Why would anyone rub the fish?” I asked.

“They will now,” Saint told me.

They did. Within an hour, a man claimed the fish had helped him win at cards. By evening its nose shone brighter than the rest of the statue. Saint began proposing commemorative fish cloths.

I rang the brass bell. He stopped talking.

The first truck came at seven. Three crates entered through the service door under paperwork describing them as kitchen equipment. Their contents would have disappointed a cook. Trinh checked the seals while I met the buyer in the back room.

He'd known Sunday. He knew whose chair I occupied. He tried to use both facts to negotiate a better price. I let him finish, then put the price back where it had started.

He paid.

I took my share upstairs and put it in the safe. Below me, the gaming room filled. A singer tested the microphone. Somebody kissed the fish. At the front door, a man removed his hat before asking whether I might be available for an expensive favor.

Trinh looked up from the landing.

“Working tonight?” she asked.

I straightened the new cuffs Shawna had fitted to my shirt.

“Send him up,” I told her.

## Chapter 28: Standing at Home

Mom asked us to come over on Sunday and bring nothing. Shawna brought flowers. I brought dessert. Saint arrived behind us carrying an antique clock whose previous owner had assured him it belonged to a king.

Dad met him at the door. “Which king?”

Saint glanced at me, then tightened his grip on the clock. “A different one.”

Dad took it long enough to turn it over. A battery dropped into his palm.

“Progressive kingdom,” Dad said. He handed the clock back and let Saint through.

The house smelled of garlic, hot oil, and the sweet sauce Mom made when she intended to spend the meal accusing us of eating too much. Shawna took her flowers into the kitchen. Saint followed her because that was where the smell was strongest. I set the dessert on the table and found Dad putting a little stool beneath the smoke alarm in the hall.

“Needs a battery?” I asked him.

Dad held up the one from his palm. “Saint brought one.”

I stayed beside the stool while he fitted it. He pressed the test button, and the alarm gave a shriek that stopped conversation in the kitchen.

“If dinner's on fire, tell the rice!” Great-Grandpa Tín called.

Dad smiled and climbed down. I bowed to him. He put his hand behind my neck and drew me close enough to kiss my temple. His palm settled in the same place it had when I was little. I had to bend farther now.

“You late?” Dad asked me.

“I'm early,” I told him.

Dad pointed toward the dining room. “Good. Your mother needs chairs.”

I looked toward the dining room. “How many?”

“More than she said,” Dad told me.

We went through to the garage. A dismantled lawn mower occupied the space where a car might have gone. Two engines sat farther back, beneath a shelf of tools. Against the wall was a wooden crate with a shipping label so old I couldn't read it. Dad nudged the mower aside with his foot and pointed to the stacked chairs under the window.

I took four in each hand. Their legs caught for a moment; I eased them apart and turned sideways toward the door.

“Careful of the paint,” Dad said.

I looked at the chairs. “On these?”

“House,” Dad told me. “Chairs will survive you.”

Behind him, the garage door started descending. Saint had followed us and was standing beside the wall controls. He looked up at the moving panels with the injured expression of a man who'd only wanted more light.

Dad raised one hand without turning. The bottom edge of the door met his palm and stopped. The motor kept humming. Above his hand, the hinges trembled along the panels.

“Other button,” Dad told Saint.

Saint pressed another switch. Outside, the sprinkler began ticking against the driveway.

“Nigga, how many kinds of darkness does this house have?” Saint asked.

Dad reached back with his free hand and pressed the correct control. The motor changed its note. As the door rose, he lowered his arm and pointed me toward the house.

I carried the chairs inside before Saint found anything connected to the gas.

Grandpa Hòa sat near the kitchen doorway with a fresh length of blue cord in his hands. He was tying it around the bent leg of his folding chair. There were better chairs in the house. There were chairs in my house that cost more than the car I'd driven at seventeen. He had brought this one anyway.

I set down my stack beside him. “You could let me fix that.”

“It is fixed,” Hòa said.

“It's got a knot,” I pointed out.

He tugged the cord. “Good knot.”

The frame settled level beneath him. He put both feet on the floor and gave me a small look of satisfaction.

“Where'd it get bent?” I asked him.

At the counter, Tín looked over. Hòa continued working the loose end beneath the cord.

“Chair had a difficult afternoon,” Hòa said.

I kept my voice low. “Was it near a railway bridge?”

His fingers paused. It lasted only a moment. Then he tucked the end of the cord away and looked past me into the dining room.

“Your father needs help with the table,” Hòa told me.

Tín bit into a slice of fruit. He was smiling around it. I pointed at him before he could go back to pretending he hadn't heard.

“You wrote on that photograph,” I said.

“I can write on several things,” Tín told me.

I pointed toward Hòa's chair. “You wrote Ask Hòa.”

Tín held out a piece of fruit. “And you asked him. Excellent system.”

I took the fruit. He walked away while I was still deciding which question would keep him in the room.

The dining table had acquired an extra leaf and an argument. Mom wanted it closer to the kitchen. Dad stood at one end, looking at the gap she'd indicated.

“Already close,” Dad told her.

Mom pushed the nearest chair into his shin. “There. Space.”

Dad looked down at the chair, then across the table at me. I took the other end. We moved it until Mom held up a hand. She walked around it, pulled out a chair, and examined the route to the kitchen again.

“Too close,” Mom said.

I kept my face still. Dad watched me over the tabletop.

“You laughing?” he asked.

“No,” I told him.

“Good,” Dad said. We moved it back three inches.

Hậu came in with two bags of fruit and a box tucked beneath his arm. He put the fruit down, opened the box, and stood a horn on the counter.

I stared at it. “You bought one.”

“You promised,” Hậu reminded me.

“For the gym,” I said.

“I'm demonstrating,” Hậu said.

He squeezed it. The blast rattled a spoon against a cup. Mom stopped with a serving dish in her hands. Saint ducked for no useful reason. Tín set down his fruit, took the horn from Hậu, and examined it.

Hậu watched him carry it to the back door. Tín opened the door and put his arm outside. A short, astonished honk came from somewhere beyond the fence. He came back without the horn.

“Demonstration finished,” Tín said.

Hậu turned to me. I spread my hands.

“You want it back, you ask him,” I told Hậu.

He looked at Tín, then picked up the bags he'd brought. “I brought oranges.”

Rafa arrived with his girlfriend and a shirt so immaculate I suspected she had supervised him all the way from the car. Voss came with his sister. Loan brought a dish Mom said she didn't need, and Mom immediately cleared a place for it. Denise entered without knocking, kissed Mom's cheek, and sent Saint away from the food with a movement of her hand that needed no explanation.

Chairs scraped across the floor. People reached around one another for plates. My grandmother Hương came in from the backyard with freshly cut herbs and rested her hand on Hòa's shoulder as she passed. He caught her fingers. She bent down to hear him, laughed at whatever he said, and struck his shoulder lightly with the herbs before continuing to the kitchen.

“What'd you tell her?” I asked Hòa.

“Private lesson,” Hòa said.

“You teach a lot of those,” I told him.

He watched Hương arrange the herbs beside the food. “Excellent students.”

At the other end of the room, Dad was trying to carry a full tray through the gap Saint occupied. Saint moved left. Dad moved left. Saint moved right. Dad stopped and waited until Saint worked out which of them needed to keep making decisions.

Eventually, we all sat. Hậu had the seat nearest the rice. He looked from the bowl to Mom with such tenderness that she pushed it a little closer to him.

Dad served Mom before filling his own plate. Tín watched him across the table.

“Look at this,” Tín said. “Manners.”

Mom lifted one eyebrow. “He's always had manners.”

Tín laughed so hard he had to put down his chopsticks. Dad continued spooning rice onto his plate.

“Careful,” Mom told her grandfather.

Tín wiped at one eye. “He came to my house wearing a jacket that still had another man's tooth in the pocket.”

“It wasn't his jacket,” Mom said.

Tín pointed at her. “Listen to the defense.”

“It was a borrowed jacket,” Mom told him.

“I didn't ask who owned the tooth.” Tín picked up his chopsticks again, still smiling.

Saint folded his hands beneath his chin. “Please continue.”

Mom passed him a bowl. “Please eat.”

Dad's ears had turned a little red. I'd watched him bend steel and stop machinery with one hand. Mom inspected him over the rim of her glass, and he found something on his plate that needed his full attention. I had never managed to produce that effect on him.

Beside me, Shawna leaned forward. “What happened to the jacket?”

“Cleaned it,” Dad told her.

“What happened to the tooth?” Shawna asked.

Tín put down his chopsticks again. “Your mother-in-law made him put it in an envelope.”

Mom covered her eyes. “It was disgusting.”

“She wrote Lost Property,” Tín added.

Denise lowered her fork and laughed into her napkin. Dad looked at Mom. She uncovered her face and looked back. Their expressions changed together, and for a moment I could see two younger people sharing something the rest of us had only just been allowed to hear.

Then Mom took another spoonful of rice. “He also forgot the soap.”

Dad set down the serving spoon. “I got the soap.”

“The next day,” Mom told him. “It was after midnight.”

“On the way home,” Dad said.

“On a different day,” Mom said.

Dad considered the distinction. “It was a large soap.”

Tín slapped the table. “A romantic!”

After dinner, we cleared the plates while the people who claimed to have cooked enough in their lives supervised from their chairs. Dad dried the serving dishes as I carried them in. When the table was clear, he asked me to get a box from the cupboard above the hall closet.

It held photographs, a packet of cards, and a cream envelope with his name on the front. The envelope slipped out when I set the box down. I picked it up and turned it over. Dad had written an address on the back beneath a name I recognized.

“Calvin Rook?” I said.

Dad looked over from the kitchen doorway. “Long time ago.”

“You knew him?” I asked.

Dad folded the towel. “He paid me.”

Saint appeared beside my shoulder so quickly I wondered whether he'd been in the cupboard with the box.

“For what?” Saint asked.

“Standing by a door,” Dad told him.

Saint looked at the envelope, then at the doorway, then at his shoes. “I do that every day.”

“You talk too much,” Dad said.

I held the envelope by its edges. “How much did he pay?”

“A hundred thousand.” Dad put the towel away and came to the table.

I looked up at him. Mom followed with a tray of tea.

“He wanted your father to make people feel safe,” Mom explained.

From his chair, Tín made a thoughtful noise. “How did that work out?”

Dad took the envelope from me and laid it beside the photographs. “For some people.”

Hậu pulled a chair closer to the table. Dad watched him position it.

“What are you doing?” Dad asked him.

“Getting safe,” Hậu said.

Shawna sat beside me, tucked one leg beneath herself, and accepted a cup from Mom. Rafa closed the back door. Voss's sister asked Mom whether this was the story about the motorcycle, and Mom said that depended on which motorcycle she meant.

People who'd been preparing to leave found places to sit. I looked at them arranging themselves around Dad, then at the cream envelope.

“You never told me about this,” I said.

Dad shrugged. “You were busy.”

“I wasn't born,” I reminded him.

“Busy elsewhere,” Dad told me.

Tín laughed. Hòa leaned back in the chair with the blue cord and folded his hands across his stomach. A pale straight mark crossed two knuckles on the back of one hand. I hadn't noticed it before.

He caught me looking and covered it with his other hand.

“One story at a time,” Hòa said.

“Yours next?” I asked him.

He smiled. “Tea first.”

Mom refilled his cup, then sat beside Dad and lifted the envelope between two fingers. She examined the paper before turning it over.

“This was terrible stock,” Mom said.

Saint bent closer to see. “Cost him a hundred thousand.”

“Then your father could have bought better paper,” Mom told me.

Dad opened his mouth. Tín raised a finger before he could begin.

“Before you start making yourself handsome, remember who was there,” Tín told him.

“I am handsome,” Dad said.

Tín glanced at Mom. “Your wife is in charge of that rumor.”

She reached over and straightened Dad's collar. “And I run it very well.”

I leaned back. Shawna found my hand beneath the table. Outside, a car slowed near the house and continued down the street. My phone vibrated with a message from Trinh: the Pearl was open and making money. I read it once and put the phone facedown.

Dad took the envelope from Mom and turned the address toward himself. Beneath it was a shopping list. I recognized Mom's handwriting, though the letters looked a little rounder than the notes she left on the refrigerator now.

He smoothed the envelope flat. His thumb stopped beside the last item on the list, and he smiled.

“All right,” Dad said.

Saint reached for his phone. Loan caught his wrist without looking away from Dad.

Dad waited until we'd settled. His cup stood beside the old envelope. Mom's knee touched his beneath the table. I watched the man who'd held a truck in the street, stopped the garage door without turning, and kissed my temple when I came home. He looked down at a list that included scallions and began to laugh.

Then my father told us what happened when he agreed to stand by that door.

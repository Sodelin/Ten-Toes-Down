## Chapter 25: Long Live Whoever Pays

The hospital gave me a room with a harbor view. I asked whether they had one facing a wall.

The nurse looked at the water, then at the bruises across my chest.

“Bad evening?” she asked.

“I'm developing a preference for land,” I told her.

She closed the curtain halfway and said the doctor would come soon. I lay against the pillows and tried to find a position that didn't involve owning ribs.

Two were cracked. My ankle had swollen until the hospital sock looked personally offended. There were cuts across my knuckles, a split inside my mouth, and a dark bruise spreading beneath my left eye. The doctor read the list while Shawna stood at the foot of the bed.

“Could have been worse,” I said.

He looked over the chart. “I'm sure you'll keep trying.”

Shawna made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob. The doctor's expression softened. He finished explaining what happened next and left us alone.

She came around the bed, sat beside me, and took my good hand. For a while we stayed like that.

“The jacket?” I asked eventually.

Shawna looked at me. “That's your opening?”

“I came home in it,” I told her.

“You came to a hospital in it,” she said.

“The direction was correct,” I replied.

She covered her face, laughed once, and shook her head. When her hand came down, her eyes were wet.

“I hate watching the phone when you're out there,” Shawna said.

I tightened my fingers around hers. “I know.”

“No,” she told me. “You know I don't like it. That's different from sitting with it.”

I kept quiet. She described Saint's first message, the sound of the trucks outside the printshop, and the stretch afterward when nobody answered because everyone was busy surviving. Her thumb moved over the uninjured side of my hand as she spoke. I hadn't been there with her, watching that screen. I let her tell me what it had been like.

When she finished, I drew her closer with the arm that still worked properly. She leaned against me carefully.

“You're not getting out of hemming those trousers,” Shawna said into my shoulder.

“I don't know how,” I told her.

“You'll be sitting down for a while,” she said. “Learn.”

I kissed the top of her head.

Mom opened the door carrying food. Dad followed with a paper bag. Behind them, Great-Grandpa Tín came backward in a wheelchair, pushing himself with his feet and arguing with the attendant trying to help.

“I can turn it around myself,” Tín told him.

“Sir, it's easier if—” the attendant began.

“I'm a hundred and four,” Tín said. “I've tried easier.”

He turned the chair, rolled into the room, and looked at me.

“You look like the other man got some opinions in,” Tín observed.

“He had several,” I told him.

“Good,” Tín said. “You needed the conversation.”

Mom set her food on the side table and came to the bed. She touched my hair, examined my face, and kissed my forehead without making a joke. Then she pointed at the breakfast tray.

“Don't eat that,” Mom said.

“I already did,” I told her.

“Then we're dealing with two emergencies,” she said.

Dad put the paper bag beside my feet. Inside were clean clothes, a charger, and the mug with my baby picture.

“You brought Master Chigga,” I said.

“Hospital cups leak,” Dad told me.

Tín peered at the gold lettering. “That's a very small master.”

Dad took the mug out and placed it where everyone could see it properly. A scrape crossed one of his knuckles. Otherwise he looked as he had at dinner before the ferry: blue shirt, dark trousers, no apparent interest in making an occasion of himself.

“What happened at the printshop?” I asked him.

Mom took out her phone. “These two idiots happened.”

She held the screen where Shawna and I could see it. The security camera showed the street at night. Two heavy trucks rounded the corner, one behind the other. The first climbed the curb toward the shop. People ran back through the doorway.

On the recording, Dad stepped into its path and put both hands against the grille. The truck carried him another foot, his shoes scraping pale lines into the pavement.

Then it stopped.

The rear tires kept spinning. Smoke rose around them. The driver leaned on the horn while Dad moved one foot back, straightened his arms, and pushed the truck's front away from the printshop. Its tires dragged sideways across the curb.

Behind it, the second truck turned toward the alley. Tín walked into its path with a grocery bag. He set the bag on a parking meter, reached beneath the front bumper, and lifted.

The front wheels left the road. The rear tires bounced and spun. Tín walked the raised cab sideways until it faced the first truck instead of the alley, then set it down.

Dad still held the first truck by its grille. Tín went around behind it, crouched, and caught the frame beneath the rear bed.

He stood up. The entire truck rose off the street.

Dad's feet went with it. For one startled second he hung from the grille before letting go and dropping back onto the road. On Mom's screen, he turned slowly toward the old man holding the truck.

Tín rotated it across the empty lane and set all four wheels down between two parked cars. The grocery bag remained on the meter.

The recording had sound. Mom's voice came from the printshop doorway.

“You blocked the delivery entrance!” she shouted.

On the screen, Tín pointed at Dad. “He chose the parking.”

Dad answered too quietly for the microphone to catch. Mom stopped the video.

Beside my hospital bed, Dad was looking at the television on the opposite wall.

“You could have put it down first,” Dad told Tín.

“You were holding on,” Tín replied.

“I was stopping it,” Dad said.

“And I finished,” Tín told him.

Shawna put a hand over her mouth. I laughed, and pain cut through my side. Mom told everyone to stop being funny until I could breathe.

Tín looked at her. “Then why'd you marry him?”

Mom pointed at the door. He took fruit from the tray and remained exactly where he was.

Granddad Hòa arrived later with flowers and a crossword book. He looked at the arrangements crowding the windowsill and carried his into the bathroom.

“Less competition,” he explained when he came back.

“You see the video?” I asked.

Hòa sat beside the bed. “I was there.”

“Where?” I asked him.

“Inside,” he said. “Holding the soup.”

Tín snorted from his wheelchair. Hòa looked toward him. Neither said anything for a moment. Then Granddad opened the crossword book on my lap.

“Seven letters,” Hòa said. “A person who insists on doing things twice.”

“Aiden?” I suggested.

“You need more letters,” he told me.

“Aiden Trần,” I said.

Hòa took the pencil out of my hand. “We'll come back to this when you're less pleased with yourself.”

By afternoon, the corridor had become a reception line. Staff limited visitors to two at a time. Saint tried to interpret this as two parties and learned that a party of eleven still counted as eleven people.

He sat beside the door with a notebook and began scheduling appointments. A nurse stopped to ask whether he worked there.

“Spiritually,” Saint said.

She gave him a stack of empty water cups to throw away.

Denise came first with food for the staff as well as me. Loan brought an embroidered pillow reading DON'T JUMP YET. Benny brought a check. Trinh came with news from the rooms and made him put it away until she finished speaking.

“The Pearl had its best night,” Trinh told me.

“While I was on the boat?” I asked.

“People stayed to wait for news,” she said. “Then they stayed to celebrate. Then several lost enough money to wish they'd gone home earlier.”

“How much?” I asked.

She showed me. I stared at the number until Saint leaned in from the doorway.

“You should almost die less often, but charge more when you do,” he told me.

Shawna looked toward him. He withdrew.

Imani and Edwin visited together. Edwin had stitches above one eyebrow and a fresh complaint about the hospital coffee. Imani gave me a folded list of names.

“Everyone off,” she said.

I read the first few, then looked up. “Everyone?”

“Guests. Crew. Your people,” Imani told me. “Even the large bastard who broke the piano.”

I leaned back. For the first time all day, I breathed without trying to hide what it cost.

“Good,” I said.

She squeezed my shoulder. “You paid for a crew. You got a crew.”

Later, Walker came with another detective and the lawyer. They asked about the bridge, Sunday's knife, and the fall. I described what I remembered. When the questions began circling the same point for a third time, the lawyer stopped them.

They told me Sunday's body had been recovered near the terminal pillars that morning. I listened without answering. Walker watched me, wrote something down, and closed his notebook.

There would be more questions. There were recordings and witnesses and a ferry full of damage. The lawyer said we'd deal with them as they arrived.

After they left, Hậu came with Voss and Rafa. All three had been cleaned, bandaged, and told to rest. None seemed interested in doing it anywhere except my room.

Rafa carried a black bag. I recognized the bag Sunday had held on the bridge.

“You kept that?” I asked.

“Found it in the lounge,” Saint said, appearing behind them. “I was moving people. It was in the way.”

“So you moved it too,” I said.

“I'm thorough,” Saint told me.

Inside were the remaining bundles of Sunday's escape money. Some were damp. Most were intact. The appearance-payment envelope sat on top.

Shawna looked into the bag, then at me.

“The jacket repairs are covered,” she said.

I smiled. Hậu put the money beneath a chair, clear of the next nurse's path, and sat beside me.

“King asked about you,” Voss told me.

“What did he ask?” I said.

“Whether you lived,” Voss replied.

“What did you tell him?” I asked.

Voss looked toward the corridor, where flowers, food, and people waited for their turn to come in.

“That he was about the only person in Eastbridge who hadn't sent a gift yet,” Voss said.

Rafa laughed. Hậu laughed. Saint laughed from the doorway.

I tried not to. I failed.

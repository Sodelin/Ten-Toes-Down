## Chapter 17: No Flash Photography

Valerie banned photographs in the house after a man tried to take one while I was brushing my teeth. He'd come to install curtains.

“My son won't believe I'm here,” he explained from the bathroom doorway.

I stood over the sink with toothpaste in my mouth. I spat, rinsed, and looked at the phone still in his hand.

“Your son can survive disappointment,” I told him.

He apologized and backed into the hall, offering to waive the installation charge. Shawna appeared behind him in a robe.

“Full price,” she said. “Then leave.”

Before lunch, Valerie posted a rule beside the front door: NO PHOTOGRAPHS WITHOUT ASKING. Saint added a smaller notice telling people to ask Saint. Valerie took it down and returned it to him folded into a very sharp square.

The ten days after the garage passed without anyone successfully setting fire to a room I occupied. I took that as progress. My arm stayed wrapped for most of them. When the doctor removed the stitches, he repeated the instructions I'd already received and asked Shawna to keep being the sensible person in our household.

“Does that come with a reduced bill?” she asked him.

It did not.

The lawyer handled the police. He told me that Walker's account and the garage cameras had made Pike's version difficult to maintain. That didn't erase the mall, the fights, or the questions about Vinh. It meant she now had to answer some of those questions herself.

Bảo left Eastbridge three days after the garage. The afternoon before he left, Phương called Shawna and asked to meet at Bishop's. I arrived late and found them drinking coffee. Baojun sat on a stool at the counter, his braced wrist resting beside a glass he hadn't touched.

He turned as I sat down.

“You didn't have to do that,” Baojun said.

“You pointed a gun at me,” I told him.

“I wasn't going to—” he began.

Phương set down her cup. “Finish that sentence in your head.”

Baojun closed his mouth. His mother took a phone from her bag and slid it across the table toward me.

“His recording,” Phương said. “From the garage. Original file.”

I looked at her. “Why give it to me?”

“Because my husband has decided that losing his rooms wasn't enough,” she told me. “He wanted to lose his family next.”

Baojun turned back toward the counter. Phương left the phone beneath my hand.

“Vinh was alive when you left,” she said. “The video shows that. Bảo called someone afterward. I don't know whom. I didn't stay to listen.”

“Sunday,” I said.

Phương gave me no answer. Shawna took the phone and put it in her bag for the lawyer. Phương watched her close the clasp.

“You were good at the cinema,” Phương told her.

“So were you at the gym,” Shawna replied.

Phương smiled faintly. “I've had more practice removing him from rooms.”

Baojun turned on his stool again.

“Dad says you're taking everything,” he told me.

I looked at him for a moment. “Your mother just saved you more than I took.”

His eyes moved toward her. Phương lifted her cup and kept drinking.

They left the following morning. I didn't ask where they were going. Phương messaged Shawna that the plane had good legroom and no speeches. Shawna showed me while we unpacked the last boxes at Orchard House.

“She should've left earlier,” I said.

“People usually know that afterward,” Shawna told me.

She put down her phone and lifted the painting of me on the tiger.

“Where?” she asked.

“Guest bathroom,” I said.

Shawna looked at the enormous jaw. “That's cruel to guests.”

“They'll leave faster,” I told her.

She considered it, then carried the painting upstairs.

Saint announced the housewarming without consulting either of us. By the time I heard, seventy people had confirmed, a caterer had offered to work in exchange for a photograph, and somebody had promised a live band.

Valerie read the list on her tablet.

“This house holds forty comfortably,” she told Saint.

“We don't want them comfortable,” he said. “We want them impressed.”

She looked at me. I pointed toward the terrace.

“Outside,” I said. “And he cleans.”

Saint nodded quickly. Before I could explain which part of that sentence mattered, he'd hired three people to clean.

The party began at seven and escaped him by eight. People came up the drive with gifts large enough to require their own arrival schedules. Someone brought a chandelier. Someone else brought a signed basketball in a glass case. A man I'd never met offered me a small percentage of a nightclub if I attended its anniversary.

“What anniversary?” I asked him.

“We can choose one you're free for,” the man said.

I sent him to Trinh. She stood near the terrace doors with a drink and a notebook. Each time I looked her way, another man was explaining why he wanted to pay me. She listened, wrote something down, and sent him along before the queue reached the hall.

Hậu arrived with a bandage at the back of his head and a new shirt that fitted his shoulders. Denise had found a tailor willing to treat him as a structural problem rather than a size.

“How's the head?” I asked.

“Still mine,” Hậu said.

“Good,” I told him.

He looked through the terrace doors toward the pool. “Can I?”

“Not in that shirt,” I said.

He nodded solemnly and went to find somewhere to change.

Mom and Dad came together. Dad carried a small toolbox. Mom carried a covered dish and immediately asked Valerie whether the kitchen had enough serving spoons.

“Dad, you don't have to fix anything,” I said, looking at the toolbox.

“I know,” he told me.

He went past me toward the terrace door, which had stuck since we'd moved in.

Granddad Hòa arrived on foot. I saw him at the gate with a pharmacy bag in his hand. The guard came out of the booth to stop him, looked at his face, and opened the gate with both hands. Granddad thanked him and came up the drive.

“You walked?” I asked when I met him at the door.

“The bus stopped,” Hòa said.

“Where?” I asked.

“Before the hill,” he told me.

He gave me the paper bag. Inside were bandages, antiseptic, and cough drops.

“Why cough drops?” I asked him.

“Your great-grandfather said you were getting hoarse from accepting applause,” Hòa replied.

I laughed. He touched my good shoulder and went inside.

Five minutes later, Great-Grandpa Tín reached the gate in his tiny electric cart. Saint spotted him and took the microphone from the bandleader.

“Ladies and gentlemen, the original—” Saint began.

Tín sounded the cart's horn until Saint stopped.

“I've been original all day,” Tín called. “Find me a plate.”

Guests moved aside as he drove onto the terrace. A man offered him a seat. Tín pointed at the cart beneath him.

“I brought one,” he said.

Dad came outside with the terrace door moving smoothly behind him. Tín looked from the toolbox to the hinge.

“You charging your son?” Tín asked.

“No,” Dad said.

“Soft,” Tín told him.

Mom set her dish on the table and pointed at them both.

“No business at dinner,” she said.

Tín looked toward Trinh, who was accepting another envelope.

“Then what the fuck is this, Mai?” he asked.

Mom opened her mouth, looked at the gifts stacked beside the doors, and closed it again. Granddad Hòa smiled into his drink.

Shawna came downstairs in a dark blue dress she'd finished that afternoon. The band was halfway through a song. Its singer saw her, missed a word, and looked toward me before finding his place again.

I met her at the terrace doors.

“You did that on purpose,” I told her.

“I made the dress on purpose,” Shawna said.

“The rest too,” I said.

She put a hand against my chest. “Try to survive an evening without a window.”

I took her onto the small dance floor Saint had rented. People clapped as we passed. Shawna raised one hand without looking at them, and the phones lowered.

For three songs, I let her lead whenever she wanted. The terrace door opened quietly behind us. Mom found the serving spoons. At the far side of the floor, Tín ate from a plate balanced on the cart's wheel. I pretended not to notice when Shawna stepped on my foot.

“That was deliberate,” I whispered.

“You were showing off,” she told me.

“It's my house,” I said.

“It's my dress,” Shawna replied.

I kissed her. The applause began again. She laughed against my mouth and told me I'd never hear the end of it.

Near ten, I found a quiet room off the hall and sat down for the first time in an hour. The door opened a moment later. I expected Saint.

Instead, a woman I didn't know stood there carrying a tray with one glass of water.

“Mrs. Nguyễn said you were hiding,” she told me.

“Resting,” I said.

“She used the other word,” the woman replied.

I took the water and thanked her. She turned toward the door, then hesitated.

“My brother works with the delivery crew,” she said. “He saw you pull that man out from under the statue.”

“How's he doing?” I asked.

“The man you saved? Fine,” she said. “My brother says they keep telling the story.”

She placed a folded note on the side table.

“He passed this along for you,” she told me.

She left before I opened it. Inside was a photograph of the worker with his two children, all three grinning beneath paper versions of Saint's wings. No money. Just their names and a thank-you.

I put it in my jacket pocket.

My phone buzzed. An unknown number had sent a photograph of the Sunday Best at its berth. Workers were hanging lights along the upper deck. Beneath it was a date three weeks away and a message about opening night: my name was on the invitation whether I came or not.

I stared at the ferry. Outside, Hậu hit the pool with a splash that drove six guests out of their chairs.

“Tidal warning!” Saint shouted from the terrace.

I put the phone away and went to see how much water was left.

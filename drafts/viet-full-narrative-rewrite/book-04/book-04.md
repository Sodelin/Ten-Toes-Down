## Chapter 1: A Man of Several Floors

The second swimming pool arrived on a Tuesday, while I was trying to eat an egg.

I knew it was the second because I could see the first through the breakfast-room windows. It remained in the garden, full of water and entirely uncomplaining. The new one stood upright on a truck outside the front steps, blue inside, white outside, with a ladder strapped across it like somebody had captured a municipal shark.

Valerie held the delivery tablet between the truck and me. “Which mistake would you like moved first, Mr. Trần? The truck is preventing the groceries from becoming breakfast.”

“I haven't ordered a pool,” I said.

The driver produced a photograph of me shaking a man's hand. Petrakis. I had helped his nephew out from under a car. Apparently I had helped much harder than the nephew deserved, because a bottle of wine, a fruit basket shaped like a shipwreck, and now several thousand gallons of potential obligation had followed.

“Underwater speakers,” the driver told me, tapping the glossy brochure.

Shawna came down the steps in slippers, scissors in one hand and a pencil in her hair. She regarded the pool for a full three seconds. Then she stood on tiptoe and peered around it at the pool we already possessed.

“They'll fight,” Shawna said.

“It's a thank-you,” I explained.

“Well, tell him you're welcome and he may stop.” Shawna turned the brochure around. “Above-water wife. Very loud. Factory standard.”

Petrakis answered my call as though I'd been drowning and he'd finally heard the splash. Refusing his generosity required four assurances that I loved his nephew, two assurances that I liked swimming, and an agreement that Hậu's gym could have the pool if Hậu approved and an engineer certified the floor. When I hung up, the driver was eating the egg Valerie had brought me.

I looked at him. He swallowed hurriedly.

“She said it was getting cold,” the driver explained.

“It was,” Valerie said. “Your telescope comes tomorrow.”

Shawna turned toward me. I held up both hands before she found the question.

“I haven't rescued anybody in space,” I said.

“Then perhaps it's preventative,” Shawna replied.

We had lived at Orchard House for almost two years. Benny Châu still owned the house; I supplied monthly appearances at his card room, which meant shaking hands, looking prosperous, and keeping my miraculous little machine somewhere it couldn't frighten the dealers. Benny's parties gained fashion. We gained a roof with room for Shawna's work and, increasingly, the delivery industry's worst impulses.

Three pieces of her collection hung from the breakfast-room curtain rail. The blue jacket was beautiful. The peach coat made me want to touch it. The black dress changed when I walked past, a dark surface discovering light along one shoulder.

Shawna caught my wrist. “Use your eyes.”

“I was reaching for breakfast,” I said.

Marisol came in with coffee and rolls, looked at my hand inside the sleeve of a couture jacket, and passed me the paper bag. “Here's something you actually own, danger.”

Outside, Saint waited beside a black car. His suit was pale green. A boy beside him held ceremonial scissors so large they needed their own seat belt.

“Ricky found those,” Saint told me before I asked.

“He paid a deposit,” Ricky said.

Saint slowly faced him. “The deposit was for a trustworthy assistant, too. We're both disappointed.”

Saint had planned a celebration of my second year owning the Wharf Circle. I'd requested breakfast at Denise's. He had heard breakfast, looked at the word, and decided it needed licensing, balloons, and a small road closure.

“What's the ribbon across?” I asked.

“Your future,” Saint said.

“Put my future in the trunk. We're late,” I said.

Bell Street had filled its windows with my face. Three portraits resembled me; one was Dad in a younger jacket; one suggested that I'd spent forty years boxing in an undisclosed republic. The sixth showed Saint wearing my coat.

“That's conceptual,” Saint explained.

Denise stood outside Bishop's beneath a bunch of gold balloons, staring at a man offering champagne to people who had come for noodles. “He's here,” Denise called. “Now move so people can pay for their actual food.”

The crowd cheered and became less permeable. A woman caught both my cheeks, kissed them, and put a hundred dollars into my palm. Her son had got into university. I'd written the employment letter: he'd carried ice at the Pearl and once asked whether my probability work would get everybody arrested faster.

“Engineering,” his mother told me proudly. “He says your machine violates a principle.”

“He'll learn several more,” I said. She laughed and kissed me again, as though I'd offered him a scholarship in violations.

At our usual table, Trinh pushed a red folder toward me. Rafa had washed everything except a black stripe at his wrist. Hậu's new gym shirt displayed COACH across a chest large enough to imply enforcement.

“You're getting a pool,” I told Hậu.

Hậu put down his spoon. “How big?”

“My wife rejected it without measuring,” I said.

“Excellent,” Hậu said.

Trinh tapped the folder. “Sign before somebody gives you a coastline.”

Denise arrived with soup, slid the folder beneath the bowl, and handed me chopsticks. “He can own things after eating.”

Saint's microphone began outside. Denise kicked the door shut. For ninety wonderful seconds I heard only spoons.

Then a white sports car mounted the curb sideways.

Its driver climbed out in a cream suit and silver motorcycle helmet. A second car stopped behind him. Two men came out carrying clubs; the helmeted man ran for our window, saw the upper pane was open, and threw a flat black case through it.

The case severed three stems of Denise's hanging plant, slapped Hậu's shoulder, and landed in my soup. Hậu looked down at the orange fan spreading across COACH.

“Bought it this morning,” Hậu said very quietly.

Outside, the driver ducked a club, trapped the arm, and drove its owner into his own white car. The second pursuer struck his knee. The helmet hit the pavement. A knife came out.

I got through Bishop's door before the knife came down. Catching the man's elbow turned the stab into an awkward invitation. I accepted, spun him across my front, and flung him over the open sports car. His back struck the opposite door. His heels hit a balloon tether and took six gold balloons with him.

The first man punched my stomach. I had just eaten two spoonfuls of soup, and he had interrupted the third. I caught his next hand and walked him backward into the second car. The door folded around his hips. He made a noise usually associated with discovering a parking charge.

“Who is he?” I asked, lifting the wrist until my captive stood on tiptoe.

“Let go,” the man said.

“That would conclude the interview very early,” I said.

The helmeted driver sat rubbing his knee. “They dislike your invitation,” he explained.

My captive tried a head-butt. I shifted him six inches and let his forehead complete its appointment with the roof. Paint flaked down his collar. I retrieved his club, bent it around the side mirror, and let him slide out of the door-shaped impression he'd made.

Saint arrived with his microphone still live. “Brief intermission, ladies and gentlemen! Please retain your—Jesus, nigga, his balloons!”

The two men pulled themselves into their car and left. One string remained through the rear window; a gold balloon followed them down Bell Street, making the escape look sponsored.

The driver removed his helmet. His hair was still perfectly combed. That interested me more than the car.

“Irene Vũ sends her invitation,” the driver said.

Trinh had brought the wet case outside wrapped in a napkin. “Aster House?”

The driver nodded. Rafa crouched beside the sports car, looked at the bent passenger door, and stood again before emotion overtook him.

“There's a postal service,” Rafa told the driver. “You can buy stamps with little birds.”

Inside the case I found a black card, a silver key, and a photograph of nine helmets around a dinner table. The date on its back fell on Friday, four weeks away. Tonight's invitation was to Aster House's Ninth Room: jacket, discretion, and permission after admission to nominate an unrelated adult club brother. Irene had added a note asking me to use the actual door.

Saint read over my shoulder. “Premium mail. Nigga, even your spam comes with attempted murder.”

Denise emerged with a mop. “Does the little key open a cupboard? We've got soup in the till.”

The driver produced an envelope Irene had supplied for complications. I passed it to Denise unopened. She counted its contents, looked from the money to the wreckage, and rested the mop against the wall.

“Lunch has availability,” Denise told him.

While Hậu took the driver inside to see about his knee, Shawna called. She had heard there was a delivery incident and hoped it hadn't become wet again.

“Aster House,” I told her. “Irene Vũ.”

Shawna went quiet. Then I heard her ask Marisol to hold a sleeve. “She's hosting my private preview. Three weeks from now. Deposit paid yesterday. She didn't mention you.”

“Some people show restraint,” I said.

“I enjoy those people. I'm coming tonight,” Shawna said.

“Jacket required,” I said.

Shawna laughed. “Fortunately, I know a woman.”

Across the street, Ricky stretched the ribbon over Bishop's entrance. Denise approached it with the delivery cart, and Saint lifted the giant scissors just as the cart's front wheel caught the satin.

“I should go,” I told Shawna. “My future is blocking Denise's vegetables.”

“Cut it loose,” Shawna said. “Bring home the husband. We have enough furniture.”

## Chapter 2: Bring Your Own Brother

That evening the doorman at Aster House permitted Shawna through immediately, checked my name twice, and attempted to return Saint to the street.

“Essential,” Saint repeated, pointing to the profession he'd supplied on the guest form.

“Promoter,” Shawna translated. “He means promoter.”

The doorman moved his finger down the list. Saint straightened his pale lapels, apparently restored to a legal category, and followed us beneath seven chimneys into a house that smelled of cedar, expensive perfume, and butter nobody intended to confess to eating.

Irene Vũ waited beside the stairs in a white dinner jacket. One ear carried a diamond big enough to influence navigation. The other was bare.

Saint leaned forward. “The asymmetry is outstanding.”

“I lost the other one in a fight,” Irene said, and kissed Shawna on both cheeks. They immediately began discussing lights and garment rails. I held my invitation until Irene finally noticed my extended hand. “And you brought someone to lift things. Excellent planning.”

“He comes with the marriage,” Shawna said.

Irene took the card and held it over a small flame. Saint reached instinctively for the curling corner. She moved the silver bowl away before he could save it.

“No souvenir invitations,” Irene told him.

“Then how do people prove they got inside?” Saint asked.

Irene considered him with professional sorrow. “Some survive without it.”

We crossed a dining room, a conservatory, and a passage of racing photographs. A burgundy-suited man held a tiny dog above his enormous cigar. The dog sneezed directly into his face, winning the argument without releasing a statement. Irene opened a green door beyond him.

The Ninth Room filled the house's rear: nine numbered booths around a sunken dance floor, bar along the garden windows, band on a platform, chandelier above the floor. The chandelier resembled a glass planet waiting to be discovered by someone's forehead.

Shawna touched my sleeve. “I'm seeing the gallery. Don't agree to anything on my behalf.”

“When have I—” I began.

“The horse wore a ribbon,” Shawna reminded me. She went with Irene. Saint spotted a singer, corrected his posture into that of an old acquaintance, and deserted me.

I was examining the chandelier when a man beside the sunken floor said, “If it falls, that's where the heavy part lands.”

He wore a black suit, an open blue shirt, and a scar near his lower lip. His hair looked as though he had pushed it back to see something difficult and then forgotten it. He held water in one hand and a folded drawing in the other.

I stepped closer to him. “Are you warning me or improving your odds?”

“I designed the support,” the man said. “People keep telling me it feels too light. Three tons apparently ought to introduce itself more firmly.”

The architect held out his hand. “Đặng Minh Khải. Khải is fine.”

“Aiden,” I said, shaking it. His attention remained on my face. I became unreasonably conscious of having one.

“Yes,” Khải said. “Your tiger painting is in the coatroom.”

My hand stopped inside his. He smiled before I looked for the door.

“A joke,” Khải explained.

“You picked something that actually exists,” I said.

“Then my condolences to the tiger,” Khải said.

At the bar he told me about An Vân, his architecture studio, and the six years he had spent translating Irene's desires into things a building could legally bear. A steam room. Hidden doors. Better acoustics for adjacent indiscretions.

“Do you ever tell her no?” I asked.

“Frequently,” Khải said. “Then I send a revised estimate.”

A woman behind him spilled champagne on a man's shoes. The man held up his other foot so she could finish. I asked Khải what people did here.

“Discover whether their courage matches their money,” Khải said. “I mostly dance. Sometimes I make an appalling decision in an excellent room.”

Irene returned before I could discover which category included me. Two brass rings lay on her tray, each engraved with a nine. She had nominated us as a pair for admission.

Khải looked at the rings, then at her. “You left that off the plans.”

“I own the house,” Irene said. “I can simplify paperwork.”

The ritual promised discretion about each other's club business and one honest request for assistance. The club called partners brothers or sisters; the words described two unrelated adults agreeing to help each other, not adoption, blood, or anything involving my family. Irene explained that a retired judge had hated the terminology, which was why it survived every revision.

“Either of you may refuse,” Irene said.

Khải lifted a ring. “What sort of help?”

“I won't ask you to move a piano,” I promised.

“You're committing early,” Khải said, and slipped it on.

I did the same. We repeated Irene's brief oath and shook hands again. His thumb brushed my knuckle when he let go. The band played a bright flourish; half the room applauded, and Saint materialized with the speed of a man hearing a cash drawer open.

An opera patron offered his private box and asked whether I liked racing. Saint assured him I adored opera. When I asked for an example, Saint supplied the expensive one, which apparently matched the man's taste perfectly.

Irene directed Khải to a velvet display case at the rear. Nine miniature cars stood beside property labels. One said THE PEARL. Another said THE DUCAL.

I put a finger on the glass. “Mine hasn't consented to being miniature.”

“Then it comes out,” Irene said. “The Crown Run. Nine crews, nine stakes. Private contests, then Briar Circuit. The winner retains his own property and takes the others.”

“And losers honor that?” I asked.

“So far,” Irene replied.

Khải looked toward the garden. I noticed the smile leaving his face and would have asked why if the music hadn't stopped.

The driver who'd brought my invitation stood in the green doorway with a pistol against his head. A bruise darkened his cheek. The man holding him had two companions, all in black. One carried a short steel baton; the other kept a hand inside his coat.

“Nobody moves,” the gunman announced.

The room produced forty separate interpretations of nobody. People crawled under tables, crouched behind chairs, and attempted to occupy the same booth. The cigar man put his dog inside his jacket. At the gallery entrance, Shawna caught Marisol's arm and drew her behind the stone jamb. I had a clear view of them for one instant before they vanished safely behind it.

Khải caught my wrist. “Service door on the left. Keep people out of the middle.”

The gunman fired upward to improve compliance. His bullet broke a side brace. The chandelier's outer ring lurched away from its center and began sweeping down toward a booth containing two motionless guests.

I crossed a table in two strides and caught the ring. My shoes punched through the tabletop. Glass climbed my trouser legs; crystal rained down my shoulders. I stopped with one foot through the table and the other standing in a wine bucket, holding a portion of the ceiling while the rest tried to orbit me.

“Get under my arm!” I shouted. The guests scrambled clear. One dragged his chair after him, then abandoned it when I looked at him.

Khải reached a panel behind the curtain. He pulled its handle, planted a foot against the wall, and turned a maintenance wheel. The ring lifted a few inches. The gunman swung his pistol toward him.

I broke off a loose crystal stem and threw it. It struck the gunman's forearm with a wet crack. His pistol fired into the floor; the driver dropped and rolled behind the doorjamb. The gunman screamed into his own sleeve.

The baton man rushed Khải. Khải swung the service door open into his face, shut it far enough to acquire momentum, and opened it again. The second impact knocked the man back across the threshold. When he lunged a third time, Khải fetched a maintenance bar and struck his knee. The man sat abruptly, feet inside one room and ambitions inside another.

The last intruder reached me, assessed my trapped feet and occupied hands, and punched me.

I lifted the leg wearing the wine bucket and kicked him squarely in the chest. He skidded across the dance floor seated inside the momentum, hit the piano stool, and disappeared beneath the instrument. The pianist retrieved his drink before returning to cover.

Khải cranked the mechanism again. The chandelier rose clear. I pulled my foot through the table, shook off the bucket, and reached the gunman as he tried to find his pistol with the hand I'd damaged. I drove him into the decorative plaster. The plaster accepted his shoulders, produced a small white cloud, and declined to release him upright.

Irene appeared with two of her own men. I gave her the pistol grip-first.

“I asked for the actual door,” Irene said.

“They used it,” I told her.

“Yes. We'll be discussing admissions,” Irene said.

Shawna took my hand. Blood ran across my palm from a long cut. She turned it toward the light, moved a crystal shard with the edge of a napkin, and pressed the napkin into the wound.

Khải stood beside us with the maintenance bar. Shawna glanced from it to the raised chandelier. “Your work?”

“The parts still attached,” Khải said.

“Good parts,” Shawna told him.

He laughed. I watched his mouth. Then I remembered my wife was holding my hand.

Saint climbed the intact end of the ruined table while Irene's men carried the intruders away. “Ladies and gentlemen, kindly resume enjoying yourselves! Nobody charge Aiden for the table. He brought his own service staff.”

The band began uncertainly. Irene clapped in time until everybody accepted the fiction that the evening had merely changed tempo. The driver acquired a drink, an ice pack, and the tiny dog, which evidently considered him safer than the cigar.

Khải looked down at our matching rings. “Quick work on the request for help.”

“You still owe me a dance,” I said.

Khải smiled, slower this time. “I remember.”

## Chapter 3: The Good Table

On Sunday Dad stood in his garden holding a tree while Mom decided where trees belonged.

The tree came in a stone planter. A delivery crew had brought a crane and a page describing circumstances under which they would deny everything. Dad had sent them around the side for water. When we came through the gate, he had the planter against his stomach and a leaf resting on his nose.

“Left,” Mom told him.

Dad moved left. Mom frowned. He moved back before she spoke, and her frown deepened at the presumption.

I bowed. Dad nodded over the leaves, waited for me to straighten, and put the planter into my arms.

My heels sank into the lawn. Then the fronts of my shoes followed. I stopped the stone two inches lower than he'd given it to me and discovered several muscles that were apparently available only by appointment.

“Allergies,” Dad explained, rubbing his nose.

Shawna passed us carrying pastries. “That shirt is clean, Aiden.”

“I'm discussing it with the soil,” I told her.

Mom kissed Shawna, took the box, and returned to the problem of sunlight. “First place. We were right first.”

Dad relieved me one-handed under the base, carried the tree back across the lawn, and set it down without making any of the holes I'd made. One delivery man returned in time to see him dust his hands. The man looked at the crane's control pad, pressed its emergency stop, and went back for more water.

Inside, Great-Grandpa Tín occupied the good table under two newspapers. One was yesterday's; the other carried an advertisement for a shop I remembered burning down when I was little.

“Which one has the news?” I asked.

“Old one,” Tín said. “I've had time to check it.”

Granddad Hòa sat beside him sorting screws in a saucer. A pencil occupied the same place behind his ear it had occupied in photographs older than me. He looked at my shoes, then through the window at my impressions in the lawn.

“Plant early,” Hòa advised. “You might get a second crop.”

I bent to kiss Tín's cheek. He caught my chin and turned my face to the light.

“Who hit you?” Tín asked.

“A chandelier,” I said.

“Did it learn anything?” Tín asked.

“I held it up,” I told him.

Tín released me and shook his head at Shawna. “Slow making friends, this one.”

He cleared the papers instantly so she could sit. Mom arrived with soup and stopped at the newly exposed tabletop.

“I've asked for an hour,” Mom said.

“She brought pastries,” Tín explained.

“I brought lunch,” Mom said.

“We're still discussing that,” Tín said, picking up his chopsticks.

Saint arrived in orange. His suit was so bright the kitchen tiles appeared to have changed color. In both hands he carried a velvet box, which he presented to Tín with the solemnity of a man about to start another problem.

“A token for Uncle,” Saint announced.

“Does it bark?” Tín asked. “Last nigga brought me a companion. Companion ate my sock.”

Inside the box lay a medal on a red ribbon. CENTURY CLUB, it read, with STILL OUTSIDE underneath.

Dad lowered the soup pot very slowly. Mom covered her mouth. Tín turned the medal over, weighed it, and looked at Saint.

“Six years late,” Tín said.

“Development takes time,” Saint replied.

“Add six,” Tín said.

“To the ribbon?” Saint asked.

“Medals,” Tín said. He put it on before Saint could renegotiate history.

Lunch moved around him. Shawna described the preview's final six pieces while Mom debated invitation paper. Shawna wanted cards buyers could fit in handbags; Mom felt an expensive invitation should announce itself by weight. Hòa slid Shawna a small dish and proposed giving everybody two cards, since most customers possessed two hands. Saint claimed this family could fix government.

“We haven't fixed you,” Mom told him. “Eat before making the program bigger.”

Dad noticed the stitches across my palm when I reached for tea. I told him about Aster House, Khải's support mechanism, the attack, and the Crown Run.

Mom asked whether Irene had refunded anything. Shawna explained that flowers had arrived before breakfast with assurances about searches at the collection preview.

“Lovely,” Mom said. “Put them beside the metal detector.”

Dad waited until I'd finished. “Which property?”

“The Pearl,” I said. “For the chance at the Ducal.”

The hotel stood over Briar Circuit with thirty private suites, its gaming floor beneath a glass restaurant overlooking the bend. Thành owned both. I wanted the room, its guests, and him less comfortably involved in our neighborhood.

“Buy it,” Dad said.

“He isn't selling,” I said.

“People sell,” Dad told me.

Tín chewed, watched him, and touched his new medal. “Listen to the manners on him. A price tag has done more than I managed.”

Dad ignored that. He asked who held the deeds. Irene. Who checked Irene? I would, with Trinh. Dad nodded once. Mom informed me that winning did not authorize bringing the hotel into her garden. Saint began to explain a commemorative alternative, saw her face, and reached for rice.

Across the table, Shawna had stopped eating.

“You like the Pearl,” Shawna said.

“I do,” I said.

“Then talk to Trinh before you start exchanging her work for a view,” Shawna said.

“Her operating share will be excluded,” I said. “The people and their work stay protected. I haven't signed yet.”

Shawna nodded. She didn't applaud. I was capable of protecting my partner's livelihood without receiving a medal for discovering the concept, though the household offered conflicting examples.

After lunch Dad asked me to remove a short iron rail beside the garden path. Mom wanted it gone before someone tripped. He held one end with two fingers while I took the other and pulled.

Nothing moved.

“Roots,” Dad said, examining the soil.

I pulled harder. My post rose slightly. His stayed put. “Let yours go.”

“It will spring,” Dad replied.

“I can hold a rail,” I said.

Dad looked at my palm. “Four stitches.”

I planted my feet and pulled with my shoulder. The rail squealed. Dad adjusted his grip; the whole thing swung gently toward him, taking me across the soft ground until one knee struck the path. In the kitchen window Saint stood holding a pastry like a ringside microphone.

“Uncle takes that round,” Saint observed.

Tín appeared behind him, medal shining. “What are you two doing?”

“Roots,” Dad said again.

Tín came down, put one hand beneath the center of the rail, and lifted. Both posts came out carrying lumps of concrete. Dad released his end. I held on too long, rose onto my toes, and let go when the rail reached my shoulders.

“Move, nigga. I'm gardening,” Tín told me, and carried the whole barrier to the shed in one hand.

Water spurted into one of the holes. Dad looked down at the severed sprinkler pipe. Tín looked back at him as though the pipe had always been a personal failing.

“I've waited a hundred years,” Tín said when Dad suggested patience. “Your mother wasn't even here for most of it.”

Mom leaned through the door. “Keep my mother out of the plumbing.”

While Dad fetched a hose and Tín explained agricultural authority, Hòa crossed the lawn with his saucer of screws. A stone sphere beside the path began rolling as he passed. It was wider than his shoulders and headed toward the planter. I took one step.

Hòa set the saucer on top of the moving stone. One fingertip rested against its side. The sphere stopped so gently that none of the screws shifted.

He examined one, returned it, and looked at me.

“Wrong screw,” Hòa said.

I looked at the sphere. He looked toward the shed, picked up his saucer, and walked away. Dad was arguing with the hose. Tín was defending the medal's resistance to mud. Nobody else seemed to have noticed.

That evening on our terrace I called Trinh. She listened to the wager, asked three questions Irene hadn't answered, then proposed a fourth that made me get a pen.

“We go together,” Trinh said. “To every place where my games meet your imagination.”

I agreed. Shawna brought two glasses outside after I hung up. The river moved below the garden lights. She sat, put her feet in my lap, and let me work a thumb into the arch of one foot.

“You want the Run,” Shawna said.

“Yes,” I said.

“Then come home afterward. Tell me what's happening before somebody sells me the video,” Shawna said.

“I will,” I promised.

She kissed me, one hand warm on my cheek. Her hair smelled of hotel soap she'd taken because she maintained we'd paid enough to own the bathroom.

My phone lit beside us. Khải offered to partner me for Thursday's cards. He could count, lie, and dress himself, his message said. A useful combination.

Shawna followed my glance. “The architect? Ask him about fixing the pool's shape.”

I laughed and put the phone face down. Her feet remained in my lap. At the time it felt like the easiest thing in the world to stay exactly where I was.

## Chapter 4: A Piano in the Pot

On Thursday I signed away the possible future of a building, ate dinner, and found a piano standing between me and the consequences.

It occupied Aster House's gallery on a wheeled platform. Two tuners circled it anxiously while a woman explained that a European prince had blessed its wood. Trinh asked which prince. The woman smiled as though geography had become intrusive.

“Good,” Trinh said. “A thoroughly documented blessing.”

The piano was black, immensely long, and inlaid with little stars. A gold E shone on its side. Saint hovered one finger over it until I told him touching it would enroll him in lessons.

“I could learn,” Saint said.

“Begin with a rest,” I suggested.

We'd spent the afternoon over Irene's wager papers. The Pearl's building and my interest in its games could enter the pot. Its operating company, Trinh's share, and the people's employment could not. Trinh made three changes. Irene accepted two, contested the third, and finally agreed when Trinh began putting her pen away. The Ducal title already bore Thành's signature. There were nine stakes, nine crews, and now a very expensive reason for me to show up.

“He wins a building,” Trinh had told Irene across the small office table. “He does not win authority to replace my managers before the transfer terms are settled.”

“Nobody is promising that,” Irene had replied.

Trinh turned the page toward her. “Then you won't mind removing the sentence that does.”

I'd leaned over to read the clause. Irene had looked at me, then at Trinh's capped pen. Outside the office Saint had been asking a waiter whether piano lessons could count as cultural sponsorship. The world kept offering me distracting things while the person beside me protected the room we'd actually built.

“Take it out,” I'd told Irene.

“I might have accepted a revision,” Irene had said.

Trinh had uncapped her pen. “You're about to.”

Now at the card table she checked the sealed copy once more before putting it in her bag. I caught her eye and nodded. She nodded back, then moved Saint far enough from the stakes that his sleeves could no longer make promises for him.

The preliminary card game settled starting positions and allowed side bets. Apparently none of us could digest dinner unless something we liked might leave with a stranger.

Khải arrived carrying a small wooden box. In it lay a plain watch with a worn brown strap. He'd bought it after his first serious commission. He held the box a moment before handing it to Irene, who accepted it more carefully than the briefcase of cash she'd just taken from another guest.

“You're willing to lose that?” I asked Khải.

“No,” Khải said. “That is the unpleasant distinction between a stake and something you leave beside a bin.”

The piano's owner introduced himself as Ellis Crowe. He wore a pearl tiepin, had an orange face shaped like a sturdy suitcase, and shook hands without letting his other hand leave his drink. Petra, his thin partner in a velvet jacket, watched the pianist test the keys with the expression of someone wondering if she could escape inside the instrument.

“The young king,” Crowe said to me. “Do you play?”

“We'll find out when it lands,” I said.

Petra coughed into a laugh. Crowe looked at her and missed Thành entering.

Other people didn't. Conversations shortened as Thành crossed the gallery in a brown suit, white scar above his brow. Long followed, taller than Hậu and built wide enough to make the doorway seem like a clerical error. One flattened ear lay scarred against his head. The ring on his little finger looked small until it passed beside the piano keys.

Thành offered his hand. I gave him enough resistance to discourage a measurement.

“I enjoyed the Pearl,” Thành said.

“You're welcome back. Reserve a table,” I said.

“I prefer keys,” Thành said.

“Then imagine how much I'll enjoy yours,” I told Thành.

Irene rang a bell before either of us improved the remark. At the round table, partners stood behind players. Khải took his place behind me. Trinh and Saint stayed along the wall, where Trinh could supervise Saint's promises without interrupting play.

The first hand cost me fifteen thousand dollars. Crowe lost twice that on the second and ordered another drink loudly enough to imply recovery. Thành folded early. His eyes spent more time on the partners than on the cards.

Khải leaned near my ear. “Crowe gets reckless when Petra looks bored.”

“She's been bored since she got here,” I murmured.

## Chapter 9: The Breakfast Alibi

In the morning Khải made coffee, and that was harder to dismiss than the night.

## Chapter 10: Heavyweight Hospitality

“I know,” Granddad replied.

“Then why the inspection?” I asked.

Hòa held the green packet toward me. “She prefers the other. I prefer these. A man should be certain before bringing an argument home.”

He put the packet behind the vegetables, then handed me the bag. At the next stall he asked the price of a little wooden toy missing a wheel. The woman said it wasn't for sale; her grandson had broken it. Hòa examined the axle, asked if she still had the wheel, and waited while she searched a drawer. He took both pieces in his pocket when she found it.

“Tomorrow,” Hòa told her.

She thanked him and put an extra orange in our bag. He didn't argue about that price. I thought about the saucer of screws at the family table and let the next question wait until tea.

We stopped for tea near the entrance. A man delivering rice sacks recognized Hòa, came over with both hands extended, and addressed him as Teacher. They spoke about children, a bad knee, and a broken gate. When the man kept looking at me, Granddad finally introduced his grandson.

“I know,” the delivery man said.

“Then why look?” Hòa asked. The man laughed nervously and returned to his trolley.

I asked what Granddad had taught him. Hòa put a cracker on my plate instead of making the lesson available.

Years before he'd shown me a photograph of a collapsed bridge and a locomotive held on bent rails, with Tín standing in the background. The writing on the back had directed me to a man who never answered properly. I brought it up again.

“Eat,” Hòa said. “Your tea is getting lonely.”

Outside, somebody shouted. The rice truck began rolling down the loading slope, its driver running beside the open window. It crossed a drain, gained speed, and headed toward the market entrance. Our table stood behind bollards that would have stopped a car. The truck appeared likely to bring the bollards inside with it.

I stood. Hòa caught my sleeve.

“Eggs,” Granddad said, pointing at the bags.

I picked one up. By the time I found the right protest, he was through the entrance.

The truck reached him. Hòa put one palm against the bumper. Metal groaned the length of the vehicle. Its rear tires turned for another moment, then stalled. The driver dropped from the window and stared at the old man standing beneath his grille.

Granddad shifted his hand away from the dent he'd made, pushed the truck back far enough to clear the door, and pointed up the loading lane.

“Park uphill,” Hòa told the driver.

He came back inside, sat, and lifted his tea. I remained standing with the eggs.

“None broken?” Hòa asked.

“You stopped a truck,” I said.

“It stopped here,” Granddad said.

“With your hand,” I said.

Hòa considered me. “Would you prefer my head?”

His phone rang. Tín wanted to know whether he'd bought the wrong noodles yet. Hòa listened, looked at me, and relayed another question: was I asking things? I said yes. He told Tín yes. The laughter came through clearly enough that people at the next table looked over.

Irene called before we finished shopping. Mick was awake and ready to speak. I took Granddad home first, carried in the bags, and received Hương's approving nod when the blue packets emerged. Hòa kept one green packet behind his back until I turned toward the door. I left him to his domestic ambition.

At Irene's safe apartment Mick sat upright with a bandaged arm and broth on a tray. Irene occupied the bedside chair in the same white jacket she'd worn at the clinic. Khải stood by the window. I took a place at the foot of the bed.

Mick told us the crash had been arranged. A track vehicle entered Oscar's path at the bend. Oscar hit the barrier, stopped in the service lane, and remained conscious, bleeding from a shoulder and furious because he knew why.

Long arrived with two men. They said the doctor waited in the silver car. Mick helped put Oscar inside. Oscar pressed his racing glove against the wound.

“I followed with his helmet,” Mick said. “Old garage under the north stand. I heard Thành there.”

His hand shook around the cup. Irene took it before he spilled it. Khải moved closer to the bed.

Oscar had threatened to tell the other entrants, Mick said. The Run had become a way to collect owners who wouldn't sell.

“Thành told Long to finish it,” Mick said.

Nobody hurried to fill the silence. I watched Mick worry the blanket between his fingers. Irene kept the cup in both hands.

“I'm sorry,” Mick told her. “I got frightened.”

“You tried to find me,” Irene said. “Even frightened.”

After a while he continued. Private crews had installed new gates, sealed passages, reinforced service doors, and ordered him to remove emergency signs. When he refused they replaced him with a Meridian man and kept him in the old stalls. There had been other captives: drivers from last year's Run, a mechanic, people who knew how the machinery worked. Thành used threats against their families to keep them working. They'd been moved before Khải and I found Mick.

“A man with keys,” Mick said. “Chlorine smell. They called him Baths.”

Irene rose. “Mariner Rooms.”

She explained Thành's private bathhouse, cards above and other business below. Khải asked her not to go alone. Irene replied sharply that forty years of survival hadn't come from walking into rooms because men said they were safe. Then she looked back at Mick and softened her voice, promising to find the others.

Outside his bedroom she gave me the photograph of Lucia, now in a clean envelope. Oscar's glove stayed with Irene.

“I want those people alive,” Irene said. “And I want to see Thành lose.”

“People first,” I told her.

Irene nodded. Her hand remained on the envelope a moment before she let it go.

On the stairs Khải showed me a photograph already circulating: Hòa in front of the rice truck. The caption described him as my elderly relative.

“That'll annoy him,” I said.

“Elderly?” Khải asked.

“Relative. He has a name,” I said.

Khải laughed quietly for the first time since I'd arrived. I touched the hand resting on my sleeve. He let me hold it for a moment, then we went down into the street.

## Chapter 12: Men Who Take Their Coats Off

Vernon Hale had bought three railway carriages and four miles of track so he could feed people dinner while preventing them from leaving it.

His station had become a kitchen. Once a month his train crept into the hills, stopped long enough for the wealthy to misunderstand the stars, and returned them to their cars. The Crown Run supper added an auction of driver favors and the drawing of sealed cards for our first city destinations.

Tess refused before Trinh finished reading the invitation. “I don't eat aboard things I can't steer.”

Hậu wanted to come. Mara pointed at his sling and explained that the doctor had said rest, a word that didn't contain railway no matter how enthusiastically he pronounced it. He remained at the gym under protest.

I took Khải. I told Shawna I was taking Saint. Saint was coming too; I'd found a truthful component and used it to carry the lie.

At the little station beyond the quarry, lamps lit the platform and a string trio worked beneath an awning. A conductor handed out champagne and warned people about the gaps between carriages. Saint asked whether his microphone needed its own luggage ticket.

Khải arrived with an overnight bag. He had an early site visit and intended to stay at the studio.

“There's a bed?” I asked.

“There are deadlines,” Khải said. “The bed followed.”

We'd seen each other four times since Rusk's party: twice among other people, once with Shawna at the gym, and once alone at An Vân. That last visit had ended with us kissing beside the model houses until an assistant knocked to ask about lunch. We'd both said no. The assistant had gone home early with the quiet efficiency of somebody declining responsibility.

Now Khải passed me on the platform and brushed my fingers. “Does Saint take the microphone everywhere?”

“Sometimes it takes him,” I said.

Hale welcomed us in a conductor's cap he'd clearly selected for himself. Saint praised a man who provided his own authority. The first carriage held a lounge and bar; the second a long dining table; the third a card room with an open viewing platform at the rear. Wood shone, brass gleamed, and the upholstery made sitting feel like an act of borrowing.

Long stood outside the window speaking to a hired guard. Yellow bruising spread along his jaw. He touched it, then pointed at me. I lifted my water. He left before the train moved.

Across the dining table sat Celeste Arden, who now owned the Lantern. Irene had sold the business after Oscar vanished while keeping enough interest to protect Lucia. Celeste knew the arrangement and the man who'd wanted to exploit it.

“Thành offered condolences with the price,” Celeste said. “He expected a discount for being sensitive.”

“Did you give him one?” I asked.

Celeste tore her bread. “I offered the same condolences for free. Economical.”

The train began to move. Somebody applauded Hale as though he'd invented wheels. Soup shivered in bowls designed to make spilling a permanent social record. Beside me, Khải watched the lights pass across the window.

“I used to draw trains,” Khải said.

“When you were little?” I asked.

“Last week. A client wanted one in his restaurant,” Khải said.

“Where would the kitchen go?” I asked.

“I asked that,” Khải said. “He was relieved to discover it was my problem.”

Under the table his knee touched mine. I let mine stay. We listened to Celeste describe her driver while Hale tried to demonstrate the cap's authenticity to Saint. It would have been an ordinary dinner if I hadn't arranged to be dishonest inside it.

After dessert we moved to the rear carriage. Hale presented a lacquered box of sealed route cards. Each team drew one. Ours bore a black crown pressed into red wax. I passed it to Dennis, Trinh's quiet representative, who had spent dinner resisting Saint's efforts to recruit him into publicity.

“Don't let him open it,” I told Dennis.

“I understand mystery,” Saint objected.

“You sell advance tickets,” I said. Dennis tucked the card inside his jacket.

Khải went onto the viewing platform. I followed into cool air. The track wound through a shallow cutting; scrub brushed the lamps along the line. We stood at the rail listening to the wheels beneath us.

“When do you stop waiting to be noticed?” Khải asked.

I considered saying I didn't. He glanced at me and waited until I abandoned it.

“Sometimes,” I said.

“When?” Khải asked.

I looked at him. He smiled without rewarding the answer.

“That's quick,” Khải said. “Try the actual question.”

I rested a hand beside his. “When I'm making something work. When the pieces stop being people watching me and become pieces.”

“That I believe,” Khải said.

He told me about his first studio, a damp room behind a friend's shop. One night a leak ruined two weeks of drawings. He sat on the floor laughing because otherwise he'd have taken the ceiling personally. He redrew everything, better and angrier.

“That sounds like you,” I said.

“You don't know enough yet,” Khải said.

“I want to,” I told him.

Khải turned and kissed me. Brief, warm, deliberate. His hand remained over mine after he stepped back, until we heard the latch behind us.

The conductor stood in the doorway with a pistol where the champagne had been.

“Inside,” the conductor said.

Khải went still. I moved forward with my hands visible. When the man shifted the muzzle to give us room, I caught his wrist and drove it into the frame. The shot punched through the roof. My other hand struck under his chin. His knees folded. I pulled him onto the platform, took the gun, and kept his head from going through the rail only because I was already holding him.

Inside, three armed men had the room. One pressed Hale against the card table. Another gripped Dennis's jacket and reached for the route card. The third blocked the door to the dining carriage, where guests crouched. Saint had attempted to conceal himself beneath a chair which hid perhaps a quarter of his ambition.

I fired at the lamp above the nearest gunman. Glass and darkness fell over him. Khải pulled the rear door open and shouted for guests to get low. I crossed while the gunman ducked, turned his pistol toward the floor, and struck his temple with mine. He fell across a chair.

Dennis's captor fired. I hauled the fallen man between us. The bullet struck the panel by my shoulder. Dennis drove an elbow into his captor's face and dropped beneath the table.

The train braked hard. Chairs slid. The gunman came over the card table toward me as though the furniture had decided to deliver him. I caught his jacket, helped him across, and put his head through the side window. Glass burst into the dark. His feet drummed against the table until I pulled him inside and let him drop.

At the connecting door Khải threw a heavy tray into the third man's face. The man staggered through the opening. Khải pushed after him; the train lurched, the door swung, and his foot slipped between the cars.

I reached the threshold in time to see him hanging by one hand. The coupling moved beneath his shoes. The gunman had fallen onto the gravel and was rolling clear alongside the slowing train. Khải's other hand reached toward me.

I caught his wrist. Pain tore across the stitched palm. I tightened my grip and hauled him up. He struck my chest; we fell backward into the carriage together.

“Door,” Khải said against my shoulder.

I kicked it shut. For one second I kept an arm around him because he was there and because he'd nearly not been. Then a shot sounded farther forward.

Khải got up. “I'll get them to the rear.”

I nodded and crossed the dining carriage. Wine ran over the floor. Celeste knelt beside an injured waiter, holding a napkin against his arm. She pointed toward the lounge and held up two fingers.

The first man stepped through as I reached the connecting door. I drove it into him, caught his necktie, and pulled his face against the brass handle. His gun fell. I kicked it under a table and hit him again when he tried to rise.

The last gunman stood at the lounge's front exit with a radio. He saw me, dropped it, and fired. I went behind the bar. Bottles burst above my head. Glass cut my cheek. I caught the edge of the marble counter and lifted.

The top tore from its base. I shoved the slab across the aisle. It struck the gunman at chest height and pinned him to the wall before sliding down. His pistol landed beside a bottle of cherry syrup. He made small sounds behind the marble. I left him there.

Outside, a van waited beside the track with its rear doors open. The man next to it stared through the window at the bar top, shut the doors, and drove away before I reached him.

The engineer had remained at his controls. He brought us back to the station, where Hale's staff found the real conductor locked in a cupboard. The attackers had boarded before supper as hired security. Hale had paid them to check invitations. Somebody else had made a more persuasive offer.

Dennis showed me the route card, seal intact. “Didn't open it.”

Saint emerged carrying a cushion. “Refund.”

Hale looked at him weakly. “For which part?”

Saint gestured toward the train. “Select something.”

We waited until the waiter had been taken to a doctor and Trinh's people had arrived. Khải sat beside me in the car while I called Shawna. Saint had texted that we were safe without explaining why, so she was already awake.

I told her about the attack. Then I said I was staying at the Pearl because the crew needed me.

After I hung up Khải stared through the windshield. “You can drop me off.”

“I want to come,” I said.

“Tonight,” Khải said. “I know what you want tonight.”

“It isn't only tonight,” I said.

Khải turned toward me. “Then stop giving tomorrow to somebody else to deal with.”

At An Vân he washed my cut cheek and put a dressing over it. I sat on the narrow bed behind his office while he found another shirt.

“Leave this one in existence,” Khải said, throwing it to me.

He stayed in the doorway a while before sitting beside me. I put a hand at the back of his neck. He leaned into it. The argument remained; we didn't have to forget it to want each other.

We talked about Oscar, the train, and the man Khải had once lived with, who'd organized their whole life around eventually leaving it. Khải asked about Shawna. I told him she was talented and funny, stubborn and good to me.

“Don't make her smaller when you explain this,” Khải said.

“I won't,” I said.

“Keep that one,” Khải told me, looking directly into my face.

We kissed. He drew me down beside him. Outside, freight trains moved past the windows while the studio went quiet.

In the morning the cat slept on my chest. Khải photographed me in his shirt beneath the tiny inspector, then showed me when I woke.

“Approved,” Khải said.

I smiled at the picture and asked him to send it. He did.

## Chapter 13: Your Wife Has a Show

Shawna sewed RETURN UPRIGHT inside my jacket.

I read it while she checked the lapels. The suit was dark green, nearly black until light touched it. Wearing it made me look like a man whose planning extended beyond the current room.

“Instructions for staff?” I asked.

“Whoever finds you,” Shawna said. She turned toward Marisol. “Last truck?”

We had ninety minutes until the collection preview at Aster House. The final garment truck hadn't arrived. Racks stood along the gallery wall; chairs faced the low runway beneath the garden windows. Khải had helped place the lights earlier in the week so the final black dress would change as its model turned. Today he was upstairs checking service doors with Irene after the train attack.

I'd seen him briefly on arrival. Shawna had seen me see him. She had said nothing.

Mom divided invitation cards into two piles, continuing her campaign for the heavier paper. Dad carried in a rack. Tín followed carrying one empty hanger.

“Proportion,” Tín explained when I looked at his contribution.

Hòa sat beside the emergency exit with Hương, peeling an orange over a napkin. She examined the seam of her sleeve and made approving sounds while Shawna pretended she couldn't hear them.

Saint arrived without a microphone in his hands. Shawna looked surprised. He opened his cream jacket to show the small wireless one clipped inside.

“Compact,” Saint said.

“Unclip it,” Shawna told him.

“I can whisper through—” Saint began.

“Darius,” Shawna said.

Saint unfastened it. Marisol locked it in a drawer. He watched the key disappear with the grief of an artist whose instrument had been censored before anyone heard it.

The missing truck called. Marisol listened and handed the phone to Shawna. Men at the lower service gate were conducting an unscheduled delivery inspection. One had begun forcing the rear latch.

“Lock the cab,” Shawna told the driver. “I'm coming.”

She was halfway to the passage before I followed. Mom came too, invitation cards still in her hand. Irene intercepted us at the stairs, heard what had happened, and sent her men ahead through another route.

In the narrow delivery court, a van blocked the truck. Three men in gray jackets stood around it: clipboard, hand inside jacket, metal tool at rear latch. Shawna went straight to the last one.

“Take that off my truck,” Shawna said.

The man turned, saw me descending behind her, and held up the tool. “Inspection.”

“The name on the label is Nguyễn Atelier. I'm Nguyễn.” Shawna pointed at his hand. “That does not become permission when you say it twice.”

Clipboard stepped toward her. Mom removed the paper from his board before he could display it.

“Wrong address,” Mom said after one glance. “And you've stretched the logo.”

He reached for the page. Mom folded it into her pocket and looked at his feet. “Wrong shoes too. I can't fix everything.”

The man at the cab pulled a pistol. I caught his arm against the truck before the muzzle cleared his coat. His elbow struck metal. He dropped the gun into my hand with a cry sharp enough to make the driver flinch behind the glass. I turned him and pressed his face against the cab.

At the rear, the tool man seized Shawna's sleeve. She drove her palm into his nose, stamped his foot, and used the wrist he'd supplied to pull his face into the rear handle. He struck it mouth-first. The latch remained closed. His nose did not.

“Imported fabric,” Shawna told him, peeling his fingers away.

Clipboard lunged at Mom. She stepped aside, caught the back of his jacket, and pitched him over the lower rail of a wheeled rack. He rolled across the court bent at the waist, legs fluttering, until the rack hit the van and dented its door. Mom followed, took his ear, and lifted his face.

“Who printed it?” Mom asked.

He made a high sound.

“Try words,” Mom suggested.

Irene's men arrived. They took custody of the intruders and their damaged theories of intimidation. The driver climbed out shaking with anger. Shawna opened the back herself and checked every garment case before stepping down.

One latch had suffered. The work was untouched.

“You did exactly right,” Shawna told the driver.

“They said they'd ruin the delivery,” the driver said.

“They've ruined a rack,” Shawna said.

“And stationery,” Mom added, reclaiming the clipboard.

Irene identified Meridian's jackets quietly. She would cover the entrances. Shawna met her eyes and told her the evening would remain about the collection. Irene agreed. Then Shawna instructed me to carry the cases upstairs. I carried them.

No announcement greeted our return. Marisol had kept the fittings moving. Dad had placed every chair on its mark. Tín had found a better seat and ordered tea for Hương. Mom joined Shawna behind the screens. I stood by the runway with a piece of torn gray sleeve still in my hand.

Saint removed it and put it in a bin. “Your wife has a show, nigga. Be a chair for half an hour.”

I sat.

The guests arrived in bright, expensive groups: singers, buyers, store owners, women whose money couldn't be located in any ordinary profession. Men from the Circle brought partners who had required them to show actual interest in clothes. Denise wore deep blue and carried her mother's little bag. Loan arrived in a white jacket Shawna had altered, looked at the flowers, and asked nobody to tell her the cost until she'd enjoyed them.

Irene welcomed everybody. She spoke about the fabrics correctly and omitted my name completely. The room belonged to Shawna before the first model stepped out.

The dark coat moved down the runway. I'd seen it pinned and unfinished on the table; under these lights its lining flashed with each stride, disappearing when the model stopped. The room watched. Shawna stood partly behind a curtain with her hands clasped. One finger moved at the turn, adjusting a seam nobody else could see.

The blue jacket followed. Then the peach coat. Pieces I'd known as loose sleeves, pinned shoulders, and arguments over thread came into the room complete and moving. The conversations stopped without Irene asking. Buyers leaned toward one another, then stopped whispering when the next garment appeared.

Finally the black dress entered. Light gathered along one side and vanished when the model changed direction. A woman in front leaned forward so suddenly her glasses slid down her nose. She didn't push them back until the model turned again.

Shawna saw her. Her hands unclasped.

Applause started before the model reached the curtain. Marisol pushed Shawna onto the runway. My wife came out laughing, embarrassed and delighted, and brought Marisol beside her for another bow. I stood with the room. Saint had somehow retrieved the microphone. Denise took it from him before he could breathe into it.

“Clap, nigga,” Denise told him. He clapped.

Afterward buyers surrounded Shawna. Cards changed hands. Marisol recorded appointments. A woman wanted to take the final dress immediately. Shawna said it belonged to the collection until the last photograph was finished.

“I'll pay more,” the buyer offered.

“Then I'll make you your own,” Shawna said.

The buyer smiled, and Marisol looked over Shawna's shoulder at me. I lifted my eyebrows. She grinned and hurried away to record another order.

A second buyer asked how many pieces the atelier could deliver in six weeks. Shawna brought Marisol into the conversation instead of producing a number to impress the room. They checked the work schedule on the tablet. Marisol named the fitting days they couldn't move. Shawna counted backward on her fingers and gave a quantity smaller than the buyer wanted.

“Another studio offered twice that,” the buyer said.

“They may have twice the hands,” Shawna replied. “These are the ones you'll receive on time.”

The woman looked at Marisol, then at the clothes. She asked about colors. Shawna took her to the peach coat and held the lining toward the light. I could hear the difference between the voice my wife used when she hoped something would work and the one she used when she knew it did. This was the second voice.

Mom came up beside me and watched them settle the order. She didn't interrupt or offer to negotiate. When Marisol returned to the table, Mom quietly gave her the heavier invitation stock to use beneath the tablet's wobbling corner. Marisol tested it, grinned, and left the paper there.

“Both kinds useful,” Mom told me, satisfied.

Dad stood by the tea table. “She did well.”

“She did,” I agreed.

He watched Shawna speaking to a buyer. “Don't stand in her light.”

“I'm over here,” I said.

Dad looked at me. “Keep knowing where you are.” He took his tea to Mom.

When the crowd thinned, Khải came down from the upper gallery. He congratulated Shawna. She thanked him for the lighting and asked about a possible showroom. For a few minutes they were two people who understood how to make a room hold work properly. I stood beside them, wanting to leave and wanting to stay.

Shawna noticed the dressing on his knuckle.

“Train?” Shawna asked.

“A door,” Khải replied.

“Thank you for helping him,” Shawna said.

Khải looked down at his hand. “He helped me too.”

After he left, Marisol handed Shawna our household tablet. We'd brought it for photographs and lists. Shawna began swiping through the show pictures while I helped Dad move a rack.

Ten minutes later she called my name.

She stood in the little room behind the gallery. The tablet lay on the table. On its screen I slept in Khải's studio bed, wearing his shirt, his cat on my chest. Months before, I'd set the shared photograph library to put new pictures where we could both see them. It had obeyed perfectly.

Shawna looked from the image to me. “Different back room.”

I remained in the doorway. She turned the tablet face down. Through the wall came music, a buyer asking for her, Marisol laughing at something ordinary.

“We're finishing my evening,” Shawna said. “Then you drive me home.”

I began her name. She picked up her glass and walked past me before I reached anything useful after it.

When I returned to the gallery she was arranging an order for three stores. She answered the buyer's questions, corrected a delivery date, and smiled for the last photograph. I stayed where I could help without requiring her to look at me.

She didn't, until it was time to leave.

## Chapter 14: A Door That Closes Properly

At home Shawna removed her earrings, put them in the dish on her dressing table, and unclasped her necklace. I stood by the bedroom door while she finished. Downstairs Valerie moved the last garment cases into the workroom.

“Close it,” Shawna said.

I closed the door. She met my eyes in the mirror.

“How long?” Shawna asked.

“Since Rusk's party,” I said.

Shawna turned on the stool. “You understood that very quickly.”

“Yes,” I said.

“The first night at the Pearl,” Shawna said.

“I was with him,” I said.

“And last night,” Shawna said.

“Yes,” I said.

She moved to the edge of the bed. For a while her face barely changed. Then she laughed once, without amusement.

“You brought his shirt here,” Shawna said. “I washed it.”

I said I knew. She looked at me sharply.

“Stop presenting awareness as a favor,” Shawna said. “I'm not thanking you for knowing where you put the laundry.”

I leaned against the door because standing in the middle of the room felt like asking her to make space for me. “I'm sorry.”

“For which part?” Shawna asked.

“Sleeping with him. Lying. Letting you believe I was somewhere else,” I said.

“You made Trinh answer questions,” Shawna said.

“I didn't ask her to lie,” I said.

“You made her choose between correcting her employer and confusing his wife. A marvelous improvement,” Shawna said.

I stopped trying to find a smaller description. Shawna stood and went to the window. Garden lights shone beneath us; the open piano-room door reflected in the glass.

“Who knows?” Shawna asked. “Saint?”

“Some,” I said.

“Your parents?” Shawna asked.

“No,” I said.

“Did Irene arrange this?” Shawna asked.

“No,” I said.

“Did he?” Shawna asked.

“I kissed him first,” I said. “I wanted him. We both chose it.”

Shawna turned back. “There you are. A sentence with you doing something.”

Her jaw tightened. She looked away, swallowed, and asked whether I wanted to remain married to her.

“Yes,” I said.

“Then what did you expect the marriage to do while you weren't using it?” Shawna asked.

“I wasn't thinking properly,” I said.

“You thought enough to name a room,” Shawna said. “You thought about who'd be there, what I'd ask, which name would sound ordinary. Don't suddenly become stupid because I'm asking you to explain.”

I looked down, then made myself look back. “You're right.”

“I know,” Shawna said. “It hasn't improved the evening.”

She took a tissue and pressed it beneath one eye. Her makeup had lasted through the entire preview. A dark mark spread into the paper. I wanted to cross the room and hold her. I stayed by the door.

“Was there something you wanted to tell me before him?” Shawna asked. “About men. About yourself. A conversation I somehow missed while sleeping beside you.”

“I hadn't had it with everyone else,” I said.

“Fine. Smaller guest list,” Shawna said. “Answer.”

I took a breath. “I didn't expect to want him. Then I did. I still wanted you. I still do.”

She shut her eyes for a moment. “You understand that doesn't compliment me.”

“Yes,” I said.

“I wasn't competing for whatever attention survived your surprise,” Shawna said.

“I didn't think of it that way,” I said.

“You were getting all the good parts,” Shawna said. She put the tissue down with unnecessary care.

The house had grown quiet. I heard Valerie close a downstairs door and walk toward the service hall. Our bedroom was the same size it had been that morning. Nothing in it offered a place where this conversation would be easier.

Shawna asked whether we'd talked about her.

“Yes,” I said.

“Was I cold? Too busy? In love with work? Which convenient wife did you give him?” Shawna asked.

“None. I said you're good to me. That I love you. That you're talented and funny and stubborn,” I said.

Shawna watched my face. “Then he knew.”

“He knew I was married,” I said.

Her eyes went to my ring. “Subtle clue. Did you remove it?”

“Not to pretend I wasn't,” I said.

“That answer has a lot of architecture,” Shawna said.

I stopped. “I took it off to wash my hands. Left it on the table. Put it back afterward.”

“Congratulations on finishing a laundry cycle,” Shawna said.

The joke sounded exactly like her. I hadn't lost the woman who could make me laugh. I'd given her reasons to turn the same precision on me.

“I don't want to lose you,” I said.

She looked at me for a long time. “That thought should have gone to the apartment with you.”

There was nothing I could put beside that. Shawna went to the wardrobe and took out a clean shirt, trousers, and the small bag I used for short trips. My shoulders straightened before I could stop them.

“You want me to leave?” I asked.

“This room, tonight,” Shawna said. “Guest room. I need to work tomorrow without you following me through the house explaining your latest discovery.”

“All right,” I said.

Shawna set the bag on the chair. “And you don't buy something to fix it.”

I started to say I wouldn't. She pointed at the wall beyond which the piano occupied most of a room. I closed my mouth.

“No gifts,” I said.

“No dramatic announcements. No new men outside my door because you've decided feeling guilty is a security assignment. The existing security stays because somebody attacked my collection. Your guilt doesn't get a uniform,” Shawna said.

“I understand,” I told her.

“You'll have time to demonstrate that,” Shawna said.

She handed me the bag. I held it between us.

“The show was beautiful,” I said.

## Chapter 23: The Man in the Wrong Lane

## Chapter 25: A Million Dollars and a Toaster

“Ask Hòa,” Tín said.

Hòa opened the drawer and inspected Mom's spoons as though their workmanship might explain every outstanding question. I looked down the stairs again and learned nothing new.

The movers returned. Their leader stopped in the doorway, took in the piano, and looked at Dad. Dad shook his head once. The man looked at Tín. Tín lifted his cup. Hòa gave him the folded blanket and thanked him.

After a long moment the mover handed me the delivery sheet. I took one of Mrs. Farrow's pens out of my pocket.

“Finally,” I said. “Something I can explain.”

By evening the flat contained my family, most of the crew, and more food than its kitchen had surfaces. Denise delivered a tray before going to dinner with Shawna and Marisol. She kissed Mom, inspected my healing face, and told me I looked cheaper to repair.

Loan brought a good kitchen knife. Saint reached for its wrapping with his phone ready; Loan told him he'd lose a finger if he opened it for a video. He placed both objects down with unusual care.

Hậu brought the old Bell Street Fight Club sign, then admitted Mara had only permitted a loan. Voss wanted it back when he returned from Fresno. Three wins, Hậu reported. Voss had developed opinions about standards.

Rafa put a framed Argent photograph on my study desk. Tess had signed it under DRIVER INCLUDED NO MIRACLES REQUIRED.

“For you,” Rafa said.

“Recognized the affection,” I told him.

Louis arrived on a crutch with Eddie and Nessa. His thigh had healed enough to let him reject every offered chair. Felix came behind with the replacement folding one and gave Louis first use. That suited him. Nessa sat nearby with Celeste's bottle, finally opened, and laughed when Felix explained the chair's contractual position.

Irene came late. She examined the crowded rooms, placed a small silver bell on the kitchen counter, and told me it was for visitors who needed to leave. Saint touched it. Mom caught his wrist before it rang. He withdrew respectfully.

Khải answered laughing at something in his own room.

“Inspector?” I asked.

“Model shopping center,” Khải said. “Blocking three fire exits.”

“Wednesday. Seven. Dinner,” I told him.

He asked where. I named a small restaurant near the river, tables close enough that nobody could pretend they hadn't seen us.

“I like it,” Khải said.

“So do I,” I said.

“Wednesday,” Khải confirmed.

We ended without inventing a second destination. I stayed by the window a moment. Somebody had moved the welcome banner so it no longer hid Mrs. Farrow's shop sign. My parking structure leaked somewhere to the west with my name on it. The Ducal stood beyond the ridge. The toaster waited in my kitchen beside Mom's six spoons.

Dad called me back. I put away the phone, went to him, and bowed. He rested a hand briefly at the back of my neck, then let me straighten.

“Eat,” Dad said.

“Again?” I asked.

Tín tapped his empty plate. “Listen to this rich nigga. First day in his own place and he's rationing rice.”

Mom passed the pot. Hòa held out Tín's plate, already prepared for the larger portion he'd demand. I filled it.

Saint found the next piano note. It was wrong.

Tín pointed his spoon across the room. “Twenty thousand dollars. Play one I recognize.”

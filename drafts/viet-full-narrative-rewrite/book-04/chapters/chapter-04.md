## Chapter 4: A Piano in the Pot

On Thursday I signed away the possible future of a building, ate dinner, and found a piano standing between me and the consequences.

It occupied Aster House's gallery on a wheeled platform. Two tuners circled it anxiously while a woman explained that a European prince had blessed its wood. Trinh asked which prince. The woman smiled as though geography had become intrusive.

“Good,” Trinh said. “A thoroughly documented blessing.”

The piano was black, immensely long, and inlaid with little stars. A gold E shone on its side. Saint hovered one finger over it until I told him touching it would enroll him in lessons.

“I could learn,” Saint said.

“Begin with a rest,” I suggested.

We'd spent the afternoon over Irene's wager papers. The Pearl's building and my interest in its games could enter the pot. Its operating company, Trinh's share, and the people's employment could not. Trinh made three changes. Irene accepted two, contested the third, and finally agreed when Trinh began putting her pen away. The Ducal title already bore Thành's signature. There were nine stakes, nine crews, and now a very expensive reason for me to show up.

“He wins a building,” Trinh had told Irene across the small office table. “He does not win authority to replace my managers before the transfer terms are settled.”

“Nobody is promising that,” Irene had replied.

Trinh turned the page toward her. “Then you won't mind removing the sentence that does.”

I'd leaned over to read the clause. Irene had looked at me, then at Trinh's capped pen. Outside the office Saint had been asking a waiter whether piano lessons could count as cultural sponsorship. The world kept offering me distracting things while the person beside me protected the room we'd actually built.

“Take it out,” I'd told Irene.

“I might have accepted a revision,” Irene had said.

Trinh had uncapped her pen. “You're about to.”

Now at the card table she checked the sealed copy once more before putting it in her bag. I caught her eye and nodded. She nodded back, then moved Saint far enough from the stakes that his sleeves could no longer make promises for him.

The preliminary card game settled starting positions and allowed side bets. Apparently none of us could digest dinner unless something we liked might leave with a stranger.

Khải arrived carrying a small wooden box. In it lay a plain watch with a worn brown strap. He'd bought it after his first serious commission. He held the box a moment before handing it to Irene, who accepted it more carefully than the briefcase of cash she'd just taken from another guest.

“You're willing to lose that?” I asked Khải.

“No,” Khải said. “That is the unpleasant distinction between a stake and something you leave beside a bin.”

The piano's owner introduced himself as Ellis Crowe. He wore a pearl tiepin, had an orange face shaped like a sturdy suitcase, and shook hands without letting his other hand leave his drink. Petra, his thin partner in a velvet jacket, watched the pianist test the keys with the expression of someone wondering if she could escape inside the instrument.

“The young king,” Crowe said to me. “Do you play?”

“We'll find out when it lands,” I said.

Petra coughed into a laugh. Crowe looked at her and missed Thành entering.

Other people didn't. Conversations shortened as Thành crossed the gallery in a brown suit, white scar above his brow. Long followed, taller than Hậu and built wide enough to make the doorway seem like a clerical error. One flattened ear lay scarred against his head. The ring on his little finger looked small until it passed beside the piano keys.

Thành offered his hand. I gave him enough resistance to discourage a measurement.

“I enjoyed the Pearl,” Thành said.

“You're welcome back. Reserve a table,” I said.

“I prefer keys,” Thành said.

“Then imagine how much I'll enjoy yours,” I told Thành.

Irene rang a bell before either of us improved the remark. At the round table, partners stood behind players. Khải took his place behind me. Trinh and Saint stayed along the wall, where Trinh could supervise Saint's promises without interrupting play.

The first hand cost me fifteen thousand dollars. Crowe lost twice that on the second and ordered another drink loudly enough to imply recovery. Thành folded early. His eyes spent more time on the partners than on the cards.

Khải leaned near my ear. “Crowe gets reckless when Petra looks bored.”

“She's been bored since she got here,” I murmured.

“A serious condition,” Khải said.

I lost once more, won twice, and began to hear the room. A bracelet rubbing against the table. Crowe's breath before he enlarged a bet. Petra's glass arriving between his fingers and another refill. The dealer left our mistakes room to develop.

When Khải laughed at something I said, Thành looked at his mouth. When I turned to enjoy it, Thành watched mine. I stayed turned another second because I disliked being examined and liked the object of the examination.

“Anything I should share with the table?” I asked Thành.

“Irene finds compatible pairs,” Thành replied.

Khải rested a hand on my chair. “She finds expensive ones. Compatibility invoices separately.”

Just after midnight Crowe's drink had become water, his jacket had come open, and Petra had stopped pretending interest in the room. He pushed out a stack. I matched it. Thành remained in, though he'd barely glanced at his cards.

Then Crowe added the piano.

I looked past him at all those stars. One tuner closed his eyes.

“It won't fit in my car,” I told Crowe.

“That's your opportunity to fold,” Crowe said.

Khải's watch lay among the side stakes near the dealer. His fingertips tightened against my chair, then relaxed when he saw me look. I had a strong hand. Crowe thought he had the strongest. Thành appeared more interested in watching a young man become stupid than in the pot itself.

I raised. Crowe swallowed. Petra closed her eyes. Thành smiled and folded.

Crowe laid his cards down with a flourish that sent one sliding into the dealer's cuff. The dealer retrieved it. I set down mine.

Saint's yell struck the gallery ceiling before the applause. Khải put both hands on my shoulders and laughed. His thumbs pressed through my jacket. Looking up at him, I lost the room for a moment, which was an extraordinary way to behave after winning most of its contents.

“You keep the watch,” I told Khải.

“You keep the structural problem,” Khải replied.

Crowe rose so fast his chair struck Petra's knee. “Again.”

Petra caught his sleeve. “Ellis. Enough.”

Crowe repeated himself, louder. Thành set down his glass. Long moved.

Three steps took him across the gallery. He caught Crowe's collar, lifted him until his shoes brushed the chair seat, and turned him toward the piano. Petra stepped forward and stopped when Long looked down at her.

I stood. “That's mine now.”

Long lowered Crowe onto the piano bench hard enough to punch its legs into the floor. The bench cracked. Crowe's mouth hung open over a breath he couldn't get back.

“Then he shouldn't damage it,” Long said.

Long put a hand beneath the piano's front corner and lifted. Its wheels left the platform. The long body tilted over Crowe's knees. A tuner reached for the falling music stand instead of the person, discovered what he'd chosen, and retreated in horror with both hands around a page of scales.

“Apologize,” Long told Crowe.

Crowe apologized to me, Irene, Petra, the people standing nearest, and somebody he'd evidently offended in childhood. Thành watched over the rim of his glass.

I took the opposite corner underneath. The weight came into my arm. My stitches pulled. I lifted until the piano leveled, removing its threat from Crowe's legs without taking my eyes off Long.

“Put down my piano,” I said.

Long's jaw moved. Between us the instrument trembled slightly; one open string hummed inside it. Crowe stopped apologizing long enough to slide off the bench on his backside.

Thành said Long's name.

Long lowered his end. I lowered mine. The wheels touched without a bang. The tuners inhaled together. Petra pulled Crowe up by his sleeve and walked him out before he remembered his pride.

Irene waited for the door to close. “How fortunate that nobody has mistaken this for an acceptable way to tune.”

Thành apologized for his man's enthusiasm about manners. Irene suggested furniture lessons. He took the envelope containing his starting position and paused beside me on the way out.

“Briar on Saturday,” Thành said. “Find a driver.”

“I have several,” I said.

Thành glanced at the piano. “One who can steer.”

Long followed him without looking back. The room began speaking again by degrees. Khải returned wearing his watch and turned my palm upward. A red line had spread through the dressing. He folded a napkin and held it there.

“Attention-seeking injury,” Khải said.

“It knows its audience,” I told him.

His hand stayed over mine until Trinh arrived. She noticed him withdrawing it, because Trinh noticed everything except appeals to mind her own business.

“Third,” Trinh said, tapping our envelope. “Out of nine.”

Saint joined us with the pianist's card and news that he'd arranged lessons for the household. I reminded him he didn't live in my household. Saint called this an inclusive ambition.

On the way out Khải stopped beside last year's racing photograph. A man in a white jacket held a silver cup above his head and laughed beside Irene.

“Oscar,” Khải said.

“A friend?” I asked.

Khải nodded. When I asked where he was, Khải kept looking at the picture. “That's what Irene would like somebody to answer.”

Outside, Saint negotiated piano delivery while Trinh took a call. Khải stood beside me beneath the entrance lamp.

“Two things,” Khải said. “I'm a better architect than gambler. And I'm gay.”

“All right,” I said.

“You keep looking as though you want to ask something.” Khải glanced at my wedding ring. “Think about the answer first.”

He touched my arm and went down to his car. I watched until Saint thrust my phone at me. Shawna had heard from Valerie.

“A piano?” Shawna asked.

“Stars all over it,” I said.

“The sky has stars, Aiden. We've shown admirable restraint about bringing that into the breakfast room,” Shawna said.

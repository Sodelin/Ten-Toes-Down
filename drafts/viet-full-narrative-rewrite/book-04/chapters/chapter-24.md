## Chapter 24: The Last Lap Is on Foot

The north stand grew no closer for the first hundred steps.

My ankle swelled inside its shoe. Every few yards I shortened the stride before the pain reached my knee. My left arm hung against my ribs; the fingers moved, but nothing above them would accept instructions. Thành disappeared into the tunnel with his men while I was still crossing the service road.

A young cleaner crouched behind stacked cones with an older kitchen worker. Both watched me approach. She pressed her hands over her ears.

“East court,” I told them.

The man pointed at smoke covering the road. He was right. Wind had carried it across their route. South remained clearer, along the inner fence toward Hậu's opening and the red transporter.

I pointed. The cleaner lowered one hand.

“Still shooting?” the cleaner asked.

“Less,” I said, and heard how little use that was.

I called Saint, gave their position, and stayed until I saw him running along the fence with two of June's people. His jacket had one sleeve. He still held the microphone, though the situation had stopped accepting its authority.

He reached us, looked at my face, and stopped. “Jesus, Aiden.”

“Take them,” I told him.

Saint glanced at the tunnel. “You need somebody.”

“I need the north road clear when I come out,” I said.

He had arguments ready. Then the cleaner gripped his remaining sleeve. Saint put the microphone in his pocket, took her hand, and nodded to me.

“I'll tell Tess,” Saint said.

I entered the tunnel. Old oil lay beneath fresh smoke. Caged lamps lit a sloping passage toward the Ducal. Openings on the left led to former horse stalls, now storage and workshops. One sign promised AUTHENTIC MOTOR HERITAGE above a leaking pipe and plastic bucket. The bucket, at least, was doing its job.

Thành's voice carried from ahead, ordering Coulter to get the car.

“Your tanker blocks the exit,” Coulter said. “I don't have the driver.”

At the first junction I saw them twenty yards away. One guard watched down the passage. The other tried a locked side door. Thành stood under a strip light facing Coulter.

“You said contained,” Coulter told him.

Thành struck him across the mouth. Coulter staggered against the wall, looked at the blood on his hand, and changed his face completely.

“I'm done,” Coulter said.

He turned toward the passage I occupied. Thành drew his pistol and shot him in the back.

The shot hit every hard surface at once. Coulter fell forward with one hand reaching for something that wasn't there. He struck the floor and didn't move. The nearer guard stared at him. Thành pointed the pistol toward the locked door and repeated the order to open it.

I moved behind a wheeled platform piled with old trophy boxes. The watching guard saw me and raised his gun. I shoved the platform ahead and followed. Bullets went through cardboard, sending brass cars and miniature cups spinning across the floor. The trolley struck his legs. He fell over its far end.

I caught the second guard as he turned from the lock. My right hand closed on his gun wrist. I drove it into the wall, heard metal strike stone, and pulled him between me and Thành.

Thành fired anyway.

The guard jerked. His weight dropped against me. I let him slide down and threw myself into the nearest workshop as another round struck the jamb. My injured shoulder hit the floor. For a moment I couldn't breathe through the pain.

Footsteps passed the doorway. Thành ran farther up the tunnel. Outside, the first guard pushed the trophy platform off his legs. I heard its wheels, got onto my knees, and took a short metal component from an open cabinet drawer.

When his pistol appeared around the doorframe, I threw the component. It struck his hand. He dropped the gun. I reached him before he recovered, caught his coat, and drove his face into the platform's side. He went slack. I kicked the weapon under a heavy cabinet.

The guard Thành had shot lay against the wall holding a wound below his collarbone. His eyes followed me. Coulter remained facedown beyond him.

I called Trinh. “North service tunnel. First workshop. Two wounded guards. Coulter farther up.”

“Police at the east gate,” Trinh said. “Aiden, stop moving.”

“Medics through the lower entrance. Irene knows it,” I said.

Trinh repeated my name. I said soon and put the phone away before she could describe the word accurately.

At the next junction Thành fired from a doorway to the right. I dropped behind a low stone divider. The bullet broke a lamp above me. Glass fell through my hair.

“Still shopping?” Thành called.

Dust held fresh tracks into the restoration workshop. Khải's plans had shown two exits beyond it, toward restaurant or hotel service level. I remembered the shape more clearly than I could feel my left hand.

“Service is disappointing,” I called back.

He fired into the divider. Chips struck my neck. I crawled along its base into an open stall holding tire racks. A narrow connecting door led from there behind his position.

I pushed the first rack. Its rusty wheel screamed. Thành heard. By the time I entered the restoration room he had turned toward the noise.

A stripped racing car stood on a wheeled service frame beside a long workbench. Thành raised the pistol between them. I kicked the tire rack into his knees. His shot went high and struck the car's underside. He fell against the bench but kept the gun.

I hit his wrist with the rack upright. The pistol clattered underneath. He struck my throat with his free hand, quick and practiced, then drove a knee into the ribs Long had already spent the evening breaking. My right leg folded. He took the back of my head and slammed my face into the bench edge.

Blood poured from my nose. A tooth struck the wood and stayed there. Thành stepped back, breathing harder now, and looked at me.

“Long did most of it,” Thành said.

“Should've invoiced,” I told him.

I caught his jacket and pulled him toward my forehead. We collided brow to brow; his old scar split above the eye. I drove my knee between his legs and struck the side of his throat with the edge of my good hand. He made a small strangled sound and folded against the bench.

I straightened enough to get air. His hand found a heavy tool among the fallen things. He swung it into my left upper arm.

Pain put me on one knee. The fingers went dead again. He raised the tool toward my head. I caught his wrist with my right hand and forced it sideways. Metal struck the service frame with a ringing blow.

We shoved against each other beneath the stripped car. His shoes gripped dry concrete. Mine slipped in blood I'd carried in. He had two working arms and a chest that expanded without an argument. He used both advantages.

He drove me back under the chassis overhang, seized the injured arm, and twisted. I heard myself shout. Thành smiled.

I stopped trying to lift the arm. I stepped closer, hooked my right foot behind his ankle, and drove my head into his face again. His grip broke. We struck the service frame together; its locked wheels skidded across the floor. The car shifted in its cradle.

Thành looked up. I kicked the frame away from the bench. It rolled into his legs and pinned him against the wood. He pushed with both hands. I set my right shoulder against the crossbar and drove forward.

The workbench groaned. Tools fell over his shoes. He hit my ear. I kept pushing. He hit me again; the room tilted, and I widened my stance until the floor stopped moving beneath it.

The bench tore from the wall. Thành fell backward into the gap. The frame rolled onto fallen tools, stopped, and tipped. The car lurched toward me.

I ducked underneath its side and wrapped my right arm around a crossbar. For one sick moment I held the whole tilted arrangement with my back against the doorway. The wheels spun beside my knee. My shoulder pressed into the frame. A strip of bodywork scraped down the jamb by my face.

Thành crawled out behind the broken bench. The pistol was in his hand.

He stood, blood across his eye, and aimed at my chest. “Let go.”

I looked at his feet, the wrecked bench behind him, and the car balanced between us. I put my right foot on the low crosspiece. His finger tightened.

I straightened my leg and threw my weight forward.

The frame tipped away. The stripped car fell across the bench in a crash of metal and splintering timber. Thành fired into the ceiling as the chassis struck him and drove him against the stone wall. A lamp cage tore loose and fell among the tools.

I lay inside the doorway. A little wheel on the frame kept turning. The gun slid from Thành's hand. His head rested at an angle no living man would have chosen. I watched his chest while the wheel slowed and stopped.

Then the power failed.

In the dark I stayed still for three breaths. Water dripped beyond the wall. Smoke entered from the upper passage, bringing the sharp smell of hot plastic over the oil. My phone lit when I touched it. Missed calls from Tess and Khải. A message from Trinh containing only my name.

I called Tess and gave the workshop location.

“Can you reach the road?” Tess asked.

I tested the ankle. “Slowly.”

“Slowly,” Tess agreed. “I'm here.”

I used the frame to stand. My left arm went inside my torn jacket against my ribs. A broken bench leg made a bad cane; I took it anyway and limped toward the main passage.

Flashlights came toward me. Medics, with Irene behind them in a soot-streaked red jacket. She saw my face and stopped halfway through a sentence.

“Thành?” Irene asked.

I pointed behind me. She went into the workshop without another word.

A medic reached for me. I showed him the two guards and Coulter. He called his partners; they divided among the fallen men. A third medic stayed with me, took my bad cane, and put my good arm across her shoulders. She walked at a pace I could manage without making it a debate.

Outside, Tess had brought an ordinary car with the rear door open. Khải waited beside it holding a towel. He crossed to me and stopped just short of grabbing all the damaged pieces at once.

“Terrible,” Khải said, looking at my face.

“Won the argument,” I told him.

He pressed the towel to my hand. “Sit before you start another.”

Beyond him emergency vehicles filled the eastern court. Dad stood among cooks. Mom had a blanket and was arguing with an officer about interrupting assistance to provide her name. Tín still wore both medals. Hòa held his cushion.

All standing. I closed my eyes for one second and allowed that fact to reach me.

Irene returned. The prizes were safe. Louis was at the clinic; the bullet had missed the important things. Coulter was dead. The other guards were being treated. The bathhouse survivors and kitchen staff were out. Our wounded had help.

Irene took the bent crown badge from my pocket and set it on the seat beside me. “Circuit stage suspended. Thành disqualified. You still owe me a race.”

Tess looked at her. “Let him grow skin.”

“Suspended,” Irene repeated. “I can read a face.”

The medic fastened a support around my arm. Pain made me grip the seat. Khải put his hand over mine, avoiding the cut. I held on until the support was secure.

“Clinic,” I told Tess.

“After?” Tess asked.

Beyond the city lights lay Orchard House. I couldn't distinguish it, but I knew the direction. I looked away from it.

“Trinh has a room at the Pearl,” I said.

Khải squeezed my fingers and let go so the medic could close the door. As we pulled away Tín called after the car.

“Keep receipts! His hotel's maintenance is terrible!” Tín said.

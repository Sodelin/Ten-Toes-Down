## Chapter 20: The King's Weekend

Dennis had kept our sealed route card in a sandwich bag since the train.

He presented it behind Aster House on Friday evening, dry and unbent. I asked why it needed the bag. Dennis explained that it had been raining when I handed it over. I reminded him that had been some time ago.

“Still dry,” Dennis said.

Trinh looked at me. “That's why he works for me.”

Nine cars waited in the forecourt beneath strings of lights. Irene had moved the ordinary party here: food stalls, musicians, guests delighted by the revised invitation, and security sufficient to make them feel important without explaining the alternative. Beyond the railings people photographed the cars.

Saint stood beside the Argent wearing KING'S PROBLEMS and promising his phone that tonight I'd acquire something with its own lobby.

“Door,” Tess told him.

“History—” Saint began.

“Has a handle,” Tess said.

He opened my passenger door. The Argent gleamed under the lamps. Rafa had repaired the fountain damage while leaving its bullet scar visible. A white ribbon fluttered on the bronze radiator bird.

“Mara,” Rafa explained. “If Hậu needs a sling, the car can dress appropriately.”

At the support van Mara made Hậu demonstrate how high he could lift his left arm. He lied about the pain. His face contradicted him. She replaced the sling, and he looked to me for solidarity. I adjusted the ribbon on the car instead.

Long stood beside Thành's low black racer with its red wheels. Thành watched from Irene's terrace, posing for photographs with people who believed his ambitions stopped at their property.

Irene rang a brass bell. Three city stations, she announced. Cards supplied the first destination. Complete the stations in order, bring three tokens to Briar, and entry order determined the circuit positions.

Crowe raised a hand. “Impediment still allowed?”

“Inside the contest grounds,” Irene said. “Public streets remain public. Hurt a spectator and I will personally damage your evening beyond recognition.”

“Vehicles?” Crowe asked.

Tess leaned out of the Argent. “Buy a faster one.”

The bell rang again. I broke the seal. Regent Exchange. East window. Take a step up. Beneath the words was a boot.

Saint leaned over it. “Fashion.”

“Your expertise,” I told him. “At last.”

Crowe's pink coupe went first. Celeste's blue rally car followed, Bree at its wheel. We left third through a corridor of phones and cheers. A boy at the rail threw a flower. I caught it. He wanted me to win because his mother worked at the Ducal and the manager charged her for parking.

I asked her name. He told me. I tucked the flower by the dashboard.

Tess looked at it. “Collecting commitments at speed.”

“We could slow down,” I said.

She gave me one appalled glance and drove.

The Regent Exchange had once sold furniture to people who called sofas investments. Tonight its empty ground floor held Run displays. We entered a closed delivery court, where Crowe's coupe had stopped crookedly by the loading door. Petra argued through its window. Bree parked neatly beside him and took out a short crowbar.

“Dressed for work,” Tess observed.

In the east display window stood a silver boot taller than me. It revolved above artificial clouds with a small crown on its toe. An attendant held a sign asking us to respect the exhibit. He said the token was inside and pointed toward a ladder leading to an overhead walkway.

Crowe's man was already climbing. Bree circled the platform instead. Tess touched my arm and nodded toward her.

I ran six rungs up the ladder, stepped onto its side, and jumped for the beam above. My shoulder objected. I swung once, let go, and landed against the boot's calf. The display boomed. Its upper half leaned toward the glass. The attendant lowered his sign and said sir with the despair of a man witnessing his deposit leave.

I hooked the top rim and reached inside. A little brass shoe hung on a cord, second destination card tucked underneath. I pulled it out as Bree landed on the platform beside me.

“Could've waited,” Bree said.

“Could've stayed home,” I said.

Bree grinned and spun the platform hard by its rail. The boot turned under my foot. I slipped, caught the rim again, and swung across the front window with my legs over the clouds. Outside people cheered. Saint pressed both hands against the glass.

“Wave, nigga!” Saint shouted.

“Loading door!” I yelled.

“Both can happen!” Saint said.

Bree opened a compartment near the heel and took her own token. There were nine. She'd read the diagram while I was interpreting the poetry. I dropped beside her, rolled through the artificial clouds, and stood wearing weather from my shoulders down.

Tess waited at the door. “Lose the forecast.”

I threw away stuffing and ran. Crowe reversed into pallets while Celeste's blue car shot through the gate. We followed second. Tess smiled.

“You're enjoying losing,” I said.

“Somebody finally made it worth driving,” Tess replied.

The second card led to a closed open-air cinema at the industrial edge of Bell Street. Nine silver batons hung beneath its giant screen. A narrow spiral access ramp climbed behind it. Bree reached the bottom first and began running up. Thành's black racer arrived behind us. Its driver stayed at the wheel while two Meridian men climbed out with lengths of chain.

I glanced at the rules board. “Theatrical.”

Tess shut off our engine. “Go. I'll watch the art.”

The first man swung his chain across the ramp entrance. I caught the loose end against the handrail and stamped the hanging length. His pull brought him toward me. I drove my shoulder into his chest, carried him into the screen support, and took the chain when his breath left.

The second came down from above and swung at my head. I ducked. Steel struck the rail, throwing sparks. I stepped inside his arms, caught his belt, and lifted him over the inner barrier. His feet pedaled over the ramp's open center.

“Don't,” the chain man said.

“Sit,” I told him.

I set him on a narrow ledge against the support column. He clung to it. I removed his chain; he showed no desire to retain company. Above us Bree reached the batons.

Then the screen shook.

The black racer had driven into the ramp's base. Its engine roared as it pushed. An outer support folded. The ramp dropped at one end and tore its upper bracket from the screen frame. Bree fell onto her stomach; one leg slid through the widening gap beneath the rail.

I ran upward. Boards shifted under my shoes. I caught the back of Bree's belt just as the section beneath her fell away. She swung against my thigh. My feet slid down the incline toward the open edge.

Below, Tess drove the Argent between the black racer and the support. Its long flank shoved the black car sideways. The other driver braked late; his front wheel climbed a concrete stop. The car hung on its underside, wheels scrabbling against nothing useful.

The ramp steadied for half a breath. I hauled Bree onto the screen frame and asked whether she could climb. She nodded, eyes wide. I sent her toward the intact maintenance ladder, grabbed two batons, and tucked hers into the back of her jacket.

The lower ramp began peeling away. I looped the captured chain over a beam, took both ends, and jumped clear. The falling structure swept beneath my feet. I swung into the screen frame knees-first and hung there while the ramp hit the empty ground in a crash that drowned the audience.

For a moment I could only breathe through my teeth. The chain cut into my hand. Below me Tess leaned over the wheel and looked up through the windshield.

“Coming?” Tess called.

“View's good,” I said.

“Better inside,” Tess answered.

I climbed down. Attendants were already moving toward the ledge man and the stranded racer. Bree reached the ground just ahead of me, pulled out her baton, and held out a hand.

“Thank you,” Bree said.

I shook it. “Still racing?”

She looked toward Celeste, who had both hands on the black car's window frame and was explaining something its driver didn't enjoy. “Harder,” Bree said.

We left together. Tess took the next corner so smoothly that my ribs received no new information. I thanked her for kindness. She warned me that the following corner would be awful. It was.

The final station occupied a fenced freight yard beside the harbor. On a short track loop, a little industrial engine painted like a dragon pulled a railway flatcar. Nine glass cases rode the flatcar, each holding a red racing glove.

Saint arrived in the support van and stared at the dragon. “Hale's going to propose.”

Dockworkers watched through the fence. One shouted my name, raised both hands, and nearly dropped his sandwich. His sister had got the Pearl job. I called congratulations. He threw me sealed water. I caught it, drank, and passed it to Tess.

The flatcar moved past its boarding platform. June's driver reached its rear ladder first. Celeste's teammate came aboard from the front. Crowe's crew chased it on foot, arguing about the speed of dragons.

I watched the wheels and couplings, counted the gap, and waited until the corner passed a floodlight pole. The machine could have listed every permitted move here. It couldn't persuade my knee to stop throbbing. I flexed it, ran, and jumped from the Argent's running board to a concrete block, then across to the moving car.

My right foot landed. The left missed. June's driver caught my sleeve as I caught the rail. For a moment we stared at each other over the gap.

“Excellent entrance,” June's driver said.

“Expensive shoes,” I explained.

She pulled. I got both feet aboard and thanked her properly before turning to the cases.

Our brass shoe fit our lock. Inside the red glove's cuff was the final stamp. Across from me Crowe's man shook his entire case because he'd left the shoe in the car.

Petra shouted from the platform to try hers.

“Not ours!” Crowe's man called.

“Ellis said that about three things in our house,” Petra shouted. “Try!”

I laughed while jumping down. This landing hurt less. Tess had the door open before I reached it.

We had all three tokens. The road west rose beyond the freight line toward Briar and the Ducal's white terraces. Other crews followed in a ragged string, their engines loud enough to carry every new personal grudge.

Trinh called as we reached the circuit gate. Irene had watched the sealed prize cases load into the Crown Wagon. Louis was inside. Eddie drove. Nessa had the service route ready. Aster House was full, and the last ordinary guest coach was leaving Briar now.

The coach rolled past us carrying laughing people toward music instead of the planned enclosure. A girl pressed her face against its window when she saw the Argent. I raised the red glove. She waved with both hands.

The coach cleared the gate. Behind it a guard began hauling heavy steel across the return lane.

Tess saw it. “Earlier.”

I put the tokens on the dashboard, opened my jacket to let my ribs expand, and reached for the phone.

“Then we move earlier,” I told her.

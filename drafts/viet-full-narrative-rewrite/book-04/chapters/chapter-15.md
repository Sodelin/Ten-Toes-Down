## Chapter 15: Take the Side Entrance

Irene sent a photograph of white shoes beside a key ring on blue tile. Her message contained four words: Take the side entrance.

I called. No answer. On the second attempt a man answered and said she was occupied.

“Then unoccupy her,” I said.

He recognized my voice with a satisfaction I disliked. René Duval, he called himself. Irene had come to the Mariner Rooms to discuss a misunderstanding. It wasn't understood yet.

Baths. The man Mick had described. I looked at the yellow-painted key in the photograph and told him I was coming. I hung up while he began explaining why that might be inconvenient.

Khải waited outside the Pearl when I came down. He'd received the same photograph. His hands were in his pockets, which didn't quite conceal how tightly he held them.

“East entrance,” Khải told me. “Private clients. I measured the place before refusing Duval's renovation.”

“What did he want?” I asked.

“A room with no visible exit,” Khải said.

“Confident host,” I said.

“Poor brief,” Khải replied.

Tess brought a dark wagon with Rafa beside her. Hậu argued over the phone that he still possessed an excellent right arm. Mara took it away from him and told me he would stay with the gym. Trinh assigned two of our men to follow in a clean car.

“Bring Irene back,” Trinh said beside my open door. “Don't inherit a bathhouse on the way.”

“Towels?” I asked.

Trinh waited. I met her eyes and promised to bring Irene back.

The Mariner Rooms occupied an old municipal pool near the docks, polished stone and bronze doors replacing every sign that ordinary people had once been welcome. The public entrance faced a little square. The east door opened into a narrow lane beside a restaurant kitchen. Tess stopped beyond the corner.

Rafa leaned forward. “If the building falls, encourage it away from the car.”

Khải and I entered through the side door. A man in a white robe over black trousers sat behind a small desk. I put Irene's photograph before him. His hand moved underneath the counter.

I lifted the desk.

It rose around his knees, bringing his hand into view with a little radio inside it. A bowl of wrapped peppermints slid toward me. I tilted the desk until it stopped.

“Radio down,” I told the attendant.

He obeyed. I lowered the desk carefully enough to preserve his shoes, because I needed him walking. “Show us Irene.”

He led us into warm eucalyptus air. Two men in towels looked up from reclining chairs. One recognized me and opened his mouth, then picked up water instead. At the next junction our guide pointed right. Khải touched my elbow and pointed left.

I looked at the attendant. He revised his route without requesting further feedback.

A guard waited beside frosted glass with a pistol low against his thigh. I pushed the attendant behind a pillar as the guard raised it, caught the wrist, and turned him into the wall. His forehead struck tile. I took the gun and passed it to Khải.

Another man emerged from the door with a wooden staff. He swung low at my knee. I stepped over it, trapped it beneath my foot, and pulled the first guard into him. Their heads met. The second man let go of the staff; I kept hold of the first until both had finished falling.

From inside Irene called, “You've taken the scenic route.”

She sat tied to a wooden chair beside a shallow pool. One half of her jacket was soaked. Duval stood behind her, short and shaved-headed, with a gold chain at his throat and the photographed white shoes. A knife rested near Irene's shoulder.

“No need for unpleasantness,” Duval said.

Irene looked up at him. “You tied me to patio furniture.”

“You entered private rooms,” Duval said.

“Where you keep my missing driver,” Irene answered.

The knife moved closer to her neck. I stopped three paces inside. Khải remained to my left with the captured gun lowered; Irene blocked any safe line to Duval. He saw that and stood more comfortably.

“Return the car,” Duval told me. “Withdraw. Everybody goes home.”

“Send an email next time,” Irene said.

“Quiet,” Duval said.

“Offer something worth listening to,” Irene replied.

A bronze pipe fed hot water into a waist-high basin to our right. I looked at it. Duval followed my eyes and tightened his grip on the chair.

I stepped toward the basin. He jerked Irene backward. She planted her heels and drove sideways instead, tipping the chair across his legs. His balance changed. The knife moved away from her neck.

I crossed the gap. Khải caught the chair before Irene's head hit tile. I caught Duval's knife arm and drove it against the bronze pipe. Metal bent. Hot water burst through a joint across his sleeve. He screamed and dropped the blade.

I hit his stomach and folded him face-down over a bench, arm behind his back. Khải cut Irene's restraints with the fallen knife. She sat upright on the floor, rubbing her wrists, and pointed at Duval's belt.

“Yellow key,” Irene said. “Seven. Basement.”

I removed the ring. Duval shook his head so violently his cheek squeaked on the wood. Irene stood, took the keys, and straightened her jacket.

Footsteps sounded beyond the passage. Several people running. I pushed Duval ahead of us by one shoulder. He found an impressive pace for a man who'd wanted everyone to remain comfortable.

The basement stairs were slick. A steel door blocked the bottom. Duval fumbled until Irene took the ring back and opened it herself.

“Hospitality has been slow today,” Irene told him.

Eight people occupied old changing cubicles beyond. Some lay on cots. A man with a bandage around his head sat with his back against the wall. A woman with cropped hair stood immediately, watching the keys.

“Who are you?” the woman asked.

“Leaving,” Irene said. “Would you like to?”

The woman saw Duval, reached for a broken piece of wood, and came forward. I moved him aside.

“Later,” I told her.

“Eleven days,” the cropped-haired woman said. “I've had later.”

“One minute more gets you outside with witnesses,” I said.

She looked from me to the open door, dropped the wood, and helped the bandaged man stand. Her name was Nessa Cruz, a mechanic. She'd accepted a job modifying equipment at Briar and discovered it included a locked bedroom. Irene recognized Eddie Chan and Louis Green, drivers from the earlier Run.

Khải opened the remaining cubicles. Two men could barely walk. I carried one under each arm. Nessa supported the bandaged man while Khải took another's weight. Irene kept Duval in front with his own knife pressed through the back of his robe.

We reached the stairs. Shots struck the wall above us. I backed down, keeping both men inside my arms. One began apologizing for his weight.

“You're not heavy enough to apologize,” I told him. “Save it for something interesting.”

Khải pointed down a lower passage. Laundry connected to the service court. He'd spent six weeks arguing about its drainage and sounded pleased to get something besides an invoice from the experience.

Steam filled the laundry. Machines lined one wall, wheeled baskets the other. Daylight showed beneath a broad loading door at the far end. I set down the injured men, tried its edge, and found it locked from outside.

I hooked my fingers beneath the bottom and pulled. The metal bowed toward me. Behind us the basement door opened. Khải and Nessa got everyone behind the machines; Irene dragged Duval with them.

I pulled again. The top track tore loose in a long metallic rip. I ducked beneath the bowed lower section and held it above my shoulders. Through the opening I saw bins, Tess's wagon, and Rafa waving with both arms like a man attempting to land a building.

The captives crawled through. Our following car waited behind Tess. Khải brought the two men I'd carried; Nessa guided the bandaged man, turned back, and freed a coat caught under the torn edge.

A bullet struck above my hand. The door rang down my arm. When Khải shouted clear, I shoved the steel upward and back. The remaining track gave way. The door folded into the laundry across the pursuing guards. One dropped his pistol and covered his head before the lower section hit him.

I climbed out. Irene still held Duval.

“We're taking eight people,” I said, looking at our cars.

“He can sit badly,” Irene replied.

He did, between our two men in the second car. We left as guards reached the service entrance. A shot starred Tess's rear window. It held. She looked at the mirror, then at Rafa.

“Bill him,” Tess said.

“Emotionally underway,” Rafa answered.

At Irene's clinic Mick tried to get out of bed when he saw Eddie. The two men held each other while the doctor waited. Nessa wouldn't lie down until she'd told us what she knew. We took an empty examination room. She borrowed a pen and drew Briar on the back of a paper pad.

“Northern stand,” Nessa said. “New gates. Inside handles removed. Seating over old machinery rooms. They called it a fire exercise.”

Khải leaned over her drawing and named the doors she marked. She nodded at each one. Tankers were scheduled to enter after the finalists reached the circuit. Rival owners would be in the stand. Families and guests would occupy the hospitality rooms above. Meridian's people had instructions to leave at the final bell.

“And the money?” Irene asked.

Nessa drew a box across the service tunnel. “Crown Wagon. Armored hospitality truck. Trophy, cash, stakes. It takes everything to the Ducal before the gates shut. He wanted the door changed because the old one jammed.”

“Couldn't risk trapping his money,” I said.

Nessa looked at me. “He wasn't as concerned about us.”

Irene stood. “We cancel.”

I looked at the drawing. “He moves the wagon, scatters his people, and tells every owner you invented a trap to keep the stakes.”

“They can argue alive,” Irene said.

“Show them first,” I replied. “Privately. Mick, Nessa, Eddie, Louis. Show the doors and the wagon. They choose whether to stay. Ordinary guests go elsewhere. We take his truck before he fills it and put our people inside.”

Khải stared at the marked tunnel. “Let him set it off with the exits already open.”

“And his prizes moving the wrong way,” I said.

Irene considered the drawing for a long time. “I decide which guests remain. Anyone who doesn't need to be there goes. The owners hear it before they choose.”

“Agreed,” I said.

Nessa tapped her box with the pen. “Truck transfers Thursday.”

Khải looked up at me. He was tired, angry, and still there. I asked Nessa to draw the depot next.

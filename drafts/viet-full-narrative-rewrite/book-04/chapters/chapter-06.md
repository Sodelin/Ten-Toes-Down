## Chapter 6: The Architect's Bad Idea

The cat in Khải's model swimming pool refused to move for either coffee or scale accuracy.

It occupied the reception desk at An Vân, its flank pressing an entire proposed palm tree into the shallow end. Khải set down the coffees beside it. The cat opened one eye, judged the development, and resumed.

“Your client?” I asked.

“Inspector,” Khải said. “We're awaiting approval.”

His studio occupied a second floor over a ceramics shop, with freight trains moving beyond the windows and drawings clipped to wires between the beams. Two young architects argued over a staircase at the back. One shook my hand normally. The other checked my face against his phone and tried to hide the result.

“He isn't our fee schedule,” Khải told him.

“Checking the time,” the young architect said.

Khải looked at my face. “Remarkably broad clock.”

In the smaller room behind reception, Briar covered a long table. Khải had designed the guest pavilion and press rooms in 2022, before Thành bought the place. He showed me the track, the service lane, the hotel, and old horse stalls beneath the northern stand.

“Storage now?” I asked.

“That's what the invoices said,” Khải replied. “I wasn't paid to open every door.”

He brought out a photograph of himself with Oscar, both wearing cheap paper crowns. Irene appeared behind them pointing angrily at somebody outside the frame. Oscar owned the Lantern before he'd disappeared. He liked cars he couldn't park and women who explained why.

The official account, Khải told me, was that Oscar had escaped his wreck and left town with money. Nobody had heard from him since.

“His daughter still has his dog,” Khải said. He put the photograph between us.

I looked at the laughing man and asked why Khải hadn't told me before I joined. Khải leaned against the table instead of defending the delay quickly.

“Irene thought he might return,” Khải said. “I thought she knew more than she could tell me. Then men brought guns into her house. You can withdraw, Aiden. I'll show you the building anyway.”

“I want the Ducal,” I said. “And I want the man who tried to kill Tess out of it.”

Khải looked down at the photograph. “Expensive view.”

Trinh had found the course-inspection clause. Irene had made Thành honor it. Our appointment was that afternoon. In Khải's silver car I had to move a small model district off the passenger seat before sitting down.

“Most people don't get to flatten housing on the way to work,” Khải said.

“I usually charge,” I told him.

During the drive he asked about the machine Benny kept warning people about. I explained the impossible part I'd proved at twenty and the usable version I'd built at twenty-one. It sat in a locked room over the Pearl now, because people had begun treating it like a customer-service department.

“Can it fix a printer?” Khải asked.

“Nobody can fix a printer,” I said.

Khải nodded solemnly. “So your genius has standards.”

At Briar the guard insisted I wear my visitor badge. I pointed at the enormous fresh patch on the loading platform I'd damaged. “They'll remember.”

Khải clipped the badge onto my jacket himself. I looked at his fingers longer than the job required.

Coulter, the site manager, met us with rolled sleeves and a radio he kept turning down. He answered questions about balcony loads and temporary seating readily enough. At the northern stand he stopped beside a fresh steel door in old brick and informed us that maintenance had closed the area.

Khải examined the hinges. “Maintenance has expensive taste in keeping people inside.”

Three dull knocks sounded beyond the door. A pause. Two more.

I looked at Coulter. “Your equipment wants out.”

He stepped in front of me. I put a hand on his shoulder and moved him aside. His radio crackled; he answered too quickly, and the old catering lift behind Khải began rising.

A steel platform emerged carrying folding chairs and two men. One raised a shotgun.

I grabbed the back of Khải's jacket and pulled him behind a brick pillar. The shot chewed the place where his head had been. Dust spread into my mouth. Coulter ran for the stairs.

“Active maintenance,” Khải said, breathing hard against the pillar.

The lift kept climbing. Beside us stood a catering trolley stacked with empty trays. I shoved it across the passage into the platform's underside. Trays flew into the gunman's feet. He ducked as though we'd released an extremely hostile dinner service.

I ran after the trolley, jumped, and caught the lift's lower frame. My shoulder hit its edge harder and higher than I'd planned. One corner jumped out of its guide. The platform tilted; folded chairs skated toward me with both men behind them.

I caught the shotgun barrel and pulled. Its owner kept hold long enough to bring his forehead against the steel lip. The gun came free. His companion seized the rail while a chair unfolded between his legs.

Khải reached the control panel and stopped the lift. I hauled myself onto the slanted deck. The second man reached inside his coat. I caught his wrist, took his knee in my ribs, and drove the heel of my hand into his throat. He folded. I put him face-first into the chairs. One snapped open around his neck, seating him halfway through a problem he couldn't stand up from.

Below, the steel door opened. Two guards dragged an older man in a filthy groundsman's jacket into the passage.

Khải shouted my name. I jumped off the lift and landed with one foot on the trolley. The trolley shot sideways. For one humiliating yard I became express catering. Khải caught its handle, swung me toward the guards, and let me step off into the nearer man's chest.

We crashed through the steel doorway. His shoulder struck the brick with a crack. I caught him before he fell and used his back to intercept his companion's club. The older man dropped between us. Khải pulled him out by the jacket.

A van reversed into the passage mouth. Its side door opened around a rifle. I shoved the guards into the room and threw myself through the doorway as shots struck the opposite wall.

Khải crouched behind the pillar with the groundsman. Blood darkened the old man's sleeve.

“Behind the lift,” Khải shouted. “Stairs!”

I crossed low, took the man's belt and collar, and lifted him into my arms. Khải led us through the narrow opening. We climbed two flights into a dark storeroom, crossed it, and came out beside the pavilion's empty bar. Khải locked the connecting door. Something heavy hit it immediately.

A decorative bronze wheel stood beside the bar, waist-high and fixed to a ridiculous wooden plinth. I tore it free and wedged it against the door. The lock burst; the door opened half an inch into bronze and stopped.

We walked out through the public entrance with the groundsman between us. An early hotel tour watched us cross the polished floor.

“Kitchen accident,” Khải told them.

A woman asked whether lunch would be late. I looked at the blood dripping off my sleeve. Khải said probably, then held the outer door for us.

In the car, the groundsman said his name was Mick Baines. He'd worked at Briar for twenty-nine years. When I asked why they'd locked him up, he managed Oscar's name and the words I saw before he lost consciousness.

Irene sent us to a doctor she trusted and arrived before the bullet came out of Mick's arm. The look on her face when she recognized him made the room feel smaller. She'd been trying to find him for months. Once the doctor said he would live, Irene told us to go and promised to call when he could speak.

At An Vân, after the staff had left, Khải poured water through the dust in my hair. I bent over a wastebasket while pieces of Briar dropped out of me.

“Some people pay for this,” I said.

“Then send them here,” Khải told me. “We could use a quieter profession.”

He brushed grit from my collar. His fingers slowed beside my neck. I straightened. The model houses were visible through the open office door, their tiny windows carrying the late light. There was plenty of room for either of us to step away.

I put my hand at his waist. Khải said my name, softly this time, and I kissed him before it became a question.

He kissed me back. One hand closed against my jaw; the other gripped my ruined shirt. I drew him closer. His mouth was warm, and the dust still caught between our clothes made the embrace feel terribly physical, terribly present. I didn't think of a way to make it accidental. It wasn't.

Khải stepped back first. “You're married.”

“I know,” I said.

“That doesn't shrink because we say it quietly,” Khải said.

I looked down at my wedding ring. He watched me do it and moved farther from my hand.

“Go home, Aiden,” Khải said.

I wanted to stay. I wanted an argument that didn't insult him, my wife, or both of them. None arrived. I picked up my jacket.

At the doorway I turned. Khải stood beside the table with a hand on the circuit plan, looking at a place we'd escaped together.

“I'm not sorry I kissed you,” I said.

Khải looked back for a long time. “That will make this harder.”

Downstairs the ceramics shop was closing. Its owner waved. I waved with the hand that wore my wedding ring, then went to the car and drove toward the house I'd been told to return to.

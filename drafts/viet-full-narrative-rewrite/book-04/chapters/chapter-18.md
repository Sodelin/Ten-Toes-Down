## Chapter 18: No Dancing in the Garage

Khải found me washing blood out of my hand at Rafa's utility sink.

He asked whether it was new. I explained that it was an old cut maintaining a vigorous social life. He took a clean towel from the shelf, wrapped my palm, and watched red appear through it.

“Somebody should look at that,” Khải said.

“You are somebody,” I said.

“My license concerns buildings,” Khải said.

Through the partly open door Saint negotiated delivery of a dance floor. Rafa objected that his garage contained open inspection pits. Saint suggested this would encourage proper footwork. Something struck a workbench; Rafa's reply brought several generations of mechanics into the conversation.

Khải smiled at the noise. He wore a blue shirt I knew the buttons of. He stood carefully beyond my reach. I watched him shift half an inch to avoid a pipe behind his shoulder and wanted to put my hand where the pipe had been.

“How's Shawna?” Khải asked.

“Working. Buyer today,” I said.

“That wasn't the question,” Khải said.

I looked at the towel. “She doesn't tell me everything she's feeling.”

“She's earned privacy,” Khải said.

I nodded. Outside, Saint proposed putting white tape around the holes so guests could distinguish them from dancing.

“After tomorrow,” I told Khải, “I'd like to see you without a plan between us.”

“I'd like that,” Khải said. I looked up. He kept speaking. “After you've moved out. After she knows because you told her, instead of because a tablet has better manners than you.”

I didn't answer quickly. He watched it land and left it there.

“I'm not proud of my part,” Khải said. “I knew whose shirt you changed out of. I let you stay. I wanted you enough to accept something I'd tell any friend to refuse.”

“It wasn't only—” I began.

“I know,” Khải interrupted. “Being more than sex doesn't make what we did to her kinder.”

The dance floor hit something else outside. Khải glanced toward the door and let out a breath.

“I also don't want every conversation to become a funeral,” Khải said.

“Saint would book chairs,” I told him.

He laughed unwillingly. I folded the towel and left it by the sink instead of reaching for him.

“I'll move,” I said.

“Because your marriage has reached that conversation,” Khải replied. “Don't make me the address you run to.”

“I can afford my own address,” I said.

“The houses aren't in doubt.” Khải touched the unbruised side of my face. I held his hand there briefly. Then he stepped away. “Come on. Before Saint opens a nightclub over a hole.”

The garage had acquired tablecloths, standing lamps, and a waiter arranging flowers around a transmission. Rafa had stopped explaining and put an oily rag under the vase. The Crown Wagon filled the rear bay, its ornamental crown reattached by Nessa with visible distaste. Irene arranged glasses inside its freed display cabin.

Celeste arrived first with an expensive bottle. Saint reached for it. Celeste moved it past him into Nessa's hands.

“Bad week?” Celeste asked.

“Two,” Nessa said.

“Drink it with somebody you like,” Celeste said.

Crowe and Petra came together. He admired the Argent, looked around, and asked about the piano. I said it was learning to live without him. Petra smiled into her glass.

Hale arrived in his conductor's cap, requested chairs that remained stationary, and sat pointedly on a toolbox. Alma Serrano brought two women who checked the doors before accepting drinks. Felix Dunn brought a folding chair because expensive venues mistreated his back. Moses King's driver carried a bag of keys. June Park brought pastries and told Saint they belonged to everybody before he developed an interpretation.

Seven rival crews had dressed for dinner in a mechanic's garage so they could decide whether tomorrow would kill them.

Saint clapped. “Private technical demonstration! Drinks free! Dancing apparently a felony!”

Rafa pointed to a covered pit. “I'll put you in that one.”

Irene stepped out of the wagon. “René would like to explain his week before we eat.”

Our men brought Duval from the office. His burned wrist was bandaged. His ordinary shoes seemed to have diminished him more than the escort. Crowe recognized him and lowered his glass.

“You said the steam room was closed for tile,” Crowe told him.

“Work was being done,” Duval answered.

Nessa stepped down. “I was the work.”

She described the changing cubicle, forced trips to Briar, and locking bar. Eddie and Louis stood where she could see them. Nobody interrupted. When she faltered over one day, Louis quietly supplied it. She thanked him and continued.

Duval attempted to explain that Thành had exaggerated his authority. Irene took his good wrist and led him into the display cabin.

“Show us how little authority this had,” Irene told him.

She closed the inner door. Nessa slid the removed bar through temporary demonstration brackets. Duval pushed from inside. His face appeared at the armored window. Bottled water shook on the shelf as he struck the door.

Irene stood beside it and described the final procession. The titles would be inside; the wagon would leave; the north gates would close; tankers would arrive. The guests stared through the window at a familiar man discovering a small portion of his own hospitality.

Felix put down his pastry. “Our families?”

“Aster House,” Irene said. “Different supper, good band, doors they can use. They're being told before they travel.”

“Open it,” June said.

Nessa removed the bar. Duval stumbled out breathing hard, though the room had air. June looked at his bandage and asked how many days he'd left the workers inside. He couldn't answer without making himself worse.

Moses took the bag from his driver. His niece and her friends had planned to come. Irene told him to send them to her house; she'd pay for every drink keeping them away from Briar. Moses selected a key and gave it to Tess.

“Red transporter. Twelve seated. More if frightened,” Moses said.

“I'll take it,” Tess said. “Driver decides for himself.”

The driver nodded. “I'll drive.”

Khải spread his drawings across a covered hood. Alma's women examined the passages with Celeste. Hale removed his cap and asked whether we still had a competition.

“Yes,” I told him. “I intend to win.”

“While we help you?” Hale asked.

“Help yourselves go home. Race me as hard as you like before that,” I said.

Celeste smiled. “Good. I've brought someone who can drive.”

Tess looked up from the keys. “Refreshing change for you.”

We ate around cars. The entrants compared the plans they'd received with Nessa's account of the changes. They selected their own people for passages. June's security chief went outside to call the families. Alma marked a second evacuation route in pen. Louis corrected a turn. The plan became less mine as more people supplied parts they were willing to do.

Felix folded his chair and placed it inside the Crown Wagon.

“Reserving a competent seat,” Felix explained to Rafa.

Irene gave Eddie the transfer papers. He would deliver the wagon after dark. Nessa would arrive as mechanic, Louis with the display cabin. Their white jackets matched the captured crew, and Thành's men expected employees too ordinary to study closely. The altered locks would look intact from outside while leaving exits usable within.

Petra approached with Crowe's hand held in hers. She told me Ellis had something to say. Crowe looked at her, then at me.

“I behaved badly,” Crowe said. “Over the piano.”

“You nearly became the bench,” I reminded him.

Crowe swallowed. “Thank you for stopping that.”

He gave me an envelope containing twenty thousand dollars for the moving damage. Saint appeared at once and suggested lessons also qualified. I passed him the envelope.

“Mrs. Devlin,” I told Saint. “Advance payment.”

Saint's delight weakened. “How far ahead?”

“Until she respects your left hand,” I said.

I left before the wagon. Everyone had a job; mine at eight was to arrive where I'd told Shawna I would.

She waited in the workroom at Orchard House. Samples for three stores hung beside the window. On a tray sat two empty mugs. We normally began difficult conversations by making tea. She'd left this one without that little shelter.

“Buyer?” I asked.

“Confirmed,” Shawna said. “Delivery in six weeks.”

I told her it was wonderful. She agreed. She sat on the high stool and pointed me toward a chair so she wouldn't have to crane her neck while angry. A small notebook lay in her lap. She closed it without showing me what she'd written.

“I want us separated,” Shawna said.

I had anticipated the words. My hands still went cold when I heard them.

“All right,” I said.

Shawna watched me. “Easy?”

“No. I'm trying to listen,” I said.

Shawna looked at the notebook before continuing. “I keep asking if I could sleep beside you without wondering which room you're thinking about. I can't. I won't practice until I get good enough to make you comfortable.”

“You don't need to improve anything,” I said.

“I know, Aiden,” Shawna said.

Outside the window the pool bent around the terrace in its ridiculous shape. We'd laughed there together about asking an architect to fix it. Her feet had rested in my lap. That evening remained real; it didn't defend me against this one.

“I'll move into town,” I said.

“After tomorrow's immediate danger. I won't have a suitcase in the hall turning the race into one of your performances,” Shawna said.

“I won't,” I said.

“Don't take a bullet as an apology,” Shawna said. “I'd still be angry. There'd just be forms.”

My breath nearly became a laugh. Her mouth moved briefly too, then settled. She told me she would use the workroom until her showroom was ready. Valerie had spoken to Benny, who was willing to extend the arrangement while she moved properly. My monthly appearances at his games remained my obligation.

“He doesn't punish furniture for what happens on it, apparently,” Shawna said.

“Benny,” I said.

“I told him never to describe my marriage through upholstery again,” Shawna said.

“You,” I said.

She looked toward the samples. When she spoke again her voice had roughened.

“I loved living here with you,” Shawna said.

“I loved it too,” I said.

“I know,” Shawna answered. “That's part of what's so fucking cruel.”

I stayed in the chair. She wiped beneath one eye, annoyed at the tear and then at being annoyed. I said softly that she could be sad.

Shawna looked at me. “Thank you, mayor of my face.”

I lowered my head. She laughed once, painfully. Then she stood and told me she'd spend tomorrow evening at Denise's with Marisol, a bottle, and a long list of opinions. The security car would stay outside. She didn't need ten-minute calls from me.

“I won't,” I said.

At the door I hesitated. “The piano?”

Shawna shut her eyes. “Take your fucking sky piano.”

We laughed at the same thing for the first time that evening. I let it last the length it lasted. Then I went upstairs to the guest room, and she closed the workroom door.

## Chapter 5: Everything Looks Better Moving

Rafa's office doors opened and a red car reversed through them with a desk lamp balanced on its roof.

Rafa walked alongside, holding his coffee level through an effort visible in his neck. “Tess. This is the room where I do paperwork.”

The driver turned around a stack of tires and stopped beside my knees. “Then put your paperwork farther from the route.”

The lamp toppled into Rafa's hand. He looked at it as though wondering whether a man could quit his own building.

Tess Calder climbed out wearing a green work shirt and black jeans. Her hair was cropped on one side and tied back on the other. A pale scar crossed her chin. She assessed me from head to foot without making any of the adjustments people normally made when they recognized my face.

“You want the Ducal,” Tess said.

“You want to drive my car,” I said.

“I want a good car. We'll discuss the pronoun afterward,” Tess replied.

Rafa explained that she had driven for films, then for people who preferred fewer films of what they did. The hours had improved; the catering had suffered. I liked both of them better for telling me that before negotiation.

His garage occupied a former bus depot behind Bell Street. Hậu waited in the yard beside our white coupe, a low, polished wedge that seemed designed to pass underneath a disagreement. Tess crouched beside it, checked the tires, then opened the hood. Rafa listened to her insults with the faint pride of a man hearing his work understood.

Saint arrived holding white racing jackets trimmed in gold. Each had a crown over THE KING'S MEN.

Tess took one, borrowed Rafa's marker, crossed out MEN, and wrote PROBLEMS.

“Collective term,” Saint objected.

“Now it's accurate,” Tess said. She held the marker out until he took it.

Hậu laughed hard enough to lean against the coupe. Rafa moved his elbow off the paint. I told Saint the revised jackets were approved.

Tess put me in the passenger seat. We circled the yard at walking pace. Then she asked if my heart had any complaints and, without waiting for a useful answer, introduced it to several.

The car launched toward two buses. I saw a gap between them, understood its width, and rejected the information. Tess folded the mirrors against the windows. We shot through. She swung around the office, reversed faster than I liked going forward, and stopped at the same mark where we'd begun. The desk lamp was still in Rafa's hand.

“Now,” Tess said, “ownership.”

I agreed to her fee, disputed the percentage, and reconsidered when she observed that she could also drive very quickly with somebody less wealthy beside her. By afternoon we'd hired each other and headed for Briar with Hậu, Rafa, and Saint following.

Before we left, Tess made me sit with her on the workshop step while she wrote the agreement on Rafa's invoice pad. Saint tried to supply a ceremonial pen. It leaked gold ink onto his thumb. Tess used Rafa's pencil.

“This amount if we start,” Tess said, drawing a line beneath the fee. “This share if we finish first. Repairs aren't deducted from my share because you decide to wear a car through a fence.”

“A suspiciously specific concern,” I said.

“Rafa describes you vividly,” Tess told me.

I read the page. “If we don't reach the start?”

“You pay for the time I've already worked. I don't charge you for days I haven't. Your people don't stand outside my house negotiating it.” Tess handed me the pencil and waited.

I added my signature. She read it, tore off her copy, and folded it into her shirt pocket.

“You came from films,” I said. “Why leave?”

Tess looked across the yard at the white coupe. “People kept explaining what a camera could hide. Eventually I wanted someone to care whether the car actually got through.”

“And the later employers?” I asked.

“Cared very much,” Tess said. “About the wrong person arriving.”

She stood and held out the keys, not offering them, only letting me see she'd taken possession. I found I liked being told the terms by someone who expected me to understand them. Saint tried signing beneath us as witness. The leaking gold pen left a thumbprint instead. Tess looked at it, drew a little box around the mark, and labeled it DAMAGE ALREADY INCURRED.

The city thinned into western warehouses and scrub-covered hills. Briar's grandstands curved around the old banked circuit. Above the garages stood the Ducal, thirty suites and a glass restaurant catching enough sunlight to look expensive from a mile away.

Saint pointed through his window when we stopped at the gate. “Portrait up there.”

“Which one?” Tess asked.

“We're negotiating the face,” Hậu explained.

The guard studied Tess's driver's card twice and waved us through with more respect than my name had earned.

“You know the place?” I asked her.

“They owe me a fence,” Tess said.

Eight other teams occupied the paddock. Sports cars gleamed under awnings; a black wagon vibrated beside a tool chest with an engine that sounded like a helicopter eating cutlery. Thành stood on a balcony with Long. He raised a glass. I raised a hand toward Saint, who was already attempting to arrange the photographers into a favorable weather system.

“Let her drive,” I told him.

Practice was three laps and a timed service-lane turn. Hậu fastened my harness so thoroughly I suspected he was trying to mail me. Tess looked at my hands until I put them on my knees.

“You own those,” Tess said. “Keep the rest decorative.”

On the first lap the banking climbed around us. Tess made little corrections before I knew corrections were required. We entered openings I would have measured twice; by the time I'd finished measuring them in my head we were somewhere else.

The black wagon appeared behind us on lap two. Tess moved aside. It followed. Its front bumper struck our rear quarter and pushed us up the banking toward the outside wall.

Tess corrected without speaking. The wagon came again. Through its side window I saw the passenger bracing himself against the dashboard, laughing.

“Somebody you owe money?” Tess asked.

“I prefer creditors who knock,” I said.

Tess braked. The wagon overshot us. She turned beneath its tail and accelerated for the service lane, which should have opened between the garages and a loading platform.

A barrier truck rolled across it. The gate behind the truck began closing. Ahead of us were truck, wall, and the low gap beneath a raised loading deck.

“Under there?” I asked.

Tess looked once. “If you remove your head.”

I unlatched the harness, shoved my door open, and reached for the platform's hanging edge as she angled toward it. My fingers hooked underneath. The moving car tried to leave my arm behind. I got one foot under the seat frame and pulled upward with everything above it.

The platform rose crookedly. Its safety arm tore loose, swung past my ear, and rang against the support. Tess drove beneath the lifted deck. I released it and fell inward. My door hit the barrier truck's rear wheel; metal folded across the opening where my knees had been.

We burst into the lane behind the garages and stopped. Tess looked at the folded door, then at me.

“Rafa's going to need a quiet room,” Tess said.

The wagon entered behind us. Its passenger came out with a bar. The driver removed his yellow-striped helmet and drew a pistol. I squeezed through what remained of my doorway before the passenger reached it.

I caught his bar in both hands and pushed it straight back against his chest. He went across the lane and into the wagon's side window. Glass burst around his head. I pulled the bar free as he folded over the sill, leaving him hanging there by the pockets of his jacket.

The driver raised the pistol. Hậu hit him from the garage passage, wrapped the gun arm, and carried him sideways into a tower of tires. Rubber collapsed around them. Hậu rose with the gun. The driver rose with a tire around his waist and the wrong expression for keeping it.

I caught his collar and put him on the wagon's hood. “Who sent you?”

He spat. I turned his cheek against the warm metal. “That answer costs more.”

His eyes moved upward to the balcony. I followed them. Long stood at the rail alone; Thành had disappeared.

Rafa ran into the lane, saw the coupe, and stopped so abruptly Saint collided with him.

“My door,” Rafa said.

“Our door,” I corrected.

“Don't marry it after the accident,” Rafa snapped.

Track security removed the two attackers quickly and asked almost no questions. Trinh arrived in time to advise Hậu to keep the pistol in our bag. The guards' eagerness to take custody of everything interested her no more favorably than it did me.

Ten minutes later Thành appeared with an apology and an offer to replace the coupe. I stood beside its ruined opening while he explained competitive enthusiasm.

“Your men understood the assignment,” I told him.

“It's my circuit. Naturally my staff arrived quickly,” Thành said.

“For now,” I replied.

Thành looked at the crooked loading deck. “Your father's son.”

“He'd have taken the whole platform,” I said. Long's eyes moved to my hands.

We left before Thành could offer lunch. Tess had learned the route. I had learned how its owner arranged hospitality. Rafa had learned that a door could become an invoice before the warranty expired.

That evening I found Shawna fitting a singer at the atelier. The singer, famous enough to require three people to order water, stood perfectly still while Shawna moved a pin near her ribs.

“Breathe as usual,” Shawna told her.

“I'm not sure what's usual,” the singer admitted.

“We'll work it out,” Shawna said, and continued pinning.

Marisol gave me coffee and directed me to an area where all surfaces could survive it. When the singer left, she ordered six more pieces. Her manager paid the deposit without asking for a discount. Shawna watched the confirmation arrive and grinned, unguarded and delighted.

I took both her hands and drew her close. She kissed me, then caught sight of the sleeve the loading deck had torn.

“Most of the car still exists,” I told her before she asked.

Shawna sighed, fetched scissors, and put them carefully on the counter. “Take it off. Explain while I can still mend something.”

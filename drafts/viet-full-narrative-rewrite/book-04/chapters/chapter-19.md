## Chapter 19: The Old Men Have Tickets

Tín brought his own cushion to Friday's preview at Briar.

The invitation woman explained that their seats were upholstered. Tín told her the dentist's were too. She looked toward Dad for assistance. Dad had already decided this conversation belonged to her.

Our preview occupied the hospitality wing above the northern stand. Workers below polished sponsor boards and wheeled equipment toward the garages. The banked western bend rose at the far end of the circuit. Beyond it the Ducal's white terraces caught the morning sun.

Mom followed my gaze. “Expensive distraction.”

“Good view,” I said.

“You own chairs with views,” Mom reminded me.

Hòa examined an old renovation plaque by the entrance. He ran a finger beneath a name, smiled to himself, and put his hands behind his back. Before I could ask, the invitation woman found Tín's age and announced there must be an error.

“Several,” Tín said. “Be specific.”

“One hundred and six?” the invitation woman asked.

Tín touched his medal. “Don't shout. Somebody will ask for a speech.”

Her manager arrived, heard the number, and immediately offered a commemorative presentation. Tín asked if the medal would be heavier than his current one. The manager said that could be arranged. Dad rubbed his forehead. Mom photographed him doing it.

Khải met us inside with drawings and a visitor badge. Mom thanked him for the exit plans. Dad looked at him for a long second, then shook his hand. Nothing broke. I noticed I'd been waiting to establish that and disliked the relief.

“You've marked the routes yourself?” Dad asked Khải.

“Every one I could enter,” Khải replied. “The others are marked uncertain. Nessa knows changes I wasn't shown.”

Dad opened the folder Khải offered. “Which goes outside without crossing a public room?”

Khải turned a page and pointed. I stood beside them, recognizing the drawing we'd argued over at Rafa's, while Dad followed the stair with a fingertip. He asked about a landing width. Khải gave the measurement without looking at me for help.

“We'll start there,” Dad said, handing back the folder.

Khải nodded. Dad hadn't given him permission about my life. He'd asked about a door and accepted a competent answer. I was the one trying to make every gesture carry another conversation. Mom moved between us to inspect the page herself.

“I want to see daylight at the bottom,” Mom said. “A sign promising daylight is not daylight.”

“Agreed,” Khải replied. He turned toward the suite, and we followed him in.

Tín looked from Khải to me. “Architect?”

“Yes, sir,” Khải said.

“These stairs were made by a man who hated knees,” Tín said.

“There's another stair east,” Khải replied.

“I hate his knees too,” Tín said, handing him the cushion while he adjusted his medal. Khải laughed and returned it. Apparently they'd completed an introduction.

The suite held four white sofas, a bar, glass facing the straight, and a main corridor door. Another door hid behind a curtain and a cabinet whose base blocked it. Dad walked directly there and tried the handle.

Locked.

A young attendant hurried over. “Private staff access.”

“To where?” Dad asked.

“Kitchen level,” the attendant said.

Khải confirmed the eastern stair and service court. The attendant suggested guests use the main corridor. Mom examined the cabinet.

“Move your preference six feet left,” Mom told him.

Dad put two fingers beneath the cabinet and lifted it away. Its glass shelves stayed level. A tiny porcelain horse remained upright on the middle shelf, staring at the attendant with exactly Mom's expression.

“Where?” Dad asked.

The attendant stepped back into a sofa and pointed. Dad set the cabinet down. Mom took the key ring from the hand the young man had finally extended and opened the door. Cool air rose from below.

Hòa had stopped by the main corridor, looking at an old iron rail through the next doorway. He said he'd take that way. I suggested we remain together.

“Then walk faster,” Granddad said kindly, and disappeared.

Tín settled on his cushion. Dad and Mom went down the eastern stair with Khải to check the bottom landing. I stood between the two doors wondering when the operation had become a school trip led by hostile teachers.

Thành arrived with liquor and two men who didn't look at the view.

“Your family honors us,” Thành said.

Tín lifted his eyes. “Does it?”

Thành's smile paused. He tried Mr. Lê. Tín pointed out that several people present answered to that and suggested a more useful question. Thành set down the bottle. Tín examined the label and asked whether it was for his age or Thành's nerves.

One escort turned toward the windows, shoulders moving. Thành noticed. The man stopped enjoying himself.

“Looking forward to the result,” Thành told me.

“So am I,” I said.

“Your car ready?” Thành asked.

“Tess says so,” I said.

Thành smiled. “Old machines can surprise you.”

Tín patted the cushion beside him. “Sit down and try that again.”

Thành stayed standing. Ordinary guests, he told us, had received Irene's revised evening invitations. He treated the move to Aster House as a social inconvenience. His thumb scraped at the liquor label until a tiny curl of paper came free. He knew something had changed. I couldn't tell how much he understood.

A crash sounded below. Dad called my name. A second crash struck the stairwell before I reached the door.

The eastern landing overlooked a freight shaft down to the service court. The catering lift hung tilted between levels, its upper support peeling away from the wall. A loaded trolley pressed against the bent rail. Two cooks clung to the deck. A third hung from its lower edge.

Dad had an arm hooked beneath the platform. His shoes ground shallow lines into the concrete as it dragged outward. Mom lay flat on the landing, reaching for the hanging worker. Khải held her ankles.

“Take him,” Dad said.

I crouched beside Mom and caught the man's slippery wrist. He stared at me without blinking. I got his other arm; Mom caught his belt. Together we pulled him over the edge and rolled him onto the landing. He tried to stand, discovered his knees had gone elsewhere, and sat.

The remaining cooks couldn't get around the jammed trolley. Dad lifted the deck another inch. A bracket tore out of the wall with masonry attached.

Tín appeared behind us with his cushion. “Who's redecorating?”

He handed the cushion to the rescued worker and put a palm under the platform's near corner. Dad's bent elbow straightened. The platform rose off his fingers. For a second his hand held nothing.

Tín pushed the deck level. The final support tore loose. He held it there, entire lift, trolley, cooks, and trays, while looking at the men above his hand.

“Get off,” Tín told them. “I don't deliver lunch.”

They scrambled over the trolley. Dad lifted one by the apron onto the landing. I hauled the other across. Tín drew the detached deck toward the opening like a stubborn drawer, then rotated it onto its edge. Dad caught the upper corner before it could swing into the workers.

“Good,” Tín said. “You can take the light end.”

They rested it against the inner wall. Plaster dented the length of the steel. The trolley finally rolled free, tipped, and released a hundred tiny bread rolls down the stairs. One bounced off Dad's shoe and continued to the service court.

Mom watched them go. “Lunch is downstairs.”

The rescued worker on Tín's cushion laughed so hard he began coughing. Dad cleared the lower flight. Tín turned the platform edgewise, carried it down the steps, and left it against the court wall. Then he came back for the cushion.

Thành had followed us. I saw his face before he restored its expression. His chest had gone still inside the pale jacket.

The worker returned Tín's cushion with both hands, then tried to give him a wad of tips. Tín took one dollar and tucked the rest back into the man's apron.

“Keep earning,” Tín told him. “I'll come for a bigger meal.”

The man nodded until Mom steadied his shoulder. Someone below began applauding. Others joined who had seen nothing except a freight lift suddenly parked upright where no lift belonged.

The cook I'd pulled over the landing tried standing again. This time his legs held, but his hand shook when he reached for the rail. Mom put a cup of water in it and told him to sit until he'd finished. He stared at the cushion on Tín's arm.

“I got it dirty,” the cook said.

Tín examined the smear. “Good. Now it looks used. They won't try to sell me another.”

The cook laughed weakly. Dad fetched the worker's fallen shoe from the landing edge and put it in front of him. Two of his kitchen colleagues sat on either side. They'd stopped apologizing and begun counting who else had been in the lift. All three. All out. I heard them repeat that and let them have the certainty before I turned back toward Thành.

Dad looked at Thành. “Maintenance?”

“Accident,” Thành said.

Khải examined a bright cut through the old rust of the support. He didn't touch it. I saw him photograph it, then put his phone away.

“We'll deliver through the east court now,” Khải said.

“We'll secure it,” Thành replied.

“We'll keep it clear,” Dad told him.

They looked at one another until Mom asked whether any other parts of the building wanted to fall before lunch.

Hòa returned through the upper corridor carrying three visitor badges and a man's expensive shoe. Dust marked his sleeve. He said he'd found the staircase.

I looked at the shoe. “Where's the owner?”

“Sitting,” Granddad said.

I followed him around the corner. Three security men sat beside the iron stairs. One held his bare foot. Another stared at the rail, which had become a smooth loop around the lower newel. The third began rising when Hòa appeared, then sat again with remarkable promptness.

“They were closing it,” Hòa explained.

Hòa returned the shoe. “Foot's swollen. Loosen the laces.”

The man thanked him. I examined the twisted iron and the block of masonry shifted out of the wall behind it. I could see the results. I couldn't reconstruct a sequence that felt at all reasonable.

Granddad watched me considering it. “Don't block the stairs.”

I moved.

At lunch Tín received a heavy silver racing medallion on a blue ribbon. He wore it beside the red one. Saint arrived with our crew jackets and asked why nobody had invited him to arrange the presentation.

“You'd add a microphone,” Mom told him.

“History needs volume,” Saint said.

Tín held his glass toward the waiter. “History needs water.”

From the restaurant terrace I watched the Crown Wagon cross the paddock. Eddie drove. Nessa leaned from the side and raised two fingers. Louis remained with the cabin. The bar we'd removed lay in harmless pieces at Rafa's garage. Our people were in place.

Long stood under the garage awning. He'd seen the lift carried into the court. Now he watched my family eat. His nose was still flattened, his jaw yellowed. When he saw me looking, he touched the bridge of his nose and held my eyes.

Khải followed my gaze. “He's decided something.”

“So have I,” I said.

Mom put a plate between us. “Decide after fish. Neither of you has eaten.”

We took it. At the other end Tín watched Long go into the garage.

“Big boy,” Tín told Dad.

“Paid to be,” Dad said.

“Hope he saved,” Tín replied.

Hòa folded his dusty sleeve over its pale smear and asked the waiter whether any of the bread rolls had survived lunch's journey downstairs.

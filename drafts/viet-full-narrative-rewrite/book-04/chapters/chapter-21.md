## Chapter 21: Bad Seats

The gate shut behind the last coach with a noise I felt through the Argent's floor.

At the token booth a young attendant stared at the brass shoe, silver baton, and red glove while his radio repeated an instruction he'd clearly never practiced. He stamped our card third. June had used the south approach; Celeste was already inside.

Tess leaned across me. “Tell June her driver has good hands.”

The attendant nodded without understanding and handed over a black crown badge. Beyond him Meridian guards carried short heavy cases toward the garages. I called Trinh.

“Move staff,” I told her.

“Started,” Trinh replied. “Family in hospitality. Your mother's found the man responsible for the cabinet.”

“Unfortunate,” I said.

“She sounded pleased,” Trinh said.

Tess took us through the paddock. Bree stood beside Celeste's blue car with a torn sleeve wrapped around her wrist. June waved from the next bay. Hale's people were trying to remove a decorative radiator flag without burning themselves. Across the paddock the Crown Wagon waited near the north service entrance. Eddie sat behind its high windshield. Nessa stood at an open inspection panel, making herself look like a reason to leave the vehicle alone. Louis guarded the prize cases inside.

Irene met us at the garage steps. Coulter had changed the schedule: everyone was to attend a presentation in the stand before the circuit stage. I said no. Irene told me she'd used more words.

Thành appeared on the giant screen over the pits, smiling beneath a nine-helmet banner in the hospitality restaurant. The camera moved enough to show empty tables. He looked past it and seemed, finally, to count the audience he didn't have.

“A memorable evening,” Thành announced.

Irene took out her phone. “I'll improve the audience.”

While she called the screen booth, four vans moved along the outside road. Two turned east toward the service court; two continued toward the old north stalls. Tankers followed. One blocked the northern gate. Another turned into the passage the Crown Wagon was meant to use beneath the hotel.

Thành knew enough to change his exits.

I told Tess to keep the Argent ready and ran toward the hospitality stair. She called after me not to leave a message from beneath something. Khải caught up at the lower landing carrying a tool bag and a radio taken from an unattended desk.

“East stair clear,” Khải said. “Your father put the cabinet outside.”

“How far?” I asked.

“Catering is upset,” Khải said.

At the upper landing a guard pulled the corridor door shut from within. I caught the edge before it latched and opened it, carrying him backward with it. His shoes squealed across the floor. Khải caught the radio falling from his hand and stepped around him.

“Another useful one,” Khải said. “They keep telling people to close things.”

In the suite Mom stood holding the cabinet's porcelain horse. Dad had a guard against the wall by his vest. Tín sat on the cushion wearing both medals. Hòa watched the television, which repeated Thành several seconds behind the voice coming through the restaurant door.

“Reception's bad,” Hòa told me.

Mom handed me the horse. I put it on the window ledge. The guard in Dad's hand explained that he'd followed instructions.

“Where were they going?” Dad asked.

The guard blinked. Dad clarified that he'd followed instructions here; where next? The man couldn't find an answer he wanted to give.

Through the glass I saw a van turn into the service court below. Two cooks pushed a trolley toward the exit. The van accelerated at them.

Mom said Dad's name. He put the guard down, crossed the room, opened the terrace door, and stepped over the rail.

It was a floor and a half down. Dad landed between trolley and headlights. He caught the bumper, bent his knees, and stopped the van so hard its rear wheels rose. The driver's face hung above him through the windshield, mouth open, hands welded to the wheel.

Dad pushed backward. The rear wheels hit and rolled. The driver tried steering around him. The bumper twisted. Dad lifted one corner, pivoted, and laid the whole van onto its side against the court wall.

“Turn it off,” Dad told the driver through the window.

The engine stopped.

Behind me Mom snatched the porcelain horse from the ledge and threw it past my ear. It struck the hand of a gunman entering through the restaurant door. Porcelain burst; his pistol hit the floor. Mom reached him before he bent, drove a knee into his stomach, and struck beneath his chin. He went backward into the drinks trolley. Glass crashed across the floor. Hòa lifted his shoes without looking away from the television.

“Why does your building keep sending men?” Mom demanded through the doorway.

A second gunman fired from the corridor. I caught the sofa frame and tipped it toward him. Shots thudded into the upholstery. Khải pulled Mom behind the inner wall. Tín rose with his cushion held protectively against his chest.

I shoved the sofa through the doorway. Its feet snapped off at the threshold. The gunman tried getting around it; I drove it harder into the wall. He disappeared behind the back, gun arm projecting over one end. I took the pistol and stepped aside. He slid into the seat he'd created.

Tín examined him. “My place.”

The man attempted to rise. I told him to stay. He reconsidered and remained seated inside most of the sofa's stuffing.

Below, another van entered the court and stopped beyond Dad. Its side doors opened. Guns pointed toward the workers crouching behind the trolley.

Tín placed his cushion on the table, crossed the terrace, and jumped.

His medals lifted from his chest. He landed on the overturned van's roof, dented it inward, and stepped down between the vehicles. Dad pulled the cooks through the eastern doorway. The overturned van's driver began scrambling out through the rear.

Tín took hold of its exposed lower frame. Dad hauled the driver clear before the body moved. Tín lifted the empty vehicle, turned once, and sent it sliding across the court on its side.

It struck the second van broadside. Both slammed against the wall. Gunmen scattered. One fell under the open side door, clear of the wheels; another landed inside a stack of empty catering crates and remained there kicking at plastic. Tín walked after them, apparently having seen something he wanted to discuss in person.

Mom stood beside me. “He'll lose a medal.”

“That's the concern?” I asked.

“You'll hear about it for years,” Mom replied.

Dad looked up from the court and pointed toward the paddock. “Finish your job.”

I hesitated. Dad said my name. I nodded.

Hòa came to the terrace holding the remote. The northern stair was open, he said. He'd spoken to people who were confused about that. Mom asked where they were now.

“Below,” Hòa said.

“Alive?” Mom asked.

“They have opinions,” Hòa said.

On the television Irene had taken Thành's microphone. A Meridian guard lay across a table behind her with a cloth tangled around his head and an enormous floral arrangement on his back. I hadn't seen how she'd done that. The result was persuasive.

“Program change,” Irene announced. “Everybody without an assigned crew role goes to the eastern court. Follow white jackets. If somebody tells you to sit beside a locked exit, ask why he's standing.”

Irene looked into the camera. “Thành, you cheap little bastard, I liked Oscar more than your hotel.”

The screen went black.

Khải took my arm. We went down the northern stairs past three men whose arms had been confined inside a length of railing bent into a large untidy ring. One asked for help. Khải said later, which seemed enough to keep him from adding requirements.

Smoke pushed along the old stable corridor. The gate to the paddock stood half closed. Beyond it two of Celeste's people struggled to move a wounded man on a catering trolley; the gate had caught its rear wheel.

I got both hands beneath the steel and lifted. My palm opened again. I felt the wet warmth before the pain. Khải freed the wheel and pulled the trolley through. I kept pushing until the gate's rollers jumped out of their guides. It tilted backward into the emptied corridor.

Khải called clear. I stepped through and let it fall. The impact woke the wounded man.

“Did we win?” the man asked.

“Collecting the prizes,” I told him.

Outside, crews dragged barriers across the approaches. June's people loaded cooks and cleaners into Moses's red transporter. Hale held a fire extinguisher upside down until Petra took it, turned it, and handed it back. Headlights swung through smoke where the tankers blocked the ridge road.

The Crown Wagon began moving toward its assigned tunnel. Eddie couldn't stay in the open: Meridian men were crossing toward its loaded cabin. North was blocked. East filled with evacuees. The tunnel was the route they'd expected to use before the new tankers arrived.

I ran to the wagon. Nessa opened the side door.

“Truck across the tunnel exit!” Nessa shouted. “And they're closing behind us.”

A utility truck swept into the paddock firing toward our cars. Tess brought the Argent out in reverse, its immense tail across the truck's nose. The driver braked. Celeste's people dragged their wounded man behind the blue car.

Khải pointed beneath the restaurant. “Lower apron. Our route.”

Three broad stone steps descended from the loading platform to the infield lane. We'd planned that route for the Argent. The Crown Wagon looked large enough to rent the stairs as staff accommodation.

I caught its side handle and climbed aboard. “Apron, Eddie.”

Eddie looked through the windshield at the drops, then at me. “You people hate doors.”

Dad called. I told him we were taking the wagon down steps and Eddie was complaining about architecture.

Tín's voice came through beside him. “Tell him the architect started it.”

Khải, running toward the steps below us, looked up when I laughed. I pointed ahead. He raised his hand and cleared the first barrier from our path.

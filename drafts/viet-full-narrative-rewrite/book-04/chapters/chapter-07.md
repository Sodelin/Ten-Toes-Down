## Chapter 7: Sunday Clothes on a Thursday

The piano entered Orchard House on a morning when everyone had something more useful to do.

Valerie directed six movers through the breakfast room. Shawna pointed at the tiger painting, and two men took it down before I could decide whether to defend the tiger or the wall. The stars on the piano caught the window light. Crowe's gold initial remained visible on the side, as if the instrument were still blaming him.

Saint arrived forty minutes later with a scarf and Mrs. Devlin, a small gray-haired woman in shoes that appeared to have outlasted several musical eras.

“Artistic warmth,” Saint explained when I looked at the scarf.

Mrs. Devlin opened the piano, played four notes, and frowned. “Somebody moved this badly.”

“Several times,” I said.

She sat Saint at the bench and rearranged his hands, back, knees, and apparent expectations. By the tenth minute he had a recognizable children's tune. By the eleventh he'd fitted his own name to it.

Shawna closed the door. “I have a meeting.”

“Here?” I asked.

“Anywhere else,” Shawna said.

We drove to Bell Street together. She went to a fitting. I went upstairs at the Pearl, where Trinh had laid photographs of Briar on the desk with my coffee.

Mick was alive. The doctor had found an infected arm and three broken ribs. He'd been held eleven days. Before sedation he had said Oscar survived the crash, and Thành's men put him into an enormous silver car.

Trinh slid a photograph toward me. The Argent Special looked like a shoe designed to impress other shoes, with a bronze bird above its radiator and exhaust pipes curled along the hood.

“Rusk has it,” Trinh said. “Private collection. Thành lent it for an event. Mick thinks Oscar left something inside.”

“And we need to inspect the upholstery,” I said.

“Irene has frozen the wagers until Thành signs revised terms. Northern stand open to inspection. Proven private attacks disqualify the attacker's stake,” Trinh said.

“He'll sign and attack more privately,” I told her.

Trinh tapped the page. “Then he'll have something to lose when we prove it.”

We planned the theft without calling it a loan. Tess liked the armored shell. Rafa's response had apparently exceeded the space available on Trinh's notes. Hậu's gym would remain open, the Pearl would keep its ordinary games, and extra men would cover both entrances. None of our employees had volunteered to become an unattended part of my adventure.

Outside, the rumor that I'd lifted a platform had become a report that I'd lifted a racetrack. By lunch the track had been moving. A woman caught me beside the bakery and asked me to bless her son's knees.

“What's wrong with them?” I asked.

“Nothing yet,” the mother said.

The boy stared at the pavement with the dedication of somebody hoping it would open. I told her to ask his coach, accepted another basket of fruit, and stood beside the boy for her photograph.

“My mother made me wear velvet to buy a washing machine,” I told him.

The boy looked up. “Why?”

“The machine was on sale. Nobody ever explained the jacket,” I said.

He laughed just as his mother took the picture. She thanked me like I'd fixed the knees after all.

At Denise's that afternoon Saint had arranged what he called a respectful acknowledgment. There were balloons, two microphones, and a cardboard cutout of me leaning toward the till as though considering a robbery.

Dad came in his work clothes. Mom wore the blue jacket she still called new after fifteen years. Hòa carried a paper bag; Tín wore sunglasses and his medal. Saint showed them to a table near a speaker. Tín looked at it until Saint moved the speaker, the stand, and finally an extension lead that had done nothing wrong.

Shawna arrived with Marisol and a magazine writer interested in the atelier. The writer looked at my cardboard face.

“Part of the collection?” the writer asked.

“Storage problem,” Shawna said.

I watched her talk at the far end of the table, shaping a sleeve with her hands. At nineteen she'd drawn a jacket on a restaurant bill and refused to leave until the waiter found another pen; the first had made the shoulders wrong. I still had the bill. She still moved her hands that way.

Saint tapped a microphone. “Friends, family, creditors with appointments! We honor a man who has changed the height of things.”

Dad leaned toward Mom. “How long?”

“Scarf,” Mom said. “Order dinner.”

The first gift was a heavy gold chain. The second was tires for life, which Rafa accepted before anyone could specify whose life. Two men carried the third in a long polished box.

Denise left the counter. “Absolutely fucking not.”

The undertaker held up both hands. “Display model.”

“It's a coffin in my restaurant,” Denise said.

Tín stood, inspected the lid, and knocked on the wood. “Too short.”

The undertaker blinked. “For you, sir?”

“His ego,” Tín said, pointing at me.

Mom folded over Dad's shoulder laughing. Shawna put a napkin in front of her face; it did nothing to conceal the sound. Even the undertaker laughed, then explained that I'd helped recover his cousin's van and he was offering an advance credit.

“Offer it on paper,” Denise instructed him.

His men carried the coffin outside. Tín followed, asking about discounts for people he wouldn't name. Saint regained the microphone and announced that I was a man of action. Mom told him to let me eat. He handed me the microphone instead.

Faces turned toward me. People I'd known since childhood, people who worked with me, people who managed perfectly well without me and still wanted to feed me. Hòa had opened his paper bag and was handing a repaired wooden toy to a child. Dad's arm rested behind Mom's chair. Shawna watched me over her glass.

“Thank you,” I said. “Anyone bringing furniture must consult my wife before unloading.”

Shawna lifted a hand. “No.”

“Excellent response time,” I said, and surrendered the microphone while the room laughed.

We ate. The magazine writer interviewed Shawna; Denise supplied three other women she should interview and corrected her account of the neighborhood twice. Outside, the coffin had gone into the wrong van. Two men argued over the open rear doors when Shawna and I walked back toward the atelier.

Before we left the table, the writer asked Shawna whether growing up around the neighborhood had influenced her work. Shawna held the blue sleeve of Mom's old jacket between two fingers and asked permission before turning its cuff.

“Look at this,” Shawna told the writer. “She lined it so it turns back properly. You can shorten the sleeve without making it look shortened.”

Mom tried to pull her arm away. “It's an old jacket.”

“It's a very good old jacket,” Shawna said. “You don't get to call it new at lunch and old when someone admires it.”

The writer bent to see the stitching. Mom gave up and extended her arm like a woman providing evidence under protest. Shawna explained the little decisions that let clothes survive a moving body: where pressure gathered, what fabric would recover, which seams shouldn't be visible simply because the wearer reached for something.

I watched the writer stop taking notes long enough to understand. Mom watched Shawna instead. Her face softened before she reclaimed her sleeve and instructed the writer to speak to the woman who repaired uniforms two streets over.

“Nobody discusses her because the work keeps being worn,” Mom said. “Go look.”

Shawna slipped her arm through mine. “You like cheering.”

“You didn't?” I asked.

“I like them. You become very shiny,” Shawna said.

I kissed her beside the bakery. She stopped, turned into me, and kissed me properly, her hand against my chest.

“Better,” Shawna said afterward.

At home she worked late. I brought tea and sat on the floor while she pinned a hem. We talked about the magazine and a house that might someday belong to us rather than Benny's arrangement. She wanted a sensible pool. I offered a differently shaped one. She told me I had already supplied enough architecture for the week in the form of a piano.

My phone vibrated beside my knee. Khải had sent Rusk's invitation: a masked party and private collection viewing the following night. He could get us through the front door. Was my jacket mended?

I stared at it. Shawna looked down from her hem.

“Another meeting?” Shawna asked.

“Rafa,” I said. “The car.”

The lie came out cleanly. She nodded and went back to work. Nothing broke to tell her what had happened.

Upstairs I put the gold chain beside the ring box she'd given me on our wedding morning. Then I answered Khải and asked where to meet him.

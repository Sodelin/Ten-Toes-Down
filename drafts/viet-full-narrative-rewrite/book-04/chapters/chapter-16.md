## Chapter 16: Mother Knows the Address

Mom asked me to bring paper samples to her shop, which meant I arrived with something she could take from my hands before telling me what she thought.

She put the box on the counter. The printer behind her fed out a wedding order in gold letters. Somebody was paying to make promises look expensive.

“Sleeping?” Mom asked.

“Badly,” I said.

“Good. Everybody else has been awake. It would be irritating to find you well rested,” Mom said.

She checked a sheet's alignment, set it aside, and told me Shawna had called. First about the woman who handled showroom leases. Then about why she was crying.

“I hurt her,” I said.

“Yes,” Mom said.

“I'm not asking you to excuse it,” I said.

Mom opened the paper tray. “Excellent. I'm running out of excuses in your size.”

She adjusted a guide and closed the tray harder than it needed. I watched the next sheet emerge.

“You could have spoken to her,” Mom said.

“I should have,” I said.

“Before, Aiden. The word doing all the work is before,” Mom said.

I nodded. She leaned on the counter with both hands.

“I love you,” Mom said. “You're also being an expensive idiot. Stop charging everybody else for the lesson.”

The bell over the door rang. A man in a charcoal coat entered carrying a pale envelope. He had a shaved line through his hair and a confidence that wavered when he saw me.

“Mrs. Lê?” the man asked. “Private matter.”

“Then strange premises,” Mom said. “We print things.”

He put the envelope on the counter. Thành wanted the family to reconsider its position.

“Our position is open until six,” Mom told him.

“Your son—” the messenger began.

“Then face him,” Mom said. “He's been difficult to miss for years.”

The man turned and told me to return the car and Duval, withdraw from the Run, and Thành would consider the matter closed.

“His definitions need work,” I said.

“Your mother doesn't need trouble here,” the messenger said.

Mom picked up the envelope and rubbed its edge. “Cheap paper. You threatened a printer on cheap paper.”

He reached for her wrist. She caught his hand first. One finger bent; his shoulder followed; his face arrived on the counter beside the sign asking people not to touch the cutter. The envelope slid off the edge and landed by his shoes.

“Read it,” Mom instructed him.

The messenger stared sideways. “Don't touch the cutter.”

“Now expand your understanding to include me,” Mom said.

He looked desperately toward me. I folded my arms.

“She's the owner,” I explained.

Mom released his finger, took his coat, and marched him to the door. She opened it with her foot and sent him into the planter Saint had installed without consulting her. The planter cracked around his hips. Soil fell into his lap. A plastic palm leaned across his head in a last effort at decoration.

Mom looked at the pieces. “Finally.”

He crawled out and hurried away. Mom read the threat, then fed it into the shredder. I suggested keeping it. She told me she could remember eight words and didn't need their awful paper to help.

Dad emerged from the rear carrying boxes. He'd heard enough. I bowed; he waited for me to straighten.

“Your wife?” Dad asked.

“Working,” I said.

“Good,” Dad said. He looked through the window at the planter. “Other problem?”

“Growing,” I said.

“You noticed,” Dad replied.

We closed early and went to the house. On the porch Tín and Hòa argued over a little radio. Tín believed somebody had paid the weather report to exaggerate. Hòa adjusted the dial without improving either forecast.

Dad looked at Granddad. “Your grandson's been busy.”

“Your son,” Hòa replied.

Tín lifted a hand. “He's all of yours. Stop passing the receipt.”

Mom sat on a step and removed soil from her shoe. She explained the shop threat. Tín asked whether the man had managed to finish. She said no. Tín criticized his preparation.

I told them about the captive workers, the locked doors, tankers, and Crown Wagon. Dad asked about the hospitality suite. Mom asked who searched the staff. Hòa wanted to know if the north staircase still had its old iron rail.

“You know it?” I asked.

“I've been places,” Hòa said.

“When?” I asked.

Hòa turned the radio lower. “Before weather was commercial.”

Tín smiled into his cup and said nothing. I explained Irene's private briefing, the guest diversion, and the truck seizure. Dad listened, then reminded me that the family had tickets.

“Stay home,” I said.

Mom put her shoe back on. “He sent a man to my shop. Home seems to be part of his route.”

“We'll inspect the rooms,” Dad said. “Your mother isn't sitting behind a locked exit.”

Tín leaned forward. “If I dislike the room, I'll add a door.”

Dad suggested using the doors already present. Tín pointed at him, delighted.

“Careful nigga,” Tín said. “Your first house got a new door because you lost the key.”

“The wall was damaged,” Dad replied.

“After you arrived,” Tín said.

Mom laughed. Dad warned me with his eyes not to join her. I failed. Tín turned to me at once.

“Seven bedrooms,” Tín said, “and nowhere comfortable to sleep. You don't get to look pleased.”

My smile went. Hòa offered Tín fruit. Mom stared into her shoe. Dad discovered something useful about the radio.

“Shawna still comes here,” Tín told me.

“Of course,” I said.

“I like her. Finishes a sentence without giving herself a medal,” Tín said.

I looked at the medal on his chest. Tín caught me.

“I earned years,” Tín said. “You're collecting afternoons.”

I sat with that while Mom asked about the green noodles. Hòa looked at the radio as though it might provide representation. I left them to the argument and went to Rafa's garage.

The crew stood around the Argent. Tess adjusted the seat. Rafa lay underneath on a mat. Hậu wore his sling and held ice against his shoulder, glaring at it as though frozen water had won unfairly. Trinh had brought the black machine case from the Pearl. Saint stood beside it with both hands clasped behind his back.

“Untouched,” Saint announced.

“An excellent opening position,” I said.

Khải arrived with Irene and Nessa. Eddie and Louis followed, looking better for sleep, food, and doors that opened. Irene put the course book on Rafa's bench.

“Thành signed,” Irene said. “Duval supposedly acted without his knowledge.”

“Very productive ignorance,” Trinh observed.

Celeste and three other entrants believed Mick. The remainder wanted to see the wagon and the gates. We would give them both.

I opened the machine case. Screen, controls, and the dense black box holding the part nobody else could reproduce. The proof lived in a notebook I didn't take to dinners anymore, since strangers would ask whether it could help with taxes. The thing was extraordinary and looked less impressive than Saint's luggage.

I put the course map in the camera cradle and entered the destinations. Routes crossed and recrossed the screen faster than Saint could follow.

“It knows where we park?” Saint asked.

“It can find every permitted route under the stated conditions,” I said.

“So no,” Saint said.

“Parking is cultural,” I told him.

Khải stood beside me as I added the closed gates, the tanker schedule, the wagon's tunnel, our replacement crew, and the cleared exits. The machine handled the impossible search. It didn't decide whether Thành would honor his own map. That part required people who had seen him lie.

Tess pointed at a turn. “Too tight.”

“For the coupe,” I said.

Tess looked at the Argent. “For the car with its own time zone.”

“Lower apron instead,” I said.

Rafa rolled out from under the engine. “Three steps.”

“Broad ones,” I said.

“Cars count steps too,” Rafa informed me.

We argued until Tess could drive what we'd drawn. Nessa corrected the Crown Wagon's width, then its folded height. Khải added a service opening absent from Thành's plans. Eddie remembered a gate with a narrow inner frame. Louis marked the passage that flooded after wash crews finished. Hậu ate peanuts out of the little trophy marker until Trinh removed it from his reach.

By midnight the plan had become a sequence of things particular people could do. Thursday: seize the Crown Wagon at the private depot during its transfer. Tess and Rafa handled vehicles; Nessa identified modifications; I handled objections. Eddie and Louis would deliver it to Briar in the expected jackets and stay with the display cabin.

Irene would show the entrants the evidence and let them choose their roles. Ordinary guests would go to Aster House before the final stage. The family would inspect the hospitality wing and keep exits usable if Thành moved early. At Briar, our crew would take custody of the loaded prizes, open the trapped passages, and meet Meridian from both sides.

The Ducal remained the prize. I still wanted it. Finding something worse beneath the gamble hadn't erased that.

At the side door I thanked Khải. He said Irene needed the plans. I told him I meant it anyway.

Khải rested the rolled drawings on his shoulder. “I want you alive afterward.”

“On my list,” I said.

“Don't put your wife on the same list,” Khải said. “Winning a hotel won't answer that conversation.”

“She's told me,” I said.

“Good.” Khải touched my unbruised cheek. I leaned into his hand. For a moment neither of us turned it into a joke. Then he lowered it and left.

Behind me Saint played Mrs. Devlin's practice recording on his phone. Three notes came out before he sighed.

“Everybody expects so much,” Saint said.

Hậu threw the ice bag with his good hand. It struck the phone and knocked it neatly into the empty trophy bowl. Rafa looked up from the car.

“Shoulder's coming along,” Rafa said.

## Chapter 10: Heavyweight Hospitality

Hậu had added a third rule to the pool sign: NO BUSINESS IN THE WATER.

The other rules concerned diving and shoes. The new rule concerned two collectors who'd tried to renegotiate a debt on rehabilitation floats. One wanted interest. The other wanted the blue noodle. Hậu had removed both men by their collars and left the noodle neutral.

Hank's House occupied the old firehouse three streets behind the Pearl. It had opened as Bell Street Fight Club, but Saint printed HANK'S HOUSE on a jacket and the neighborhood adopted the error before Hậu could object. The old sign hung in his office. In the engine bay stood weights, a ring, and bags. The pool filled the rear room. Voss was in Fresno with three young fighters, sending daily complaints about a hotel gym that apparently considered a treadmill sufficient preparation for being punched.

I arrived with fruit and news that the Argent now ran without requiring Rafa to swear continuously. Hậu accepted the fruit with more interest.

Mara, the new coach, shouted corrections at two trainees working a bag. Red headband, folded arms, a stare that could stop somebody halfway through an excuse. Hậu introduced her as a woman who scared people properly.

“He means I make them finish,” Mara said.

Hậu nodded gravely. “Terrible woman.”

He showed me the recovery room he wanted to build next. People kept coming back, he told me. I said they owed fees. Some did, he agreed. Some simply came back. We stood at the glass watching the trainees until the front windows exploded.

Three men jumped through the broken frame. Two others used the door, demonstrating that the entire first decision had been optional. They wore work jackets and carried clubs. Long entered behind them, ducking only after his shoulder knocked the door closer off its screws.

Hậu put the fruit on reception. “Leave.”

Long looked at the room, then me. “You could have answered the message.”

“We did,” I said. “You disliked the answer.”

Mara began moving the trainees toward the side exit. A club man blocked it. Hậu crossed the room, lifted him beneath the arms, and threw him onto stacked mats. He landed belly-first, bounced, and lost the club into the padding.

“They leave,” Hậu told Long.

Long smiled and picked up reception.

The desk rose from one end, scattered papers, and flew across the engine bay toward the pool windows. I caught its edge. Momentum carried me sideways over a loose weight plate. My heel drove the plate across the floor with a shriek; we stopped a foot from the glass, desk against my chest, appointment cards floating past my face.

Fruit rolled everywhere. Long put a shoe on an orange.

“You're paying,” Hậu said.

Long crushed it. Hậu punched him.

The first blow turned Long's head. The second struck his ribs. Hậu wrapped both arms around his waist and drove him back two steps. On the third Long planted his feet, hammered both hands into Hậu's back, and raised a knee. Hậu staggered but kept hold.

A club swung at me as I set down the desk. I caught the arm, punched its owner in the chest, and sent him onto a bench. The bench slid into a second man's shins. As that man pitched forward I took his collar and drove him into the heavy bag. The chain snapped. Bag and man struck the floor together, and the ceiling hook followed a moment later like a late contribution.

Mara shouted the last trainee through the exit. Long turned toward her voice. Hậu hooked his leg behind Long's knee and drove him into the ring steps. Metal crumpled. Long went down on one hand.

Hậu stepped back, breathing hard. “Missed a payment.”

Long rose with blood at his mouth. He touched it, looked at his thumb, and hit Hậu so hard the space to the pool disappeared beneath him.

Hậu went through the glass. He hit wet tile, rolled, and struck the pool edge with his shoulder. His left arm stopped moving with the rest of him.

I reached Long while his fist was still coming back. I caught the wrist, turned beneath it, and drove my elbow into his jaw. I hit him again; he staggered into the ring post, bending it toward the ropes.

Long seized my jacket and head-butted me. The lights widened. Blood filled my mouth. He pulled for a second blow; I planted my palm against his face, pushed his head away, and drove my knee into his stomach. My lapel tore as I broke free.

Hậu was standing in the pool room. His shoulder sat wrong. He saw my glance and shook his head angrily.

“People?” Hậu called.

“Out,” Mara shouted from the exit.

Long walked through the broken window frame, leaving a strip of sleeve on the glass. “The car.”

“Develop another interest,” I told him.

He swung. I stepped inside and drove us into the tiled wall. Tiles burst behind his back. His hand caught my hair; his fist hit beneath my ribs. I lost the breath I'd been saving for the next insult.

I put one foot against the wall and pushed. We fell into the pool together.

Cold water shut over my head. Long kept hold of my jacket. I twisted, caught the ladder, and pulled myself above him. He came up headfirst into my chest. We broke the surface, choking. He drove me into the tiled edge and reached for my throat.

I caught his wrists. My feet slid on the pool floor. We stood chest-deep, arms shaking, his face close enough for me to see diluted blood stream from his nose.

Above him Hậu appeared with the skimmer pole in his good hand. He hooked it under Long's chin and pulled. Long's head jerked back. I planted both feet on the wall and drove him across the water.

His back struck the opposite edge. The aluminum pole bent around his shoulders. Hậu let go before it pulled him in. I climbed out, coughing, and took the weight bench beside the changing-room door.

Long hauled himself up opposite me. He looked at Hậu's dangling arm, then at the bench.

“You'll need that shoulder,” Long told Hậu.

“You'll need new fucking teeth,” Hậu answered.

I threw the bench. Long caught it against his chest, but catching didn't stop it. The impact drove him through the changing-room door and into the lockers. Their little vents flattened around his back. I followed until one of his men raised a pistol at the corridor's far end.

I hit the light switch and dropped sideways. The shot shattered a tile beside my ear. By the time I reached the corner, Long and his men were through the service exit.

Their van accelerated away down the alley. I picked up a broken masonry block and threw it through the rear door. The van swerved at the intersection, struck the signal post beside the vintage trolley line, and fled around the corner as the post fell across the rails.

The trolley was already coming. I ran.

Brakes screamed. Its leading wheel climbed the fallen post. The carriage tilted toward three people beside a bench, too close and too still.

Dad emerged from the hardware shop carrying a little bag. He set it on the bench, put both hands beneath the trolley windows, and stopped the carriage from falling.

Its wheels spun in the air. A woman inside pressed her palms over her mouth. Dad shifted one foot, lifted the whole carriage enough to clear the post, and set it back on the rails. The landing sounded like every toolbox in Bell Street being dropped together.

“Back from the edge,” Dad told the three people. They obeyed.

I reached him as he retrieved his bag. Dad looked at my wet clothes, blood, and absent lapel.

“Swimming?” Dad asked.

“Unexpectedly,” I said.

“Hậu?” Dad asked.

“Shoulder,” I said.

Dad handed me the hardware. “Take those to your mother.” He headed for the gym before I could explain anything else.

The trolley driver shouted thanks. Dad looked back and pointed at the signal post. “Call somebody about that.”

By evening the street believed Dad had lifted public transportation because he disliked the timetable. Hậu's shoulder had been put back, the windows had boards over them, and Mara had kept the trainees at a café while refusing all requests to return for pictures. Trinh paid the glazier before he finished measuring.

Hậu and I sat in his office. He ate one-handed, looking through the glass at his damaged gym.

“Pool works,” Hậu said.

“We'll mention it in the review,” I said.

Hậu put down his fork. “I'm going to hurt him.”

“We'll find time,” I said.

Khải texted asking if I was all right. I typed yes, saw the dried blood on my sleeve, and changed it to my actual location. Then I called Shawna and told her where I was too.

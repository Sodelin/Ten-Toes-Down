## Chapter 14: A Door That Closes Properly

At home Shawna removed her earrings, put them in the dish on her dressing table, and unclasped her necklace. I stood by the bedroom door while she finished. Downstairs Valerie moved the last garment cases into the workroom.

“Close it,” Shawna said.

I closed the door. She met my eyes in the mirror.

“How long?” Shawna asked.

“Since Rusk's party,” I said.

Shawna turned on the stool. “You understood that very quickly.”

“Yes,” I said.

“The first night at the Pearl,” Shawna said.

“I was with him,” I said.

“And last night,” Shawna said.

“Yes,” I said.

She moved to the edge of the bed. For a while her face barely changed. Then she laughed once, without amusement.

“You brought his shirt here,” Shawna said. “I washed it.”

I said I knew. She looked at me sharply.

“Stop presenting awareness as a favor,” Shawna said. “I'm not thanking you for knowing where you put the laundry.”

I leaned against the door because standing in the middle of the room felt like asking her to make space for me. “I'm sorry.”

“For which part?” Shawna asked.

“Sleeping with him. Lying. Letting you believe I was somewhere else,” I said.

“You made Trinh answer questions,” Shawna said.

“I didn't ask her to lie,” I said.

“You made her choose between correcting her employer and confusing his wife. A marvelous improvement,” Shawna said.

I stopped trying to find a smaller description. Shawna stood and went to the window. Garden lights shone beneath us; the open piano-room door reflected in the glass.

“Who knows?” Shawna asked. “Saint?”

“Some,” I said.

“Your parents?” Shawna asked.

“No,” I said.

“Did Irene arrange this?” Shawna asked.

“No,” I said.

“Did he?” Shawna asked.

“I kissed him first,” I said. “I wanted him. We both chose it.”

Shawna turned back. “There you are. A sentence with you doing something.”

Her jaw tightened. She looked away, swallowed, and asked whether I wanted to remain married to her.

“Yes,” I said.

“Then what did you expect the marriage to do while you weren't using it?” Shawna asked.

“I wasn't thinking properly,” I said.

“You thought enough to name a room,” Shawna said. “You thought about who'd be there, what I'd ask, which name would sound ordinary. Don't suddenly become stupid because I'm asking you to explain.”

I looked down, then made myself look back. “You're right.”

“I know,” Shawna said. “It hasn't improved the evening.”

She took a tissue and pressed it beneath one eye. Her makeup had lasted through the entire preview. A dark mark spread into the paper. I wanted to cross the room and hold her. I stayed by the door.

“Was there something you wanted to tell me before him?” Shawna asked. “About men. About yourself. A conversation I somehow missed while sleeping beside you.”

“I hadn't had it with everyone else,” I said.

“Fine. Smaller guest list,” Shawna said. “Answer.”

I took a breath. “I didn't expect to want him. Then I did. I still wanted you. I still do.”

She shut her eyes for a moment. “You understand that doesn't compliment me.”

“Yes,” I said.

“I wasn't competing for whatever attention survived your surprise,” Shawna said.

“I didn't think of it that way,” I said.

“You were getting all the good parts,” Shawna said. She put the tissue down with unnecessary care.

The house had grown quiet. I heard Valerie close a downstairs door and walk toward the service hall. Our bedroom was the same size it had been that morning. Nothing in it offered a place where this conversation would be easier.

Shawna asked whether we'd talked about her.

“Yes,” I said.

“Was I cold? Too busy? In love with work? Which convenient wife did you give him?” Shawna asked.

“None. I said you're good to me. That I love you. That you're talented and funny and stubborn,” I said.

Shawna watched my face. “Then he knew.”

“He knew I was married,” I said.

Her eyes went to my ring. “Subtle clue. Did you remove it?”

“Not to pretend I wasn't,” I said.

“That answer has a lot of architecture,” Shawna said.

I stopped. “I took it off to wash my hands. Left it on the table. Put it back afterward.”

“Congratulations on finishing a laundry cycle,” Shawna said.

The joke sounded exactly like her. I hadn't lost the woman who could make me laugh. I'd given her reasons to turn the same precision on me.

“I don't want to lose you,” I said.

She looked at me for a long time. “That thought should have gone to the apartment with you.”

There was nothing I could put beside that. Shawna went to the wardrobe and took out a clean shirt, trousers, and the small bag I used for short trips. My shoulders straightened before I could stop them.

“You want me to leave?” I asked.

“This room, tonight,” Shawna said. “Guest room. I need to work tomorrow without you following me through the house explaining your latest discovery.”

“All right,” I said.

Shawna set the bag on the chair. “And you don't buy something to fix it.”

I started to say I wouldn't. She pointed at the wall beyond which the piano occupied most of a room. I closed my mouth.

“No gifts,” I said.

“No dramatic announcements. No new men outside my door because you've decided feeling guilty is a security assignment. The existing security stays because somebody attacked my collection. Your guilt doesn't get a uniform,” Shawna said.

“I understand,” I told her.

“You'll have time to demonstrate that,” Shawna said.

She handed me the bag. I held it between us.

“The show was beautiful,” I said.

For a second her mouth trembled. She pressed her lips together and nodded.

“It was,” Shawna said.

“You were—” I began.

“Don't put this conversation inside that one,” Shawna told me.

I said I was sorry again. She said she'd heard. I went into the hall. The latch closed behind me with an ordinary, precise sound.

I'd slept in the guest room once after a fever, when Shawna had finally threatened to move me herself if I kept insisting I could work while sweating through the sheets. She'd brought soup and mocked me from the doorway until I'd stopped arguing and eaten. Tonight I set my bag on the same chair. The doorway stayed empty.

I called Khải. He answered at once and asked about the show.

“It went well,” I said. “She saw the photograph.”

He was quiet. Then he asked whether I meant the one he'd sent.

“Shared library,” I explained. “She asked. I told her.”

“All of it?” Khải asked.

“Yes,” I said.

“Because she found it,” Khải said.

“Yes,” I said.

He didn't congratulate me for finally reaching the conversation. He asked whether Shawna was all right. I said no. He asked if I was still at the house.

“Guest room,” I said.

“Stay there,” Khải told me.

“I wasn't asking to come over,” I said.

“I'm telling myself too,” Khải said.

Garden light drew thin bars across the ceiling. I told him I'd admitted wanting him and loving her. He sounded tired when he answered, his quick humor stripped away.

“Both can be true,” Khải said. “You don't have to make one of us a mistake in your arithmetic. You do have to decide what you're doing.”

I began saying I knew and stopped halfway through. Khải let the unfinished word remain unfinished. We ended the call without plans.

Downstairs Shawna spoke to Denise. I couldn't hear the words, only the change in her voice when someone who loved her answered. I stayed in the guest room and let the conversation belong to them.

Before six Valerie found me in the kitchen with untouched coffee and my bag beside the chair. Shawna wanted breakfast in the workroom, Valerie said. I could use the terrace.

“I can make it myself,” I offered.

“I'm aware,” Valerie said. She took down a plate. “Photographer at ten. For the collection.”

“I'll be gone,” I said.

“That will help the schedule,” Valerie replied, perfectly professional. We'd known one another two years. Her courtesy made those years more visible.

At Bell Street, Denise was opening Bishop's. She saw me through the glass, unlocked the door, and pointed at a chair in the back. I sat. She set down coffee and remained opposite me.

“You've been busy,” Denise said.

“Shawna called,” I said.

“She's my friend,” Denise told me.

I started the familiar I know. Denise looked at me until I stopped.

“She needs breakfast and somewhere to talk,” Denise said. “She gets both here. You come another hour.”

“I'm not asking you to fix it,” I said.

Denise pulled out the chair. “Good. I run a kitchen. Husband repairs would put us out of business.”

I stared into the coffee. “I've made a mess.”

“Stop walking it into everyone else's house,” Denise said.

She let me drink without answering another question. When she stood, she left a small bag by my cup. Two warm rolls inside.

“Eat. You look as if somebody hit you with a refrigerator,” Denise told me.

“A bench, mostly,” I said.

“Your father would've used the refrigerator,” Denise said.

I laughed once despite myself. Denise shook her head and returned to the kitchen. I ate, finished the cold coffee, and left before Shawna came.

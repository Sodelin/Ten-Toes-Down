Chapter 25: Everybody Brought Something

The emergency room gave Minh a plastic bracelet and a bill before a doctor had touched him. He pointed out the order of operations to the woman at the desk.

“That's why it's still small,” she told him, taking the clipboard back.

Mai laughed hard enough to need a waiting-room chair.

Inside, the doctor pressed around Minh's shoulder, asked him to turn his head, and looked at the cuts across his palms with growing uncertainty. He wanted to know what had fallen on him.

“Part of a ship,” Minh said.

The doctor asked which part. Minh said the part that opened. Mai nodded when the doctor looked at her.

“Accurate,” Mai confirmed. “Unusual, I know.”

They cleaned his hands and closed the worst wounds. The shoulder pictures showed no fracture. The doctor examined them twice, then asked Minh to raise his arm. Minh raised it and admitted it hurt.

“Stop doing it,” the doctor advised.

Mai wanted that written down. Minh lowered his arm and looked at the wall until she stopped smiling.

Outside, Leon had persuaded a vending attendant to refund crackers he had already eaten. Duy slept upright beneath a sailing magazine. Tú had told three strangers they were waiting for Master Minh, and all three had moved away. Hương stood when the examination door opened and said home. Nobody offered another destination.

The following morning, a hospital bed arrived through the garage's roller door. It had electric controls, polished wood at the foot, and Bách's card offering comfort to a giant among men. Mai corrected the spelling of giant. Leon defended the size of the bed as adequate evidence of intent.

Ten minutes later, Moss arrived with a massage chair and a man to explain its controls. Bách's driver remained to defend the bed. The chair man listed a heated back. The driver answered with wheels. Three massage programs met the possibility of urinating without getting up.

Mai put down her coffee. “Stop selling my fiancé a lifestyle.”

Minh sat in the chair. Its attendant switched on the rollers. The padded back began working against his shoulders, and he closed his eyes with an involuntary sound of pleasure. Moss smiled at Bách's driver. The driver called his employer.

Leon lay on the hospital bed and raised its headrest. “Tell him this one's also doing numbers,” he called.

Duy arrived with sweet rolls and Dante's trousers on a hanger. The trousers were pressed, cleaned, and tagged. He wanted them returned so Dante could not claim he owed him anything.

“You owe him a beating,” Minh said from the chair.

“Had the pockets checked,” Duy replied, setting the hanger against the wall.

Mai accepted a roll and pointed him toward the stool beside Minh. Duy sat with the bag in his hands. For a while he watched the chair knead Minh's shoulders and asked what one cost.

“Try being shot at by a millionaire,” Minh said. “They arrive.”

Duy folded the bag's rim, unfolded it, and said Carter wanted him back tomorrow with the lawyer. Minh told him to go. Duy said he intended to; he wanted Minh to know.

Outside, someone tested the bed's wheels on the driveway. Mai informed them it was not a skateboard. Duy looked toward the door, then down at his hands.

“Thought if I came out with money, I'd stop being the man who borrowed it,” Duy said.

Minh opened his eyes. “Then you gambled the Cadillac.”

“After,” Duy said.

“Strong system,” Minh replied.

Duy tried a smile and failed to keep it. Minh let the chair hum for a few seconds before telling him he had driven well at the yard. Duy said he knew, then apologized when Minh told him not to ruin the compliment.

“At the pier too,” Minh added.

Duy nodded more carefully.

Moss needed somebody who could drive the recovery truck without folding his fence. He had asked Minh. Duy looked up from the bag and asked whether it was a real job.

“Mostly,” Minh said. “You live here, Duy.”

Mai came through the roller door. She had already established Monday as the start and wanted to enjoy four days before hearing about the first borrowed wages. Duy asked why Friday. Mai told him she had answered that.

He folded the empty bag flat and stood. “I'll do it.”

Minh offered his wrapped fist. Duy touched it gently with his own. As he reached for the trousers, Mai said Dante's lawyer was coming by and could take them. Duy looked relieved.

“He still doesn't like me,” Duy said.

Minh glanced at the hanger. “You left that car wash in his trousers. Complicated friendship.”

The lawyer arrived with cigars, wine, and a handwritten note. Mai read the note before passing it to Minh. Dante thanked them for letting him leave the television station alive, apologized for the flowers and dress-shop attack, noted that his lawyer discouraged discussing details in writing, and said he had ignored that advice because the lawyer had never met Mai.

“Learning,” Minh said, returning it.

The lawyer asked whether his client could do anything else. Mai gave him a folder. Three window estimates, one roof estimate, a letter for Felicia's landlord establishing whom the armed men had actually come to threaten, and a request for the yellow stone's owner's name. Two people had already called pretending it belonged to them.

The lawyer opened the folder and reviewed it. Mai added the trousers. He accepted the hanger with the uncertain composure of a man whose fees had not prepared him for every form of restitution.

“I see,” the lawyer said.

Leon lifted his head from the bed. “No, you don't. Be thankful.”

When the sedan left, Moss opened the cigars and declared them excellent. Minh told him to distribute them among the guests outside. Mai took the wine upstairs before Leon could assign it medical properties.

That afternoon she drove Minh to the printshop. At three consecutive stops, he looked along the scratched wing of the Rolls. Mai caught him at the third.

“It'll get fixed,” she said.

“Liked that paint,” Minh answered.

Mai looked at him. “Liked the street without men firing at it.”

He nodded. “Paint loses.”

Her assistant had kept the presses moving. Labeled boxes held invitations; menus waited under a metal weight. The shop smelled of warm machinery, paper, and lunch somebody had hidden behind the counter. Minh stood in the doorway enjoying a business that continued without armed supervision.

Felicia came in carrying the gray jacket between two fingers. She wanted the identity of whoever put cash in it. Minh raised a bandaged hand. She then wanted the identity of whoever put fish in it.

Mai examined the ceiling. Minh said they had been on a boat.

“I know where fish live,” Felicia told him. She opened the lining and displayed a strip of dried bait caught in the lower seam.

“Leon sat on something,” Minh said.

“Leon sits on everything,” Felicia replied. “That's not a description.”

She bagged the jacket and issued instructions for the wedding suit. Minh would come to her shop on the morning. She would put it on him. He would go straight to the ceremony. If someone waved a gun, he would explain that he was dressed.

Minh asked what happened if the man was rude. Felicia said Mai could handle it; her dress had a train.

Mai leaned against the counter laughing. Minh observed that supervision at this standard was expensive. Felicia reminded him he had received a neighborhood discount.

They inspected the empty storefront next door after that. Its former office-furniture tenant had left two desks, a broken filing cabinet, and a sailboat picture nobody liked. The landlord met them with three keys and a smile whose size worried Minh before the price arrived.

Mai and Felicia walked the space, arguing about fitting-room walls and daylight. Felicia's clients would be undressing. Mai pointed out that this strengthened her case for the dividing wall she had already suggested.

Minh moved the filing cabinet. A water stain ran down behind it. The landlord called it old damage. Minh pressed a finger against the plaster. Water came out around his knuckle.

“Interesting weather,” Minh said.

The rent came down. The landlord then named a deposit, an alteration deposit, and a fee for the keys. Minh asked how much one key cost. On hearing the number, he bent it at a right angle between thumb and forefinger and put it on the desk.

“Defective,” Minh said.

Mai covered her face. Felicia turned toward the window. The landlord looked at the two remaining keys and decided they were included.

Minh put his checkbook in front of Mai and invited the women to choose their wall. Mai reminded him she would pay rent through her business and Felicia would pay her share. He said he knew. Felicia said he could buy the good mirrors.

“How many?” Minh asked.

“Now you're talking like a fiancé,” Felicia said.

On the way home, Mai stopped under a narrow restaurant's red awning. Minh leaned forward, surprised it remained open. New owner, she told him; same cook.

She came around and opened his door before he could do it himself. Minh said people would talk. Mai reminded him they already had a film.

Inside, dark blue vinyl covered the old booths. The jukebox still stood by the bathroom with tape on its lower panel. Two teenagers shared fries at the counter, and the wall clock remained crooked in exactly the way Minh remembered.

Mai took the window booth. He sat opposite with his bandaged hands on the table.

“Remember this one?” Mai asked.

“Every second,” Minh said.

“Terrible burden. You said stupid things.” Mai unfolded her napkin.

“Twenty-three,” Minh reminded her.

“For a whole year,” Mai said. “You had time to practice.”

The cook came out with plates. He was heavier now, but the gold tooth appeared in the same place when he smiled. Mai told him they were getting married. He looked at Minh's hands and asked whether she had made him ask twice.

Minh laughed. The cook set the plates down without an order. Four years earlier, he had done exactly that after one expensive dinner had failed to fill either of them.

A police car came through the gate before they could choose a test target. Carter got out, shut the door, and crossed the yard slowly. His gaze traveled over the tracks, the blade, the cannon, the gold dollar signs, then stopped on Bách.

“Yours?” Carter asked.

Bách looked at Minh. Minh looked back. Leon explained that a family discussion was in progress.

“Why is it running?” Carter asked Minh.

“Because it wasn't,” Minh said.

Carter took a folded coronation flyer from his pocket. His lieutenant had received one. Somebody was using Minh's name to recruit armed men for Pier Nine that night. One prospective employee had asked whether police would be there before agreeing to carry a gun.

Moss took a small step away from the truck bearing his company name.

“They'll be expecting you,” Carter told Minh. “Some of the men will be mine.”

Minh folded the flyer along Carter's crease. The printed offer of donations had now developed into an employment agency. He handed it back.

“Someone's arranging my schedule again,” Minh said.

Carter looked at the tractor. “That stays off public streets.”

Leon raised a hand to ask what happened if a street became private while they were using it. Carter turned toward him. Leon lowered the hand and classified the question as academic.

After the police car left, Moss walked toward his own car. Minh asked whether he wanted the recovery truck back. Moss stopped with a hand on his door.

“My name's on the registration,” Moss said.

Bách leaned against the blade, delighted by the difficulty. “Spell it clearly. People will want to know who contributed.”

Minh went to the recovery truck. “I can take the container without it,” he said. “This saves dragging it five blocks.”

Moss asked if he really could. Minh lifted the truck's front end until both tires cleared the ground. He held it while Moss looked beneath them. Leon got his phone out and asked him not to move, needing evidence for his own mechanic.

When Minh put the truck down, Moss came back across the yard and placed its keys in his palm.

“Keep it,” Moss said. “Wedding present.”

Bách's amusement disappeared. He looked at the keys, at the truck, at Minh, and announced that the tractor was theirs too. Minh thanked both men without interrupting the competition.

Tú looked toward the shed. “We should show people broken things more often.”

That evening, Minh came upstairs with oil under his nails and two sets of keys he had not owned at breakfast. Mai sat on the bed sorting envelopes. He told her about the tractor.

“Small?” Mai asked.

Minh shook his head.

“We have to stop inviting people,” Mai said, setting down the envelopes.

He scrubbed at the basin while she watched through the bathroom doorway. She inspected the glass cut when he finished. Most of it had closed. She touched beside the remaining red line and asked if it hurt.

“When you press it,” Minh said.

She pressed again lightly. He caught her hand and kissed her wrist.

“I like proof you're still in there,” Mai said.

He drew her close. She rested both hands on his shoulders and looked at him until he stopped trying to turn the examination into a kiss.

“You like this,” Mai said. “The gifts. Money. Leon pretending he has a royal appointment.”

“He appointed himself,” Minh said.

Mai waited. Downstairs, the recovered cars occupied space among appliances and envelopes worth more than a year of repair work. Minh liked being asked, liked opening cases, liked the keys still pressing against his pocket.

“Yes,” he told her.

“Good.” Mai took one of the keys from his hand. “I don't want you taking all this and pretending somebody forced you.”

She told him what she wanted: the empty shop next to hers, a bigger fitting space for Felicia, room behind it for another press. She would pay the business expenses from the business. She wanted him to know where part of their growing good fortune would go.

“Then take it,” Minh said.

“I was going to,” Mai replied, and pulled him toward the bedroom. “First wash the rest of you. At the moment you smell like a bridge.”

Later, with Mai asleep, Minh sat by the open window and called Duy. Leon answered from his apartment, reporting that Duy had only just stopped talking about the sailor's cap. Minh told him about Carter's warning.

The television went quiet at Leon's end. He asked if they were still going to the yard tonight.

“No,” Minh said. “Wake Duy in the morning. I want the roads. We take the container when they move it.”

Leon was silent for two seconds. Then Minh heard a cupboard open.

“I'm putting my other shoes out,” Leon said.

Chapter 13: Bell Street Eats First

Duy showed Minh four roads out of Pier Nine before breakfast. By the time they sat in a diner booth overlooking the harbor road, he had described the weight limits, bad turns, and drivers likely to ignore both. He paid with a twenty from Dante's trousers.

“Still got his money?” Minh asked.

“He tried to kill me,” Duy said. “I thought the pockets were fair.”

A truck crawled around the junction outside. Its rear tires climbed the curb before the driver straightened the trailer and took it under a low bridge. Duy explained that heavier loads used that route past the refrigeration plant. Rook might ignore signs, but he respected the cost of an axle.

Minh watched the trailer clear the turn. “There.”

Duy put down his cup. “With cars behind it?”

“We'll be those cars,” Minh said.

Duy studied him, then the road. “You're smiling.”

Minh took another bite. “Nice road.”

Back on Bell Street, Leon had turned the first motorcycle bay into a kitchen. He stood at a smoking grill in an apron reading KISS THE MANAGEMENT, explaining into his phone that he could not help somebody move because he had responsibilities.

Minh looked from the grill to the repair bays. Leon pointed out that all the fuel cans had been moved outside. Mai's NO SMOKING sign watched them from the rear door.

From upstairs, Mai called for the grill to go farther out. Minh lifted it by both handles and carried it to the sidewalk. Leon walked alongside with a nearly finished piece of meat held in his tongs, asking him not to tilt the business.

Hương brought rice, pickles, and a pot nobody was allowed to open. Bích followed with herbs under damp towels and corrected every bowl's position. Felicia brought tables and cornbread, calling it payment for the emotional damage outside her shop. Leon objected that they ought to be feeding her. She had tasted his marinade and was protecting herself.

The barber installed Minh on the donated sofa. Reggie positioned the camera while a towel went around Minh's collar. Mai told him to sit still and have his head repaired.

“What was wrong with it?” Minh asked.

“You cut it yourself,” Mai said.

“I did,” Minh replied.

“Mystery solved.” Mai set plates on the table and left him under the scissors.

A young man from the basketball court stopped to ask whether this was the man who lifted the van, then asked about Al's dog. Minh explained that Al had brought the dog into the conversation himself. The young man said his cousin had heard the call and that Al began agreeing before Minh finished speaking.

“Al can be efficient,” Minh told him through the mirror.

The young man laughed and asked who had taught Minh to talk like that. Leon lifted his tongs and claimed a ten-year warranty. Asked whether he had given Minh a pass, Leon replied that the man had keys to his house and the vocabulary had arrived with the couch.

Minh watched the young man in the barber's mirror. “You need a pass to eat, or you just standing there?”

The man bent laughing. Leon pointed him toward the plates before he could become another person requiring supervision.

Tú arrived with his mother and attempted to disappear behind the grill. She caught his sleeve. He had told her he had employment in management. She wanted to meet the employer.

Minh, trapped beneath the barber's towel, received Tú's silent appeal in the mirror.

“He carried things,” Minh said.

“Does he get paid?” his mother asked.

Minh took two hundred dollars from his pocket. Tú's eyes widened. His mother collected the bills before he folded them, explaining that he owed her for the shirts. She carried her covered dish toward Hương while Tú watched his first wages become a household matter.

Leon patted his shoulder with the hand not holding the tongs. “Business, Master.”

Tín and Hòa came on foot. Tín put a small wooden box in Minh's lap as the barber finished the back. Inside lay gold cufflinks with dark stones in their centers.

“They were mine,” Tín said.

Minh lifted one carefully. “You're sure?”

“I wore them. I remember.” Tín watched Mai kiss his cheek and smiled at her over Minh's bowed head.

When the towel came off, Minh rose and bent toward the old man in thanks. Tín put a hand on the back of his neck. The bend continued farther than Minh had intended. He tried straightening; Tín's hand stayed, holding his forehead close to the box.

“Don't lose them,” Tín murmured.

Then he let go. Minh straightened slowly while Leon laughed into his apron. Hòa told Minh to take the box upstairs and come back to eat. Minh did as he was told, leaving his question about the old man's hand where it usually ended up.

The first shot came as Leon lifted the grill lid.

The lid jumped. Leon dropped it. A red line opened along his upper arm, and Minh caught him before he fell against the coals.

“Down!” Minh shouted.

Mai pulled Tú's mother through the garage entrance. Hòa put himself in front of Bích and guided her behind the refrigerator. Tín took the heavy pot from Hương so she could move. A second shot struck brick above the shop.

Minh put Leon behind the engine stand. Leon clamped a hand over his arm and protested that staying there would not interfere with any plans to go jogging.

The rifle had fired from the liquor-store roof. Minh crossed low behind the parked cars, reached the delivery truck at the corner, and jumped onto its roof. Before the sheet metal finished flexing, he sprang across the alley and caught the parapet with both hands.

The gunman turned as Minh pulled himself up. A shot passed Minh's hip. He caught the barrel, drove it up through the man's grip, and hit his jaw with the stock. Teeth scattered over tar. Minh broke the rifle against the concrete edge and dropped both halves.

The man reached inside his jacket. Minh punched him in the chest. He flew into the rooftop water tank and dropped beneath it. The tank rang, rocked, and began folding one support. Minh caught that support, pushed the weight upright, and seated its bent leg on the block below it before turning toward the second man running for the stairs.

Minh reached the doorway first. The man stopped.

“Who?” Minh asked.

“Rook,” the man said immediately.

Minh took him by his jacket and brought him downstairs. Inside the garage, Mai held a towel against Leon's wound while Felicia cut back the ruined sleeve. Leon asked whether it looked bad. Felicia said the arm could stay, but the shirt was finished.

Reggie stood in the entrance with his camera down. A thin cut marked his cheek. His hands shook around the handle. Minh placed the captive beside the motorcycle lift and watched Reggie look toward Leon.

“He all right?” Reggie asked.

“I'm fucking hungry,” Leon answered.

Reggie started to raise the camera, stopped, and left it hanging against his chest.

Carter arrived before the ambulance. He called another unit for the man on the floor, then crouched beside Leon. Leon insisted that somebody turn the meat before he would consider leaving. Hương promised, which did more than the officer's argument.

The street supplied witnesses in such quantity that Carter had to ask them to take turns. The barber complained that his client had left before the front was finished. Minh stood beside Mai while the medics wrapped Leon's arm. She found his hand.

“Rook?” Mai asked.

Minh nodded.

She looked at the hole in the brick above their door. “Stop waiting for his money.”

Minh watched Leon argue for the right to walk to the ambulance. “I am.”

Across the road, Dante stood half concealed by the phone booth. He raised empty hands when Minh saw him. Minh crossed toward him. For once, Dante did not run.

The container would move tomorrow morning, Dante said. His father had filled the yard with men and wanted him dead too. He knew the truck and could point it out.

“You sent guns to my home,” Minh told him. “You tied Duy in that car.”

Dante swallowed and nodded. There was no useful answer he could put between them.

Minh asked for the diamonds. Rook's men had taken the case from Dante's apartment. Dante had escaped through a bathroom window.

“You hid it in an apartment he pays for?” Minh asked.

“I moved the furniture,” Dante said, then shut his eyes briefly. “That sounded better before.”

Behind Minh, Tín set Hương's pot back on the table and began feeding those who remained. Mai carried Leon's clean jacket toward the medics. Reggie kept his camera lowered.

Minh faced Dante again. “Tell me about the truck,” he said. “Then ask Leon if you can eat.”

## Chapter 24: Keep the Receipt

Rook fired from twelve feet. Minh stepped behind the bridge's steel door. The shot broke its square window and scattered glass across the empty wheelhouse. Minh caught the outer edge and shoved it into Rook's gun arm, trapping the wrist against the rail.

The pistol fell into the harbor. Rook pulled free quickly enough to save the elbow and struck Minh's injured shoulder with stiff fingers. He drove a knee into Minh's stomach before stepping into another punch.

Minh grunted. Rook still moved like a man accustomed to hitting people himself when payment was insufficient. His polished shoes gripped the wet deck. His jacket tore beneath one arm as he came forward.

“Should've taken ten million,” Rook said.

Minh caught his fist. The bandage darkened where their hands met. Rook tried pulling back, then hit toward Minh's ear with his free hand. Minh turned his head; the blow glanced across his cheek.

“Should've offered it earlier,” Minh said.

Rook tried the price again. Minh said he already had eight. Rook explained that eight was not ten.

“Good at subtraction, Calvin,” Minh told him.

He turned Rook's fist down until the man bent at the waist, released him, and hit him in the chest.

Rook flew through the wheelhouse entrance. His shoulders struck the captain's chair and tore its base from the floor. Chair and man traveled into the console. Its frame folded around his hips. The ship's horn began sounding because one polished shoe had landed on the button.

Minh came inside and lifted the shoe away.

“Enough announcements,” Minh said.

Electrical ticking replaced the horn. Rook tried one armrest, then the other, and stopped when the movement hurt his chest. He demanded help. Minh crouched and checked where the bent frame held him. Jacket and belt were caught. The steel had not entered his body.

“Stay,” Minh said.

Rook complained he couldn't breathe.

“Keep finding enough to negotiate,” Minh told him, and went for the cases.

The smaller case contained the auction jewelry without its display furniture: the broad necklace, watches, two packets of loose stones, a yellow diamond in its own compartment. Minh recognized the necklace from the catalog Mai had printed. He picked up the yellow stone between two bloody fingers.

“Yours?” Minh asked.

Rook said all of it was his. Minh reminded him that the guests' property had been missing. Rook explained consignment, watching the stone rather than Minh's face.

Minh wiped it on a clean part of his trousers and returned it to the compartment. “Guest with extra steps,” he said, shutting the case.

Through the broken window, he could see officers boarding from Moss's boat. Carter climbed after the first two uniforms, holding the rail with one hand and his hat with the other. Rook saw him as well.

“Ray won't understand,” Rook said.

“Talk slowly,” Minh suggested.

Rook asked whether Minh thought police would ignore the recovered money. Minh pointed to the million-dollar case beside the console and observed that there was some right there. Rook's expression changed. He stopped trying the chair and studied Minh instead.

“I know what you are,” Rook said. “Family dinners. Little wedding. You'll want another ship after this. Another street. Men standing when you enter. You won't fix motorcycles forever.”

Minh leaned against the doorway. Salt had dried on his lips. His shoulder throbbed. The heel-less shoe put his weight unevenly on the deck.

“Like fixing motorcycles,” Minh said.

“You like being the man who can afford to,” Rook replied.

Minh considered him. “Yeah.”

The answer seemed to bother Rook more than denial. Minh looked through the window toward Moss's blue boat.

“Like that one too,” Minh said. “Two engines.”

Rook told him Moss never gave anything away. Minh said neither did he; Moss would get something. Rook began explaining again how all of this could have been available through him.

“Calvin,” Minh said. “Look where you're sitting.”

Rook looked down despite himself. One arm of the chair had caught his watch chain. He freed the watch carefully and put it in his pocket, preserving what dignity could fit there.

Carter stopped in the doorway. He looked at Minh's shoulder, the wrecked console, and Rook imprisoned in the captain's seat.

“Stuck?” Carter asked.

“Yes,” Rook said.

“Asking the man who put you there.” Carter came closer and checked the frame around Rook's ribs. He told the officers to bring medical help and something for the chair.

One suggested bolt cutters. Carter said to start with optimism.

Minh took the bent armrests and pulled them apart. One broke at its mounting. The other straightened enough for Rook to move. Minh held it clear while Carter helped him stand. Rook's knees shook; he straightened his jacket over the evidence.

“Attorney,” Rook demanded.

“Excellent plan,” Carter said, turning him toward the wall.

Rook looked over his shoulder at Minh and predicted that Dante would come to his senses.

“He's twenty-nine,” Minh told him. “Still waiting, bring a chair.”

Carter glanced at the ruined one. “Different chair.”

An officer laughed, stopped under Rook's stare, then remembered which man's hands were behind his back and went on with the work.

Minh gave Carter the jewelry case and said Mai had the catalog and owners' list. Carter opened it, looked at the stones, and asked about the larger case.

“He took it from me,” Minh said.

“Comes with us tonight,” Carter replied.

Minh sighed. “You always arrive when the shopping's over.”

Carter told him to try an invitation before the shooting. Then he lowered his voice. All thirty-one workers were accounted for. Navarro's ankle looked ugly, but he was describing it to everybody within reach. Minh's people were clear.

Minh looked through the window. Mai stood on the lower deck beside Sơn with a clean cloth over one shoulder, speaking into the radio. She looked up. Minh raised his unwrapped hand. She counted his fingers from below and raised hers.

Carter followed his gaze and told him to let somebody examine the hands. Minh headed for the stairs.

Behind him, Rook said he hadn't forgotten his wife's grandfather.

Minh turned. “He remembers you every time his boat needs paint.”

Mai met him halfway down. She examined his face, then took the right hand palm-up. Fingers moved; nails stayed where they belonged; two knuckles had split. She requested the other. The cloth had begun white. Minh complimented Hòa's supplies.

“Dad packs for you like you're a natural disaster he likes,” Mai said.

Sơn stood three steps below them, concentrating painfully on the distant water. Mai told him he could turn around. He asked Minh whether he could climb down. Minh looked at him.

“Asking because she's here,” Sơn explained.

Mai approved the policy. They took the service ladder to Moss's boat. Minh went first. Mai declined to be carried, then put a foot on his shoulder when the ladder swung away from the deck.

“You said no carrying,” Minh reminded her.

“This is a step,” Mai replied.

Leon waited in the gray jacket. He had discovered the bundle from the saloon in its pocket and wanted to know whether it came with the suit.

“Felicia's jacket,” Mai said.

“Hell of a tailor,” Leon observed.

Minh held out his hand. Leon returned the bundle, explaining that he had only been checking the care instructions. Minh peeled off several notes for cleaning. Leon looked at them and offered to dry-clean the ocean.

The ride home took twenty minutes. At first everyone spoke at once. Reggie wanted confirmation that the camera caught the ramp. Tú insisted the foam cannon deserved more credit. Duy described backing the recovery truck between trailers without touching them; Sơn corrected him. Duy argued that touching was not hitting and found no supporters.

Beyond the breakwater, the water settled. So did the conversation. A harbor medic had dressed Minh's hands before they left. Mai put a towel behind his shoulder and gave him water, requiring a second drink after the first.

Leon watched from the opposite bench. “Marriage. Same instructions as the police, but they bought the cup.”

Mai pointed toward the bow. She kept pointing until Leon carried himself and his cleaning money there.

Minh leaned against the cabin. “Eight days.”

“I know,” Mai said.

“Still have me?” Minh asked.

She looked at his torn shirt and bandaged hands. “Currently discounted.”

“Buy now,” Minh said.

Mai kissed the unbruised side of his mouth. “Deposit paid.”

At the landing, Hương waited with three cars and enough food for a missing expedition. Tín held Minh's good shoes. Hòa had a clean shirt over his arm. Hương took one look and ordered the hospital first.

“Hungry,” Minh said.

“Eat in the car,” she told him.

Tín gave him the shoes and demanded a ride to the tailor tomorrow. He did not trust Minh's friends to explain sleeves. Mai helped Minh into Hòa's clean shirt while Leon climbed into the nearest car and opened a food container.

Tú tried sitting beside him. Leon said the seat belonged to rice. Hương put the rice in Leon's lap, ending the problem.

The first car pulled away with its windows open. Minh took Mai's hand as gently as the bandages permitted and followed her to the next one.

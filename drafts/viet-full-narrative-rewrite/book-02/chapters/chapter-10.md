## Chapter 10: Very Expensive Hospitality

At Harbor Crest the following evening, Minh found a canal where other restaurants put tables. A man in a striped shirt stood in a little boat with an oar, traveling twelve feet between a dock and customers who could almost reach it themselves.

Leon watched a tray of champagne complete its voyage. “Indoor traffic,” he said. “Rich people found a way.”

Mai took Minh's arm. She wore a blue dress and matching jacket, both subject to Felicia's instructions concerning red wine, grease, and combustion. Minh had barely looked at the boat when Mai told him not to break it.

“I've only seen it,” Minh said.

“You have a look,” Mai replied.

Behind them, Sơn gave their names to the hostess. He wore a black suit over his wrist brace and held the Rolls's keys. The hostess asked if he was driving for Minh now. When he said yes, she smiled and explained that her husband owed her fifty dollars.

“He bets too much,” Sơn said.

“Apparently,” she agreed, and led them in.

Rook had seated Minh between Bách and Eugene Moss, a heavy man in green velvet. Moss introduced himself by offering a forty-two-foot fishing boat. Leon asked whether they could experience it before committing to an opinion.

Mai's place card was at another table. She carried it over, pulled an empty chair beside Minh, and arranged her glass. The hostess looked toward Rook. He shook his head a fraction before she could object.

“Perfect,” Mai told the hostess, sitting down.

Bách asked after the Rolls's seat. Mai said Minh had fixed it. When Bách expressed surprise at the speed, she said she had asked nicely the second time. Minh looked at her. She took a sip of water and left the account intact.

Gifts arrived before soup: cigars, a bottle with a handwritten card, cufflinks shaped like door handles. The cufflinks came from people who had left the auction early. Minh smiled at their card while Leon examined the miniature handles.

“Occupation got jewelry,” Leon said. “We need a catalog.”

Rook joined them with two guards. His right knuckles were red from yesterday. He complimented Mai, who informed him that answering the invitation had been expensive. Rook promised an evening worth the cost. She said Felicia charged for repairs, and the soup arrived before he could choose an answer.

The man with the auction necklace complaint had found them again. His wife listened to three sentences and put down her spoon.

“You didn't buy it,” she said. “Grieve your intention quietly.”

Bách hid his face behind a napkin. Rook waited for the laughter, then announced three vehicles recovered.

“Two drive,” Minh said. “The third needs optimism.”

Rook assigned the damage to his son's judgment. Minh added the machine. Moss wanted the location of the cash. Minh looked at the guard watching the kitchen door and the one stationed beside the stairs to the balcony.

“Still looking,” Minh told Moss.

“Where?” Moss pressed.

“Places with cash,” Minh said.

Leon nodded over his soup. “Targeted approach.”

Bách toasted their recovery specialist. From another table, his daughter called him Dad in a voice that immediately brought him around.

“You promised not to do that thing,” she said.

Bách spread his hands and asked which thing. His daughter specified the habit of offering access to her life whenever a man impressed him. He had already asked whether Minh had cousins. Bách claimed conversation. She reminded him that she was married, and her husband raised his hand to verify continued presence.

Mai touched Bách's sleeve. “Offer the restaurant,” she suggested. “It doesn't have a husband.”

Bách laughed, turned red, and asked for another bottle.

The trouble began while a server passed Sơn's chair. Sơn stood aside to let her through. A large guard from the staircase took the chair, sat with his knees spread, and asked since when drivers ate upstairs.

“You're sitting on something,” Sơn told him.

The man looked beneath himself. Leon supplied the future embarrassment before Sơn could name it.

The guard stood, shirt buttons tilting across his chest. A pale scar followed his jaw. He took Sơn's braced wrist and lifted it, asking what had happened. Minh put his glass down.

Sơn's good hand caught the man's thumb. He stepped inside the reach, knocked a knee into the man's knee, and drove his forehead into the broad nose above him. The man stumbled. Sơn followed with a kick to the stomach that lifted both feet from the floor. He landed across the stolen chair. It broke under him.

Leon pointed at the splinters. “He told you.”

A second guard came down the stairs with a knife low against his thigh. Sơn saw him but could not bring his injured hand around for the grip. As the man passed Minh's chair, Minh caught the knife wrist and kept eating with his other hand.

“You need something?” Minh asked.

The guard pulled. Minh closed his fingers. The knife fell beside the bread basket. Mai moved the basket to a clean part of the table and gave the guard a look he seemed to find worse than the grip.

Minh stood, turned him toward the canal, and pushed between his shoulders. The man crossed the carpet, touched one foot to the dock, and flew into the boat. He landed across both seats. The boatman jumped ashore with his oar as water spilled onto the polished floor.

The guard reached for a gun at his ankle. Minh picked up a brass ice bucket. The guard continued reaching. Minh tossed it underhand. It punched through the boat's bottom between the man's feet. Water rose around his shoes.

“You got time to rethink the ankle,” Leon told him.

Rook went pale. “This is a private club.”

Minh looked at the boat settling into its canal. “They should keep people out.”

Above him, a shoe scraped. Minh saw a barrel appear beneath the balcony rail. It aimed past his shoulder toward the kitchen passage, where Duy stood in a waiter's jacket.

Minh pulled Mai's chair against his own and raised the heavy serving tray. The shot struck it at an angle, punched through, cut a line across the tablecloth, and buried itself in the empty chair beyond. Mai dropped under the table.

Minh hurled the tray upward. It struck the railing and folded around the gunman's forearm. The pistol spun free. Minh jumped, caught the rail, and pulled himself over before the weapon hit the floor.

The shooter swung with his free hand. Minh lifted him by the waistband and put him over the rail. Tables cleared below them. One shoe dropped into somebody's soup.

“Who?” Minh asked.

The man kicked. Minh held him still and asked again. The shooter's eyes moved toward Rook.

Minh saw it. Downstairs, Mai had come out beside her chair and was looking at the same face. Rook stood motionless at the center table.

Minh hauled the shooter back over the rail and drove him face-first into a padded bench. When he came downstairs, Leon had Duy behind a pillar. Sơn stood beside them holding the fallen pistol in his good hand.

Rook moved into Minh's path. “I did not arrange this.”

Minh looked past him. Mai checked her ribs, then shook her head to say she was unhurt. Water had darkened one side of her dress.

“Your hospitality needs supervision,” Minh told Rook.

He took the front of Rook's jacket, lifted him clear of the floor, and set him standing on the table between the soup bowls. The wood sagged. Rook held his arms out a little for balance. Nobody offered a hand.

In the parking court, Duy explained why he had come. He wanted to identify the office man who paid him before the convoy moved. The man had been at the kitchen entrance when the shooter aimed.

“We saw where he looked,” Mai told Minh.

Leon, holding the bread basket, confirmed it. When Minh looked at the basket, Leon said they had paid with an evening.

Bách and Moss hurried out behind them. Bách offered his sixty-foot boat, stressing the bar. Moss objected that he had already offered a vessel. Leon suggested inspecting both.

“Trucks,” Minh said. “Enough to move a container.”

Both donors stopped smiling briefly. Moss took out his phone. Bách offered the old packaging yard as somewhere to open the load and handed over a ring of keys. Mai told them they could resume competing over champagne once Minh was home.

Sơn brought the Rolls around. Minh helped Mai into it and asked whether she was all right. She looked down at the wet dress.

“I'm billing everybody,” Mai said.

He kissed her forehead and shut the door. Behind them, Rook emerged with his guards and a soup stain on one trouser leg. Minh waited until Rook saw him, then took his own car keys from Sơn. He wanted the drive home beside Mai, with the new yard keys in his pocket and both hands occupied by something that obeyed him.

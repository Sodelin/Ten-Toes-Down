## Chapter 26: Before the Good Shoes

In the summer of 1996, Minh was twenty-three, Mai was twenty-one, and Leon owned a green tie covered with little yellow horses. He lent it to Minh for their date, then tied the knot while explaining women. By the time he finished, one horse was jumping over Minh's throat.

“Confidence,” Leon said from the garage-bathroom doorway. “Women like it.”

Minh worked two fingers beneath the knot. “They like horses?”

“Separate question. Don't lead with it,” Leon told him.

The knot finally moved with a tearing noise. Leon took the tie back before Minh could complete the repair. It was his interview tie. Minh asked what job. Leon said the right one had not appeared.

Minh chose an open collar.

Outside, a dark blue coupe waited under a fresh coat of wax. It belonged to Ernie, a bookmaker who had wanted Minh to frighten a debtor that afternoon. The photograph showed the barber who sometimes cut Minh's hair for five dollars. Minh told Ernie to collect his own debt. Ernie asked why he kept Minh around, then watched him lift the car's front end so another mechanic could free a trapped jack.

“Oh,” Ernie said.

The date had come up afterward, and Ernie offered the coupe. Half a tank, cassette player working if the passenger door shut hard enough, wax supplied by Minh. At the time it seemed like a fair improvement in his evening.

Leon watched him lock the shop. “Ask questions,” he advised. “Let her talk. Don't tell the radiator story.”

“She asked,” Minh said.

“Everybody asks. It's in a tree,” Leon replied.

Mai opened her front door before Minh knocked. White blouse, dark trousers, small earrings. Her hair was loosely pinned. She looked at the polished coupe, then his carefully unconsidered collar.

“Where's the green tie?” Mai asked.

Minh stopped on the step. Leon had called her mother and asked whether he had mentioned a horse. Hương appeared behind Mai with a dish towel, inspected the car, and asked whose it was. Minh named Ernie and confirmed the loan had been authorized.

“Better than last time,” Hương said.

Last time had involved a motorcycle won at cards and a former owner who followed Minh to the house disputing the result. Hòa heard both men out and told the loser to find a bus. The motorcycle stayed in the driveway and in dinner conversation for three months.

Hương said Hòa wanted Minh inside for a minute. Minh took a seat at the dining table. Hòa was repairing a fan; Tín sat by the window trimming a newspaper with scissors for a reason Minh could not identify. Mai remained behind Minh's chair.

Hòa tested the fan. It rattled, settled, and blew a corner of newspaper toward the screws. He caught it and asked what time they would return.

“Eleven,” Minh said.

“I'm twenty-one,” Mai reminded her father.

Hòa said he had asked Minh. Mai pointed out that Minh was twenty-three and both could tell time. Tín put down his scissors and wanted to know why they were still discussing it when he needed the table for cards.

Mai kissed Hòa's cheek and took Minh's hand. As they reached the porch, Hòa called that the coupe's passenger tire was low. He had not left his chair. Minh stopped to look. Mai continued toward the car and said it probably was.

The expensive restaurant had a fountain and a man paid to open its door. Minh admired the arrangement. He gave the valet twenty dollars, then another twenty after being called sir. Mai watched with interest and mentioned that the valet called everybody that.

“Said it well,” Minh answered.

A waiter led them toward the kitchen passage. Empty tables stood by the windows. Minh asked for one. They were reserved, the waiter said. Mai picked up the card on the nearest and found no name on either side.

“They'll have trouble finding it,” she told him. “We'll wait while you check.”

They got the window. Minh sat delighted, confessing that he had been about to offer money. Mai said reading also worked and opened the menu.

For a while he followed Leon's instructions. He asked about printing. Mai described a man who approved two hundred business cards with his own name misspelled, then blamed the typeface. She had printed another two hundred and charged him again.

“Needed to learn his name,” Mai said.

Minh laughed. She told him about the press she wanted, the shop she would open, customers she would refuse regardless of money. Minh said everybody claimed that until the amount got large enough.

“You refused Ernie today,” Mai said.

He lowered his glass. The barber had come to order appointment cards and described Minh as a beautiful young man with terrible friends. Minh said the barber had cut his ear last month and ought to print that on the cards.

Their wine arrived. Minh had selected a bottle from the middle of the list without establishing a relationship with its pronunciation. The waiter poured a taste and waited. Minh drank, said it was good, and watched the man continue waiting.

“Test?” Minh asked.

Mai hid a sound behind her napkin. The waiter filled their glasses and withdrew. Minh asked why she laughed. She said he looked ready to fight the bottle.

“Took too long,” Minh said.

Mai lowered the napkin. “You don't have to know everything.”

“Trying to make a good impression,” he admitted.

“Stop checking whether I'm impressed,” Mai said.

Outside, their borrowed coupe gleamed beside the valet stand. Minh could see the low tire now. Mai reached across and straightened his collar.

“This is good,” she said.

He asked if she meant the collar. She meant dinner, him, so far. Minh asked how to lose points; Mai told him to repeat the question.

The plates arrived wider than his garage sink. His steak occupied a small central district beside three vegetables and a decorative streak of sauce. Mai watched his eyebrows and told him not to start.

He ate slowly for three bites. On the fourth, the steak was gone. Mai had most of her fish left. He tried appearing interested in the passing traffic.

“No,” Mai said, moving half the fish to the other side of her plate.

“I haven't asked,” Minh protested.

Mai knew what he would ask. Minh claimed he had planned a question about her mother. Mai said her mother would have fed him more. They were laughing when Ernie arrived.

The bookmaker wore a wine-red jacket and had brought a woman in silver and two men from the card room. He put a hand on Minh's shoulder, looked at Minh looking at it, and removed it. He asked how the car ran. Then he addressed Mai, explaining that nothing was too good for a beautiful woman and that he had supplied the best.

Mai smiled politely. Ernie took it as permission to remain. He asked whether Minh had told her what he did for him.

“Ernie,” Minh said, laying down his napkin.

“Praising you,” Ernie replied.

“From here it sounds crowded,” Minh said.

One companion laughed. Ernie looked him quiet, then announced he needed the car back. Immediately. Something had arisen.

Mai glanced at the woman in silver. “He needs an audience,” she told Minh.

The woman's shoulders moved over her purse. Minh stood. One card-room man moved his jacket away from his belt. Minh caught his wrist and placed his palm flat on the table, gently enough that the water stayed upright. The man's knees bent beneath the pressure.

“Eating,” Minh told him.

The man looked at Minh's empty plate. “You're done.”

“Separate problem,” Minh said, releasing him.

Mai folded her napkin and asked the waiter for the bill. Ernie accused Minh of making things ugly. Minh took out the keys, put them in Ernie's palm, and closed his fingers around them.

“Three men to ask for keys,” Minh said. “Next time, ask from farther away.”

They reached the entrance as the valet ran in reporting a tow truck. Ernie swore and pushed past them.

Outside, the coupe's front wheels were already raised. A driver stood at the controls with a clipboard. Ernie complained that he could not do this here. The driver pointed out the public road. Minh could hear the quieter explanation that followed: three missed payments, calls unanswered, a promised check that morning.

“Beautiful machine,” Mai murmured beside Minh.

Ernie turned on him. “You could help.”

Minh asked whether he wanted help and watched relief appear on the bookmaker's face. Then he went to the coupe's rear bumper and lifted both back wheels off the ground.

The tow driver stared. Minh asked where he was taking it. Yard on Fourth. Minh inquired whether they were good people. The driver said he worked there. Minh shifted the car's rear sideways until it sat straight behind the tow truck, then lowered it without a bounce.

“Crooked,” Minh explained.

The woman in silver laughed aloud. Minh tipped the driver twenty dollars and warned him about the passenger tire. Ernie looked from the departing car to his companion with the expression of a man finding another unpaid account.

Minh and Mai walked six blocks before she admitted she was hungry. He stopped so abruptly she bumped his arm.

“You too?” Minh asked.

“Fish and decorated water,” Mai said. “I liked sitting with you. Didn't say I was full.”

Across the street, a red awning sheltered a restaurant whose biggest meal required two lines on the sign. A cook with a gold tooth smoked in the doorway, watching basketball through the glass. Minh asked whether the kitchen was open. The cook asked whether he had money. When Minh showed his wallet, the kitchen became open.

They took the window booth. Mai asked the cook for whatever filled the biggest plate, twice. An old love song came from the jukebox's one working speaker. A bus stopped outside, released two people, and went on.

Minh rested his forearms on the table. “Should've brought my own car.”

“You don't have one,” Mai reminded him.

“Problem,” Minh said. “You deserve better than the back of a motorcycle.”

She looked at him until he stopped examining the menu. “I like the back of your motorcycle. I hold on to you. Nobody from the card room can sit at our table.”

The cook brought plates heavy enough to make the water jump. Minh's face changed when he saw the food. Mai laughed before he picked up his fork.

“That's the face,” she said.

They ate. Minh told her about reaching Eastbridge at seventeen, asking at three garages for work, and discovering Leon asleep in the back of a car he was supposed to repair. Mai described arriving at twelve, furious that adults expected her to know which fears would pass. Her mother's grocery errands taught her the neighborhood.

They interrupted each other with names and questions. The grocery argument occupied several minutes: which market sold the worst fruit, which owner displayed the most confidence in it. Mai remembered carrying a bad melon home and being sent back with it. Minh remembered a garage customer paying in groceries and becoming offended when Minh tested the eggs.

By the time the plates emptied, the borrowed car had become a story rather than a disaster. Mai put her hand palm-up on the table. Minh took it.

“Next time, motorcycle,” Mai said.

He asked if there was a next time. She threatened to withdraw it if he needed an application process. Minh turned her hand and kissed the inside of her wrist.

The cook passed with coffee. “Get married somewhere else,” he told them. “Need the booth.”

Four years later, he stood in the same place with another pot.

“Told you,” the cook said.

Mai took an invitation from her bag and handed it up. “Somewhere else.”

He checked the date, then Minh's bandaged hands, and asked whether he could hold the ring. Minh said he would manage. The cook said he was not working the ceremony too and tucked the invitation in his apron.

Mai placed her hand palm-up between the plates. Minh laid his in it.

“Motorcycle,” she said. “When they heal.”

Outside, a passerby stopped to admire the Rolls under the red awning. Minh watched him for a second, then looked back at Mai and took another bite.

## Chapter 17: The Brother With the Keys

The seller knew Curtis Henderson. Minh established that before allowing him to describe the convertible. Leon stood beside him trying to hear both halves of the conversation and smiling every time the price came around.

“Top level of the waterfront garage,” the seller said. “Better light.”

Minh ended the call and gave Leon his phone back. “Trap.”

“Unusual price,” Leon conceded.

Mai watched them from the Rolls. “You're going anyway.”

Minh said he wanted to see who answered the door. Leon said he wanted to see the interior. Mai looked from one man to the other and decided they had already supplied an adequate explanation of each other.

The following afternoon, Minh drove Leon's brown sedan up four levels of the waterfront garage. Its blue passenger door rattled at every patched stretch. At the entrance, Minh had lifted the barrier over the hood while Leon hunted for change. Leon asked whether he planned to pay on the way out. Minh said the service would decide.

The top floor lay almost empty beneath bright sun. A silver convertible waited in the center. Its seller came around the rear in a tan jacket, carrying the keys.

Leon stepped out of his old car and stopped looking disappointed with his circumstances. The seller demonstrated the folding roof. It settled smoothly behind the seats.

“Even the ceiling knows when to leave,” Leon said.

Minh walked around it, checking the tires and the ground beneath the engine. He opened the driver's door and started it. A smooth idle filled the quiet floor. Leon stood beside the passenger seat with his good hand against his chest.

“Good car,” Minh admitted.

The seller smiled. Minh switched off the engine and looked at the stairwell behind him.

“How many men did it come with?” Minh asked.

The seller went for his jacket. Minh caught his wrist, turned him toward the car, and pushed his hand through the steering wheel. The stairwell door opened. Three men emerged: shotgun, pistol, chain wrapped around a fist.

Leon looked at them and then at the car. “You could've sold me this honestly.”

Minh pulled the seller over the hood and threw him into the shotgun man. They fell backward through the stairwell doorway. The blast struck concrete above them and showered dust onto their jackets.

The pistol man came around the passenger side toward Leon. Leon caught his wrist against the doorframe with his good hand, took the gun, and hit him across the nose with it. He fell across the passenger seat. Leon looked at blood reaching the leather.

“Haven't even negotiated,” Leon complained.

The chain came at Minh. He caught it, drew its owner forward, and lifted the front of his jacket. One turn carried the man to Leon's old sedan. Minh set him on the roof with enough force to flatten it against the seat backs. The blue door popped open and stopped rattling.

Leon stared. “That was sentimental.”

“It rattled,” Minh said.

“Because it knew me,” Leon answered.

The helicopter rose beyond the low concrete wall.

Rotor wash chased grit across the roof. Its pilot held the skids level with Minh's shoulders. In the open side doorway, a gunman braced one boot against the frame and lowered his rifle toward Leon.

Minh shoved the convertible sideways. Its tires screamed over paint. It caught Leon at the hips and carried him behind the stairwell wall as shots struck the pavement where he had stood.

“Stay!” Minh shouted.

He ran for the helicopter while its gunman tried lowering the barrel beneath the door. From the wall, Minh jumped and caught the near skid with both hands. The aircraft dropped a foot. The pilot added power. Wind flattened Minh's shirt against his back.

He swung his legs up, hooked a heel around the skid, and reached into the doorway. His hand closed on the gunman's ankle. He pulled him out, held him by one leg above the rooftop, then dropped him the last three feet. The rifle fell beside the man's shoulder.

The pilot tried moving away. Minh put both feet against the parking wall and pulled on the skid. It came toward him, struck the concrete, and bent around the edge. Minh stepped backward onto the roof, still pulling. His shoes slid, leaving two dark streaks. Inside the windscreen, the pilot's hands worked the controls with increasing desperation.

Minh bent his knees and hauled again. Both skids hit the roof. The helicopter bounced. He caught the upper doorframe and forced it down until the flattened skids held. A rotor tip clipped a light pole, throwing sparks. The pilot cut power.

Minh waited for the blades to slow before opening his door.

“Stay parked,” Minh told him.

The pilot nodded until his headset slipped over one eye.

Leon emerged from cover with the pistol held by its barrel. He inspected the grounded helicopter, the flattened sedan, and the convertible, whose paint had survived remarkably well.

“Couldn't have traded him?” Leon asked.

Minh wiped dust from his face. “Want to learn to fly?”

Leon considered the bent aircraft. “Want doors that stay on.”

The seller had crawled back from the stairs. Minh took his phone. Before Minh could open it, the man admitted the call had come from Rook's office. White hair. The owner. He did not know the rest of the arrangement.

Leon opened the convertible's trunk. A bag of cash and a white envelope lay inside. The envelope bore Minh's name. Its card pictured a white passenger vessel and advertised THE BELL STREET APPRECIATION CRUISE. Tomorrow evening. Beneath it, Rook had written that Minh held money belonging to his friends, while Rook had a boat full of Minh's.

Leon's smile disappeared. “The free cruise.”

Minh turned toward him.

“Tickets went out yesterday,” Leon explained. “People thought Bách paid. Businesses. Families.”

Minh called Mai. She had seen a box of tickets that morning and told the deliverywoman they hadn't come from her presses. The barber and his wife planned to go. Curtis had sold extras. Felicia's assistant had been helping prepare the ship.

“Call everybody,” Minh told her. “Cancel it. Tell them whose boat.”

Mai moved away from the press; its noise faded at her end. “What happened?”

“He delivered the invitation by helicopter,” Minh said.

Mai said his name sharply. He assured her both men were all right. She said she would start the calls. Minh then called Bách, who asked whether the money was comfortable until he heard the description of the ship.

The Monarch belonged to Rook through a company. Bách began asking why. Minh read the handwritten message. For once, the restaurant owner had nothing amusing to add.

They left in the silver convertible. Leon drove with his injured arm resting on the door, the good hand on the wheel. Minh held the card on his knee and called the garage to make sure the warning was spreading.

At the barrier, the attendant compared the car with their ticket. “Different vehicle.”

“Improvement,” Leon said.

The attendant looked toward the top floor, where the broken light pole showed above the wall. Minh put two hundred dollars on the counter and told him to call police, beginning with the helicopter.

The barrier rose. Leon pressed the roof control. The black top unfolded smoothly over their heads.

“Works,” Leon said.

Neither man smiled. Ahead, the harbor lay bright beyond the garage exit. Somewhere on it, people were preparing tables for a party Rook had already decided they would attend.

## Chapter 12: No Respectable Man Keeps a Tank

Moss sent a recovery truck with armor around its cab. Bách sent a hospitality vehicle with a bar. Leon walked between them with the grave attention of a man selecting where to receive treatment.

“We can combine the features,” he proposed.

Minh stood in Bách's old packaging yard, keys in his hand. Moss's truck had six wheels, a towing assembly meant for heavy recoveries, and a load deck that extended far enough to take a container. Bách's long black vehicle offered leather seating, smoked glass, and a refrigerator.

“I asked to move a container,” Minh reminded Bách.

Bách opened the rear passenger door. “Your men go in here.”

“Container?” Minh asked.

“Behind it,” Bách said.

Moss laughed. Bách closed the door and looked toward his warehouses as though a better answer might be waiting inside one.

The yard held two forklifts, an old rail spur, a covered loading area, and a corrugated shed wide enough to conceal an expensive mistake. Minh asked about the shed. Bách looked away too quickly.

Leon clapped his hands once. “Open the big present.”

Inside was a tracked vehicle with a shoulder-high blade, a low armored cab, and a thick tube above the windshield. Gold dollar signs had been painted on its green sides. Tú walked around it with both hands on his head.

“Uncle got a tank,” Tú said.

“Modified demolition tractor,” Bách corrected.

Leon put a palm on the blade. “Tank with employment history.”

Moss asked what possible business required Bách to own it. A client had been unable to pay. What had the client done? Bách said apparently not pay, and turned the discussion toward Minh before Moss could inquire further.

Minh climbed onto a track and looked through the cab window. Loose wires hung beneath the dash. Someone had taped a cracked pressure gauge over a hole once occupied by a clock. He asked if it ran.

Bách smiled. “That's why you're here.”

Leon bent against the blade laughing. “Nigga thought he was receiving tribute. Uncle brought a repair bill.”

Minh opened the cab and sat. The seat collapsed six inches. His knees rose beside the controls; Tú made a strangled sound outside the window.

“Check the tracks,” Minh told him.

“Yes, Master,” Tú answered quickly.

Leon looked at the gold shirt. “Brand acquisition.”

For twenty minutes, Minh removed unnecessary wiring and restored a broken linkage. He got out, opened an engine panel, and turned the mechanism through two revolutions by hand. The engine caught. Black exhaust rose against the shed roof. Bách applauded from a position the smoke had not yet reached.

Minh tested the tracks. The left moved; the right stayed fixed. He stopped the engine and pointed through the window to the shipping brace still bolted across the rear wheel. Tú had mistaken it for part of the machine. Sơn held the brace with his good hand while Tú worked the spanner, and together they pulled it free.

This time both tracks bit. The tractor ground out of the shed with two inches of clearance beside the blade. Leon walked backward in front of it wearing an expression Minh had usually seen on children outside toyshops.

“I want to go to school in this,” Leon said.

Moss pointed out that he no longer attended school.

“I'll enroll,” Leon replied.

The tube was a foam cannon, Bách explained, originally part of a fire-suppression demonstration. He had seen photographs. Minh inspected the pressurized tank, traced the hose to the cannon, and said they would test it away from people. Tú lifted his hand hopefully.

“Away from Tú too,” Leon added.

A police car came through the gate before they could choose a test target. Carter got out, shut the door, and crossed the yard slowly. His gaze traveled over the tracks, the blade, the cannon, the gold dollar signs, then stopped on Bách.

“Yours?” Carter asked.

Bách looked at Minh. Minh looked back. Leon explained that a family discussion was in progress.

“Why is it running?” Carter asked Minh.

“Because it wasn't,” Minh said.

Carter took a folded coronation flyer from his pocket. His lieutenant had received one. Somebody was using Minh's name to recruit armed men for Pier Nine that night. One prospective employee had asked whether police would be there before agreeing to carry a gun.

Moss took a small step away from the truck bearing his company name.

“They'll be expecting you,” Carter told Minh. “Some of the men will be mine.”

Minh folded the flyer along Carter's crease. The printed offer of donations had now developed into an employment agency. He handed it back.

“Someone's arranging my schedule again,” Minh said.

Carter looked at the tractor. “That stays off public streets.”

Leon raised a hand to ask what happened if a street became private while they were using it. Carter turned toward him. Leon lowered the hand and classified the question as academic.

After the police car left, Moss walked toward his own car. Minh asked whether he wanted the recovery truck back. Moss stopped with a hand on his door.

“My name's on the registration,” Moss said.

Bách leaned against the blade, delighted by the difficulty. “Spell it clearly. People will want to know who contributed.”

Minh went to the recovery truck. “I can take the container without it,” he said. “This saves dragging it five blocks.”

Moss asked if he really could. Minh lifted the truck's front end until both tires cleared the ground. He held it while Moss looked beneath them. Leon got his phone out and asked him not to move, needing evidence for his own mechanic.

When Minh put the truck down, Moss came back across the yard and placed its keys in his palm.

“Keep it,” Moss said. “Wedding present.”

Bách's amusement disappeared. He looked at the keys, at the truck, at Minh, and announced that the tractor was theirs too. Minh thanked both men without interrupting the competition.

Tú looked toward the shed. “We should show people broken things more often.”

That evening, Minh came upstairs with oil under his nails and two sets of keys he had not owned at breakfast. Mai sat on the bed sorting envelopes. He told her about the tractor.

“Small?” Mai asked.

Minh shook his head.

“We have to stop inviting people,” Mai said, setting down the envelopes.

He scrubbed at the basin while she watched through the bathroom doorway. She inspected the glass cut when he finished. Most of it had closed. She touched beside the remaining red line and asked if it hurt.

“When you press it,” Minh said.

She pressed again lightly. He caught her hand and kissed her wrist.

“I like proof you're still in there,” Mai said.

He drew her close. She rested both hands on his shoulders and looked at him until he stopped trying to turn the examination into a kiss.

“You like this,” Mai said. “The gifts. Money. Leon pretending he has a royal appointment.”

“He appointed himself,” Minh said.

Mai waited. Downstairs, the recovered cars occupied space among appliances and envelopes worth more than a year of repair work. Minh liked being asked, liked opening cases, liked the keys still pressing against his pocket.

“Yes,” he told her.

“Good.” Mai took one of the keys from his hand. “I don't want you taking all this and pretending somebody forced you.”

She told him what she wanted: the empty shop next to hers, a bigger fitting space for Felicia, room behind it for another press. She would pay the business expenses from the business. She wanted him to know where part of their growing good fortune would go.

“Then take it,” Minh said.

“I was going to,” Mai replied, and pulled him toward the bedroom. “First wash the rest of you. At the moment you smell like a bridge.”

Later, with Mai asleep, Minh sat by the open window and called Duy. Leon answered from his apartment, reporting that Duy had only just stopped talking about the sailor's cap. Minh told him about Carter's warning.

The television went quiet at Leon's end. He asked if they were still going to the yard tonight.

“No,” Minh said. “Wake Duy in the morning. I want the roads. We take the container when they move it.”

Leon was silent for two seconds. Then Minh heard a cupboard open.

“I'm putting my other shoes out,” Leon said.

## Chapter 1: Door Money

Dad turned the old envelope over. The shopping list on its back made him laugh before he got to the address.

“Summer of 2000,” he told us. “Eighteen days before your mother decided to make this permanent.”

Mom moved his tea out of range of his elbow. “I had decided. You were still trying to identify the rehearsal.”

What follows is the story he told that evening, with Mom supplying the parts he had missed and the family correcting him whenever correction offered pleasure. In their account they were young again. Dad was Minh Trần, Mom was Mai Lê, and nobody had yet bought the hospital bed.

Calvin Rook called the garage while Minh had a carburetor spread across his bench and a screw clenched between his teeth. Mai stood on the other side of the bench with two samples of wedding stationery. She had been waiting for him to stop doing something disgusting with the screw.

“A hundred thousand dollars,” Rook said through the receiver. “For one evening.”

Minh took the screw out of his mouth. “What happens in the evening?”

Rook described a private auction at the Hotel Meridian. His guests needed reassurance. Minh would stand inside the ballroom entrance until midnight and keep the door open.

Minh turned a carburetor screw with one fingertip. “Do I have to clap?”

“You aren't being hired to entertain,” Rook said.

“Good. My friend handles that,” Minh said. “Which side of the door?”

Rook repeated that he meant inside. Minh asked whether the door should be open or closed. There was enough silence for Mai to turn both paper samples upside down and back again. Somewhere in Rook's office, a second telephone rang and was abruptly silenced.

“Open,” Rook said. “Substantial sums will be changing hands. People need to know they can come and go.”

“And the people outside?” Minh asked.

“Your presence will reassure them too,” Rook said.

Minh looked through the roller door at a customer's sedan on the lift. He had spent an hour diagnosing a fault the owner had manufactured with a screwdriver, then found the missing fourth screw in the air filter. He had charged for the hour. Work was work, including the variety in which a man created a problem and paid Minh to stand beside it.

“That's an expensive doorstop,” Minh told him. “Have you tried the little rubber ones?”

Mai held the cream sample against the white one. Minh pointed to cream. She turned both over to show their prices. He pointed to cream again.

“I am familiar with doorstops,” Rook said.

“Then cash when I arrive. My friend comes, and he eats.” Minh laid the screw beside its companions. “You should write down that second part.”

Rook said it was a banquet. Minh let him keep the confidence in his voice. After they settled the address, he wrote it on an envelope and hung up. Mai borrowed the pen and added milk, laundry soap, scallions.

“We're discussing a hundred thousand dollars,” Minh said, watching her underline soap.

“Get the large one,” Mai told him. She capped the pen and let him pull her around the bench, keeping her blouse clear of his hands until he proved they were clean enough. “And I ordered the cream yesterday.”

“You asked my opinion,” Minh said against her cheek.

Mai kissed him. “I enjoy hearing it catch up.”

She held his chin after the kiss and reminded him of the eighteen days. Minh said he knew when he was getting married.

“Last week you thought the rehearsal was the ceremony,” Mai said.

“Your father wore a tie,” Minh replied.

“My father had a meeting.” Mai straightened the open collar she was holding.

“He wears a tie when somebody's about to lose something,” Minh said. “I was making a reasonable assessment.”

Mai laughed into his collar. When she drew away, she wiped an overlooked streak of grease from the side of his thumb. “Come home with the money,” she told him. “We can argue about assessment where the towels live.”

By dusk, Leon Price had parked a borrowed white limousine across the garage, the bakery, and a portion of municipal authority. His own white suit disappeared into the upholstery when he leaned from the window.

“Get in, nigga. We employed,” Leon called.

Minh came out carrying his toolbox. Mai followed with his dark jacket. She paused at the limousine and addressed the driver before either man could improve his account.

“Does its owner know where it is, Leon?” Mai asked.

“He knows it has an excellent driver,” Leon said. “I'm returning it before his daughter's wedding breakfast. She's marrying a dentist. This car is the last excitement that family gets.”

Mai laid Minh's jacket over his arm, kissed him once more, and shut the rear door after him. The toolbox went on the floor by his feet.

Leon inspected it in the mirror. “You expecting a repair?”

“Door might stick,” Minh said.

Leon also inspected Minh's trousers, the pale primer stain refusing to hide under the jacket. Minh had scrubbed it twice. Leon had watched him do the second scrub and had suggested simply buying a clean pair, as if he were not wearing a suit purchased on a philosophy of future income.

“Rook hired me to stand,” Minh said when Leon looked at the mark again.

“You can stand handsome,” Leon told him. He adjusted the little gold chain across his vest. “Costs the same in effort.”

The limousine carried them through Eastbridge toward the harbor. Outside the bakery, a woman raised a camera. Leon slowed, offered his better profile, and accelerated only after Minh tapped the envelope against the partition.

“Scallions,” Minh reminded him.

“One day,” Leon said, “we are going somewhere without a vegetable supervising.”

At the Meridian, Rook had carpeted the service entrance red. Leon passed it. A woman in a silver dress directed the expensive cars outside the main doors, and he stopped squarely in front of her before she could wave him on.

She bent to the rear window, saw Minh, and opened his door herself. Three valets converged. One spread his jacket over a luggage cart before taking the oily toolbox.

Leon stepped out into this unexpected respect. “I should've brought something dirty,” he said.

A man approached from the steps with his hands visibly empty. Minh remembered him holding a crowbar in a pool hall, then standing beside a parking meter while somebody fetched a saw. The crowbar had gone around the meter without his fingers getting permission to leave.

“City charge you for that?” Minh asked him.

The man nodded.

“Good. They fixed it?” Minh asked.

The man nodded harder and backed far enough away to become part of the entrance.

Inside, a white sports car stood on glass over the lobby fountain, its gold-plated engine displayed under spotlights. Leon acquired an unlit cigar from a woman carrying a tray meant for somebody more specifically invited. Rook waited at the lifts with a silver briefcase between his shoes.

Minh opened the case on the reception counter. The money was real. So was the pleasure of looking at it.

“We should arrive more often,” Leon said, leaning over his shoulder.

Rook watched Minh lift two bundles and return them. “Satisfied?”

“Very,” Minh said.

The bundles filled both halves of the case. Minh ran a thumb along one exposed edge and inspected the depth beneath it. Rook's dinner jacket fitted so perfectly that it made the other man's stillness look purchased; Minh wondered whether a tailor waited around the corner for any alteration caused by breathing.

“You counted all that?” Rook asked.

“Are you short?” Minh said.

“Of course not.” Rook looked briefly at Leon's cigar.

“Then we agree,” Minh told him.

Minh closed the case and handed it to Leon. “Where's my door?”

The second-floor ballroom went quiet when he entered. A trumpet continued alone for an unfortunate second. Near the front, a man grabbed a telephone and told somebody to close the betting line. Elsewhere, chairs scraped, papers disappeared into jackets, and the first applause rose before Rook had opened his mouth.

Leon leaned toward Minh. “You been here?”

“First time,” Minh said.

“These niggas having memories in advance,” Leon murmured.

Rook announced that every purchaser was free to leave at any time. Minh would see to it. A woman near the stage asked whether he could also see to the shrimp, and Leon volunteered before a waiter could intervene.

Minh set his tools beside the entrance. Decorative timber concealed two newer steel panels. Fresh bolts held their overhead tracks; a green light showed above the right panel. He raised his hand toward the housing.

Rook noticed the toolbox arriving. “Expecting trouble?” he asked.

“You hired me,” Minh said.

“I hired reassurance.” Rook kept his smile toward the guests while speaking from one corner of it.

Leon lifted the case. “You bought the deluxe package, nigga.” He smiled back at Rook, whose expression did not improve with the additional reassurance.

“Don't touch that,” a man ordered him.

The speaker wore leather trousers, a sleeveless shirt, flame tattoos, and a championship belt over one shoulder. His own face towered behind him on a poster above a custom motorcycle: SƠN THE UNBROKEN. A camera crew followed close enough to profit from anything he said.

Minh glanced at the poster. “You bring your own compliment?”

Sơn looked him over, including the primer stain on his trousers. “Workers take the back stairs.”

“You heard him,” Minh told the cameraman. “Careful with that equipment.”

Someone at the nearest table choked on a drink. Sơn handed off his belt. Minh took off the jacket Mai liked, folded it on the toolbox, and faced him on the carpet between the entrance and the display platform.

Sơn punched him in the chest. The blow sounded across the room. Minh's collar button shot past Sơn's ear and struck the cameraman above one eyebrow.

Minh looked down at the open shirt. Sơn looked at his swelling knuckles. Behind him, the camera tilted but stayed on.

“That your opening number?” Minh asked.

Sơn's answer was a kick. Minh caught his rising ankle, stepped beyond his planted foot, and lifted. The fighter went horizontal with his arms reaching for a floor suddenly much farther away. Minh turned him once, carried him three steps, and seated him on the display motorcycle.

The seat went through the frame. Both wheels jumped. Minh folded the handlebars over Sơn's shoulders, pushed the front fork backward until the tire sat against his chest, and let the rear wheel rise behind his head. A polished exhaust pipe rolled onto the carpet.

Sơn remained upright in the wreckage, knees beside his ears, trying to discover which part could still obey him.

The drummer hit one startled cymbal.

“Keep that nigga,” Leon called across the room. “He understands punctuation.”

The cameraman advanced three feet. Sơn ordered him to turn it off; the cameraman reminded him, with blood on his eyebrow, that they had a contract.

Minh hung the championship belt on the folded wheel and returned to his jacket. Rook met him beside the toolbox.

“Was that necessary?” Rook asked.

Minh held the edges of his shirt together. “You got a sewing kit?”

A gray-haired woman with diamond earrings brought one from her bag, pulled a chair beside the entrance, and sat him down. Rook tried explaining that Minh was working.

“Then his job got a chair,” she told Rook.

She threaded a larger black button onto Minh's shirt. Across the room, Leon checked the money again before going in search of a larger plate. Onstage, Rook's son Dante watched the entrance through the auction lights, one finger pressed to his headset.

The light over Minh's head turned red. Steel descended inside the doorframe.

Minh slid his right shoe beneath it. The panel hit the toe with a clang and stopped. He kept his elbow still for the needle, looked across at Dante, and smiled.

“Your father,” Minh called, “paid for open.”

## Chapter 2: Open Until Midnight

The shutter tried Minh's shoe again. Its motor whined overhead; the toe stayed on the carpet.

“Move that, baby,” the woman sewing his shirt told him.

Minh glanced down. “It isn't bothering me.”

“Your elbow,” she said, pulling the thread straight. “I'm doing useful work.”

Across the doorway, the second steel panel slammed down hard enough to cut the carpet. A departing guest leaped away and emptied his champagne into the pocket of the man behind him. That man demanded an explanation while his wife bit off the thread in Minh's new button.

“He still talks about that coat,” she told Minh. “Don't let anyone hit this button. It's his.”

Leon returned with two loaded plates and the money under his arm. He considered the sealed entrance, looked back toward the buffet, and decided aloud that captivity might suit him for twenty minutes.

Minh stood. The panel rose on his shoe until he could slide his fingers beneath it. He took the weight in one hand, lifted it past his face, and drove it into the overhead housing. Bolts dropped around his shoulders. The motor gave a noise like a large animal swallowing cutlery and stopped.

Rook had reached the stage. Minh could see him arguing with Dante, whose red jacket had become very conspicuous under the lights. Neither came to help.

“Anybody leaving?” Minh asked the room.

Several hands went up. A woman in blue started towing her husband by the wrist. He protested that he had not bid yet; she told him to keep walking while that remained his largest regret.

A guard intercepted them with a hand inside his jacket. “Everyone stays until Mr. Rook authorizes—”

Minh pulled the entire panel free. The remaining bolts tore from the track in a burst. He caught its upper edge before it fell toward the guard, rotated the slab, and leaned it against the closed half of the doorway.

The guard's sentence stayed unfinished.

“We all been instructed, nigga,” Leon told him. “My doctor said vegetables. Look at this plate.”

The guard took his hand out of his jacket. The woman in blue passed him, removed her husband's auction paddle, and dropped it in a champagne bucket. More guests followed. Minh took a napkin someone offered for the grease on his palm and stepped aside to give the line room.

His seamstress carried her chair clear herself, holding it in front of her knees while two armed men failed to think of helping. Minh reached for it; she told him to keep his hands available for the door.

“Your husband need that napkin?” Minh asked, indicating the wet pocket.

“He needs to stop turning his pocket into the subject of the evening,” she said. Then she took the napkin anyway and carried it to him.

The departing guests thanked Minh in voices that varied with how recently they had bet against him. One man offered to settle a debt Minh did not remember. A woman asked whether the lift was operating, looked at the uprooted steel beside him, and decided to take the stairs before he answered.

It halted when a man behind the stage shouted that the money was gone.

Leon set his briefcase between his feet. “Specify,” he called.

Three men staggered out through the curtain. One carried the empty lid of a jewelry display; another had a bleeding nose. They reported empty parking spaces below, an open armored truck, missing cash cases. The prize convoy had disappeared.

The woman who had sewn Minh's shirt took her husband by the sleeve before he could complain about his necklace again.

“You hadn't paid,” she reminded him. “Try enjoying an advantage.”

The service door behind the stage burst inward. A hotel worker came through backward and knocked two guards into the curtain. A man with a shotgun stepped over him, fired into the ceiling, and ordered everybody down.

Plaster rained across the tables. A hanging light dropped into the fruit and bounced into a woman's lap. She threw it at her husband. Leon collected his second plate.

“Now,” Leon said, “we leaving.”

Minh was already moving. He caught an empty chair by its back, vaulted the nearest dining table with it held in front of him, and landed on the stage steps. A man beneath his flight ducked late and removed his wig in the process.

The shotgun fired into the chair. Wood struck Minh's sleeves. He thrust the broken frame down over the barrel, twisted it toward the floor, and kicked the gunman backward through the curtain. The man landed inside the bass drum.

Minh bent the captured barrel around the metal stair rail and dropped it. The drummer emerged carrying one stick and the expression of a man whose evening had acquired an invoice.

“He hired the room,” Minh told him, pointing at Rook.

“I hired music,” Rook snapped.

“You got percussion,” Minh said, and turned toward the service stairs.

Two more gunmen were climbing. The lower one tried aiming a pistol past his partner's shoulder. Minh pulled the service door off its hinges and shoved it sideways into the stairwell. It struck the upper man across the chest. His shotgun discharged into concrete as both men fell under the panel and skidded five steps.

Minh followed. He planted a foot on the wood, waited until the guns fell from beneath it, then bent the rail across the panel to hold the men there. The lower one complained that he could not feel his partner's elbow. Minh told him that sounded like progress and took both weapons to the landing.

The upper gunman had tried lifting the panel when Minh arrived. Now he stayed very still while the rail curved over it. A shoe projected from the lower edge at an angle Minh did not like. He lifted the corner enough for its owner to straighten his leg, then settled the door across their torsos again.

“You can't leave us,” the upper man said.

Minh placed the guns beyond reach. “You were coming up together. Enjoy the conversation.”

From the ballroom, a waiter peered through the missing doorway. Minh asked him to send somebody for the men when they were finished waving guns. The waiter looked at the bent rail, then at Minh's repaired shirt, and promised that security would attend to it. His voice suggested he expected security to become a different department by the time it arrived.

When he returned, Leon stood at the ruined entrance holding the jacket, the briefcase, and a plate with impressive independence.

“Time?” Leon asked.

Minh checked the clock. “Ten twelve. He paid till midnight.”

Leon pointed to the missing shutter. “That motherfucker staying open till Christmas. Come on.”

Rook followed them into the corridor with six men who maintained a careful distance from the loose steel. Dante came after him, jacket gone, sweat spreading at his collar.

“My son says your people were downstairs,” Rook told Minh.

Leon lifted his plate. “My people was at the butter.”

“The loading crew said they came with you,” Dante said. “They knew your name.”

Minh asked for theirs. Dante could offer a brown jacket and short hair. When he stepped toward Leon, Minh put one finger against his chest. Dante's forward movement stopped while the rest of him worked out why.

“You gave twelve million dollars to a haircut?” Minh asked.

Dante began describing the brown jacket again. Minh asked whether he had collected identification, a delivery order, a signature, anything that would distinguish the men from a stranger at a bus stop. Each answer was shorter than the question before it.

“They knew you,” Dante insisted.

“So do you,” Minh told him. “It's not going especially well.”

Rook watched his son's face and stopped asking for details. His men shifted along the wall, making room for more auction guests who had come to discover what their security arrangements had bought.

At the corridor window, he looked down into the loading court. Workers surrounded three vacant spaces. Beside the open armored truck, two guards sat with their hands tied. A dark sedan was leaving through the unguarded exit.

The driver looked back.

Minh knew Duy's face before the man recognized his. Duy turned forward and accelerated around the corner. Minh left his palm against the glass for a second, then brought it down before Rook joined him.

“What?” Rook demanded.

“You need a gate,” Minh said.

Rook told him to stop joking. Minh faced him fully. He explained the contract in the same order they had made it: a door, inside, open, midnight. He had done the work. Men had tried closing the door with steel and guns. He had made their improvements unnecessary.

Dante tried interrupting. Minh pointed at him without turning. “Finish breathing first.”

Rook lowered his voice. “Twelve million in cash, diamonds, vehicles. I want it back.”

“Then hire somebody,” Minh said.

“I'm asking you.” Rook moved closer. “Two hundred thousand on recovery.”

Bách Vũ arrived from the ballroom holding two auction paddles. Bell Street called him Uncle Dollar, largely because he had spent years insisting upon it. He looked from Rook to Minh and raised a paddle.

“A million,” Bách announced.

Rook told him the conversation was private. Half the ballroom had followed them into the corridor. Bách looked around at the audience and raised his other paddle.

“A million over here too,” Bách said, raising the other paddle. “Strong demand.”

The husband in the wet dinner jacket joined the bidding on behalf of his unpaid necklace. His wife corrected him, but he explained that he was participating. Laughter traveled along the corridor while Rook tried finding an exit through six of his own men.

Minh enjoyed watching him fail.

“A million,” Rook said finally. “When everything listed comes back.”

Minh held up one hand before Bách could begin a victory auction. “Everything you listed. Vehicles, cash, jewelry. You have an inventory?”

Rook nodded. The million had aged him visibly. “The office can send it.”

“Send it to the garage,” Minh said. “Don't send another room full of people with it.”

Bách lowered one paddle to his side and used the other to fan himself. “Look at that, Calvin. Professional service. For two hundred thousand you might have got a photograph of the money.”

The husband with the wet pocket laughed first. Rook looked at him until his wife turned him gently toward the lifts.

“And nobody follows Mai,” Minh added.

Rook's mouth tightened. “I haven't mentioned your fiancée.”

“Now you don't have to.” Minh offered his hand.

Rook shook it carefully. Bách applauded with the paddles. Leon clapped the briefcase against his free palm. Minh released Rook before the man's knuckles contributed anything audible.

Downstairs, the valets had polished the toolbox. Leon accepted their assistance with the solemnity due an institution that improved dirty things for free. Once the limousine cleared the harbor gates, he asked who Minh had seen.

“Duy,” Minh said. “Driving out.”

For a block, Leon said nothing. Shop lights moved along the limousine's white side. Then he looked into the mirror.

“He see you?” Leon asked.

Minh nodded. “Looked straight up at the window.”

Leon shifted the case on the seat beside him at the next light. He did not open it this time. “Duy can explain things for forty minutes without making a thing clearer,” he said.

“He'll call,” Minh said, taking out his phone to check it.

There was no missed call. He put it back. Leon drove on, leaving the expensive hotel's reflected light behind them.

“We still shopping?” Leon asked.

Minh unfolded the envelope. “Large soap.”

At the all-night market, Leon stayed with the briefcase while Minh took milk, scallions, and laundry soap to register two. He put a hundred-dollar bill beside them.

The cashier asked for something smaller. Minh checked his wallet, looked out at Leon deliberately admiring the dashboard, and admitted defeat.

The cashier pressed her intercom. “Manager to register two. Again.”

Minh waited beside the groceries. After everything that had happened upstairs at the Meridian, this was the first door nobody would open for him.

## Chapter 3: The House Has Rules

Mai opened their apartment door with a hammer in her hand. Minh stopped on the top stair.

“You heard?” he asked.

“The cupboard,” Mai said. “What did the cupboard not tell me?”

Leon squeezed past with groceries and the briefcase. He put the milk away before displaying the money, arranged the scallions in their jar, and stood back from the table like a man opening a museum.

Mai looked inside. Then she laid the hammer down carefully.

“That much standing?” she asked Minh.

“Some sitting,” he admitted.

She found the replacement button. Minh explained that a married woman had sewn it, and Mai pointed out that she had not asked the question he was answering. Leon put the kettle on before the investigation spread to his watch.

At the kitchen table, Minh told her about the motorcycle and the doors. Leon interrupted to reproduce the motorcycle's death, using two cups and his own knees. Mai laughed until Minh reached Duy.

“Maybe they hired him to drive,” she said.

Minh described how Duy had looked back and accelerated. Leon said Duy owed money all over town. Last week he had tried selling a watch he did not fully own.

Mai looked at Leon's wrist. He lowered his sleeve.

“Excellent price,” Leon said.

Minh called Duy twice. On the second attempt he left a message: “Duy. Call me.” He closed the phone and placed it beside the untouched tea.

“Terrifying,” Leon said, setting down the third cup.

Mai leaned against Minh's knee. “I said no threats.”

“I asked for a call,” Minh said.

“He knows you,” Leon reminded him. He tried the tea, burned his tongue, and took offense at the cup. “That voice could repossess furniture.”

Mai moved the case away from the steam. The kitchen seemed smaller with a hundred thousand dollars on its table. Minh had imagined handing her the money and having her make some magnificent declaration about their future. She checked that the milk had gone in the refrigerator, then put a chipped cup in front of him. He found he liked that too.

“Which friend did Rook think you were bringing?” Mai asked.

Minh nodded at Leon. “The one driving me home with the money.”

“He chose well,” Leon said. “I drove exceptionally slowly past every house where anybody doubted me.”

Mai shut the briefcase. “Nobody brings a gun upstairs,” she said. “Seventeen days until the wedding. Put the stupidity on the ground floor.”

Leon asked how close his reception table would be to the food. Mai answered by handing him the loose cupboard hinge and asking him to hold it. He discovered an urgent interest in the ceiling while Minh repaired the cupboard above him.

The first delivery woke them at eight. A truck was reversing toward the garage, beeping at a frequency designed to travel through unmarried happiness. Minh went to the window. Below him, two men unloaded a silver refrigerator wearing a red bow.

“Wrong house,” he called.

The driver consulted his card. “Trần?”

“We have a refrigerator,” Minh said.

“Now you have options,” the driver replied.

Behind that truck came a fruit basket big enough to seat a child, followed by a washing machine. Mai joined Minh at the window and studied the queue.

“I should have added more to the shopping list,” she said.

Bách's card offered insurance against warm champagne. The washing machine came from the woman who had repaired his shirt; her husband requested that Minh stop accepting buttons from his coat. Inside the fruit basket, an auction guest Minh barely recognized had tucked three thousand dollars marked travel expenses.

“Where does he want you to travel?” Mai asked.

“Away from him,” Minh guessed.

By then the refrigerator was jammed diagonally across the garage entrance. The deliverymen were arguing about which of them had measured it. Reggie Mills arrived with his community-access camera and began documenting neighborhood prosperity until Mai directed him toward the new scratch on her doorframe.

Minh took the refrigerator off the dolly, turned it sideways, and carried it through the shop. Its bow tickled his cheek. The deliverymen stood holding an empty trolley; Reggie swung back too late to film anything but the appliance settling beside the rear wall.

“Again,” Reggie demanded.

“Buy another,” Minh told him.

One driver crossed a line out of his form and offered Minh the clipboard. CUSTOMER ASSISTED was written over it. Minh signed while Mai distributed fruit to people who had come out to inspect the trucks.

Hòa arrived in his plain blue sedan, accepted an orange from a neighbor, and waited until Minh saw him.

“Your mother cooked,” Hòa said. “Bring Leon.”

“He has to return the limousine,” Minh explained.

Hòa peeled a strip from the orange. “Then bring the owner.”

He accepted a paper bag of fruit from Mai, holding it by the thin handle while she reached beneath it to support the bottom. Hòa had already turned toward the car. The bag did not tear. Minh watched him put it on the passenger seat, then looked down at an identical bag whose handle had just broken in Reggie's hand.

An apple rolled toward the gutter. Reggie rescued it with his shoe and looked after Hòa's sedan.

“Your father-in-law scares me,” Reggie said.

“We're not married yet,” Minh replied.

Reggie tucked the apple beneath his camera arm. “Man already got jurisdiction.”

By noon, the owner was on the Lê porch eating beside Leon. His daughter's wedding breakfast had been delayed because the dentist's mother had locked herself in a bathroom over a photographer. Leon had offered Minh's professional door services. Minh went inside before the man could name a price.

Hương and Mai worked at the stove. Bích sorted herbs at the kitchen table, beckoned Minh closer, and inspected the black button.

“Good stitch,” Bích decided. “Terrible match.”

In the living room, Tín had one slipper off and a newspaper on his knee. At seventy-eight he sat straighter than men obliged to stand for a living. A crew was trying to maneuver a black safe through his front door. Its unsigned card said FOR YOUR NEW RESPONSIBILITIES.

Minh lifted it from the dolly, held it against his chest, and turned toward the old man's chair.

Tín extended a finger beneath its base. “There.”

The safe rose. Minh's arms followed it until letting go became preferable to being displayed with the gift. Tín held the entire safe six inches above Minh's hands on one finger and examined the scuffed doorframe.

“They charge for delivery?” Tín asked.

The deliverymen shook their heads. The old man lowered the safe onto the rug without getting up. His slipper went back on. Leon arrived in the doorway just in time to see Minh staring at the empty space between his palms.

“You did it again?” Leon asked Tín.

“Nigga brought it into my house,” Tín said, unfolding his newspaper.

Minh looked toward the kitchen. Mai had both hands over her mouth.

“Your face is loud,” he told her.

At lunch he gave the family the rest of Rook's offer. Tín continued eating until the name reached him, then asked whether Calvin still wore shiny shoes.

The table was full before the food finished arriving. Hương set one hot dish on a cabinet, moved it herself a moment later, and warned everybody that the cabinet was not for hot dishes. Leon's guest waited for a seat until Bích pointed with her spoon; he sat immediately, leaving no possibility that she would have to point twice.

Hòa waited until Mai settled beside Minh before pulling out his own chair with his foot. Minh reached to move the safe's unsigned card away from a bowl of sauce. Tín took it from him and put it beneath a short table leg. The wobble stopped.

“New responsibility,” Tín said when Minh looked at him.

Mai's laugh escaped through her nose. Minh put his hand on her knee under the table and warned her that he could still hear her face.

“Eight years,” Leon reminded the old man.

“Some men take longer to dry,” Tín said.

Minh was about to ask when Hòa's sleeve caught the water beside his elbow. Minh reached for the falling glass. Hòa already had two fingers beneath its base. He set it level without spilling a drop and went on serving rice. Tín looked at his son. Hòa looked back. Mai nudged Minh's ankle before he could improve his question.

“Eat,” she told him.

Wedding business resumed: band, photographer, food. The limousine owner became intensely occupied with his bowl at the word photographer. Tín finally asked how much the door had paid.

“He was supposed to be at his daughter's breakfast,” Leon told Bích, indicating the man beside him.

“Then he should eat more,” Bích said. “He's behind.”

The man accepted another helping. Hương asked whether the mother was still in the bathroom. He checked his phone and reported that she had agreed to come out if the photographer would arrive with a different camera. Minh put down his spoon and waited for somebody to ask him about the door. Mai squeezed his hand under the table before the question could be offered.

“You have a wedding of your own,” she murmured to him. “No discounts.”

“A hundred thousand,” Minh said.

Tín put down his spoon. “Hòa. We've been doing it for free.”

Bích tapped her husband's wrist. “You charge people enough for coming in.”

While the table laughed, Mai found Minh's hand underneath it. He squeezed hers and decided the safe could wait for its explanation.

Someone knocked. Leon asked if that cost anything. Reggie entered with an armful of flyers before Tín could establish a rate.

The picture showed Minh lifting an engine. The printer had cropped away the sun that accounted for his furious squint. WELCOME YOUR NEW KING filled the top. Below it, Bell Street was invited to a celebration the following morning. Debts, disputes, and donations would be accepted.

Mai took a flyer. Hòa took another. Minh read his own new business hours beneath a photograph he had never approved.

Leon leaned over his shoulder. “Nigga, employment found you at lunch.”

## Chapter 4: Congratulations, You Owe Everybody

By nine the following morning, a red carpet connected Minh's garage to the liquor store. Whoever had installed it had carpeted around two trash cans and tied a bow on a third. Above the shop, gold balloons supported a banner reading LONG MAY HE REPAIR.

Mai drank coffee under it while Minh tried finding the person who had paid. Everyone he asked identified someone absent. Leon emerged from the liquor store with sparkling cider and explained that he had improved an existing situation.

“You improved it across the trash,” Minh observed.

“Even the common people attend a coronation,” Leon said, setting down the crate.

Women from the bakery arrived with trays, followed by a man wearing a hamburger restaurant's paper crown and carrying Duy's keyboard. Minh recognized the chipped corner of its casing.

The player said his cousin had bought it yesterday for two hundred dollars. Leon muttered that Bell Street had more cousins than people. Minh tried Duy's phone again while the keyboard produced a first chord loud enough to summon a dog to an upstairs window.

The dog barked. The player apologized to the audience. The phone sent Minh to the same recorded voice.

By ten, donated furniture blocked both motorcycle bays. The barber had brought free haircuts and a photograph of himself with scissors; the corner-market owner brought Minh's change from the night before. She reported that he had frightened her cashier by carrying an entire box of soap to the till when the manager took too long.

The leather sofa arrived lengthwise through the roller door. Its deliverymen turned too early, clipped a balloon with the plastic wrapping, and discovered that half the neighborhood now had an opinion about corners. Minh moved an engine stand out of their path. Leon lay down on the sofa before the wrapping came off.

“Testing,” Leon explained when Mai looked at him.

“Test whether you can get up,” Mai said. “Those men need to pull it out from under you.”

The barber placed his framed portrait on Minh's bench beside the carburetor. Minh moved it away from the oil. The barber moved it into better light. They settled for a spot where the scissors pointed at the customer seating.

“That can be the warning,” Minh said.

“We have the washing machine,” Mai said, accepting the envelope. “Apparently we're expanding.”

Bách arrived in a pale yellow Rolls-Royce. His men brought flowers and a cake shaped like Minh's garage. A crowned sugar Minh stood in its door holding a wrench the size of a tree.

“They made you taller,” Mai said.

“Tastefully,” Bách assured her, and put the car keys in Minh's hand.

Conversation stopped around them. Minh asked whether the car was for the wedding or for keeping. Bách wanted to know whether he planned to marry temporarily. Mai took the keys, sat behind the wheel, and moved their new possession into an empty space before another delivery truck could take it.

“Thank you,” she told Bách when she returned. “The seat needs fixing.”

Bách offered a man. Mai pointed at Minh.

They traced the coronation flyers as far as Bách's restaurant. He had distributed two thousand, assuming Minh had arranged the printing. Mai rubbed the ink and smelled the stock. It had not come from her shop. Leon found the missing letter in DONATONS; Reggie proposed that a donaton was a large animal commonly worshipped near cash.

“You distributed two thousand before asking?” Mai said to Bách.

Bách held a slice of cake at the height of his tie. “People were already celebrating. I supported the occasion.”

“With my fiancé's address,” Mai said.

“He has a sign above the garage,” Bách replied, but the reply lost its courage halfway out. He looked up at LONG MAY HE REPAIR as though it might take his side.

Minh took a flyer from the stack. The photograph was real, the promise beneath it was new, and people were already arriving as if he had made it himself. He watched the mechanic approaching and folded the flyer into his pocket. He could find out who had opened the business after seeing what it paid.

Mai told Reggie to lower the camera. He lowered it six inches. She waited until the lens actually pointed at the ground.

A mechanic from Foster approached with an envelope and a complaint. An extortion crew had charged his drivers twice and now wanted money from his wife's salon. Their collector was Al, the large one with the dog.

“Little Al,” Minh said. “Big Al's little.”

Leon borrowed a pen. “That's why we need forms.”

Minh called the correct Al. The conversation lasted less than a minute. He told Al to stop charging the garage, listened, and explained that nobody had asked him to surrender the dog. Then he accepted the mechanic's envelope: five thousand dollars.

During the call, Al's voice had started loud enough for Leon to wince. It grew quieter after Minh said his name. The mechanic listened with his hands pressed against each other, expecting a price or a complicated favor in return. Minh held the phone away from his ear when Al became anxious about the animal.

“You can keep the dog,” Minh repeated. “I didn't mention the dog.”

After he hung up, the mechanic shook Minh's hand with both of his. He described the second collection, the third threat, his wife's long hours in the salon. Minh let him finish before promising to go with him if the men returned. Leon slid a piece of cake into the mechanic's free hand so he would have something to carry out besides relief.

“That's all?” the mechanic asked.

“He'll call you,” Minh said. “If he comes back, I come with you.”

The mechanic left almost running. Minh put the fee in his jacket. Mai asked whether men would now be turning up between scheduled car repairs. Minh suggested appointments. She held out her hand and informed him that she charged separately.

He was laughing when the flower van stopped beyond the Rolls.

Its driver got out first. A second man emerged wearing a florist's apron and carrying lilies in a tall rectangular arrangement. Minh watched the stems shift while the flowers stayed still. He put his cake plate into Leon's hand.

“Down,” Minh said.

A gun rose through the lilies. Minh lifted the cake table and threw it across the sidewalk. Its folded legs hit the florist's chest, driving him backward against the van. The cake flew separately, struck the windshield, and spread the coronation over the driver's view.

Gunfire punched through the table as it landed. People dropped behind cars. Leon shoved Reggie under the Rolls's front bumper and lay across his legs, still trying to keep Minh's cake plate level.

The keyboard player folded beneath his stand. The butcher dragged a woman behind the sofa, then shoved the sofa itself across the doorway until the delivery wrapping tore. Bách crouched beside his own Rolls with a hand over his head and a fist closed around the cake he had failed to put down. Mai pulled two neighbors into the garage before turning toward the tools hanging on the wall.

Minh saw her reach the tire iron. He moved faster toward the van. He wanted its door occupied before anybody inside could discover how much of the sidewalk remained exposed.

Minh reached the gunman before he could push the table away. He stamped the edge down, caught the barrel projecting beside it, and turned until the wrist followed. The weapon fell. Minh pulled the man up by his apron and threw him through the open sliding door into a second gunman kneeling inside. Both crashed through the florist's buckets. White petals and water ran into the street.

The driver accelerated. Minh caught the bumper. Its mounts tore loose after two feet, so he let the useless strip go, stepped close, and put both hands under the rear frame.

The van's back rose. Rear wheels spun level with his head; its front bumper ground along the road. Inside, the gunmen slid through bucket water into the backs of the seats. The engine screamed beneath Minh's instruction to switch it off.

He raised the rear another foot. The frosting-covered windshield cracked.

“Off,” Minh repeated.

The engine stopped. The only remaining note came from the keyboard player, who had landed chest-first across the keys.

“Let go of the music, nigga,” Leon called from beneath the car. “We understand.”

Minh lowered the van and took its keys. The driver said he only drove. Minh pointed out that drivers usually began somewhere, and asked who had hired him. In the sliding doorway, the florist tried crawling out. Mai struck the step in front of his fingers with a tire iron.

“Door charge,” Mai told him.

The driver named Benny at Crown Arcade.

Minh checked the man's identification, returned his wallet, and ordered him to stay in the seat. Reggie crawled out, camera still on his shoulder. Leon asked whether he had filmed the rescue.

“Your elbow covered the lens,” Reggie said.

Leon studied the dirt across his white suit. “I risked all this for a close-up of skin.”

Carter arrived without a siren and walked between the bullet-pierced table and the nosedown van. He asked about injuries before explanations. Reggie showed him a cut on his cheek and offered to sell the footage; Carter's expression lowered the price to public-service rates.

At the Rolls, Mai lifted the sugar figure from beside the wiper. Its crown had survived the flight. She put it into Minh's palm.

“Long may you repair,” Mai said.

He bit off the crown. She stared for a moment, then laughed so hard she had to steady herself against the hood.

The mechanic from Foster returned while Carter opened his notebook. Al had called. He had sent another ten thousand dollars and apparently wanted no more appointments.

Carter looked at the envelope. “I don't want to know.”

“Then stop reading over my shoulder,” Minh said, putting it away.

Carter stepped beside him instead and requested a statement. Minh glanced at the clock above the workbench, then nodded to Leon.

“Tell Benny I'll be late,” Minh said.

Leon took out his phone. “How late?”

Minh looked at Carter's pencil. “We'll find out how fast he writes.”

## Chapter 5: The Arcade King

Benny had prepared for Minh's arrival by declaring himself closed for maintenance. The notice covered the glass front door of Crown Arcade. Behind it, someone was moving furniture.

Leon parked his own brown sedan at the curb, its blue passenger door protesting when Minh opened it. “I told him you were coming,” Leon said. “He answered the phone enthusiastic, then had to become absent halfway through.”

A young man in red trainers approached the window before Minh could stand. MASTER CHIGGA shone across his black shirt in gold.

“You're Minh?” the young man asked.

Minh looked at the lettering. “Depends what your shirt wants.”

The shirt wanted recognition as a brand. Its owner played pool here, was building something, and eventually admitted that his name before the gold letters had been Tú. Minh asked him to move. Tú stepped sideways into the curb; Leon caught his elbow and suggested mastering sidewalks next.

Minh knocked. A man inside held up a second notice: READ THE SIGN.

Minh pulled the handle. The lock came out of the frame. He caught it before it hit the pavement and handed it to the man.

“Maintenance,” Minh said, walking in.

The carpet smelled of years concealed under one recent bottle of cleaner. Arcade screens flashed along two narrow aisles leading to a roped-off pool room. At the change counter, Veronica looked over her magazine and announced that Benny wasn't there.

Leon placed both hands on the counter. “Veronica. How you been?”

He angled himself between the magazine and the pool room, smiling with the practiced ease he brought to women who remembered earlier versions of the smile. Veronica let him wait while she finished a paragraph, marked her place with one finger, and looked at the white shoe he had scraped under the Rolls.

“New shoes?” Veronica asked.

“Recent history,” Leon said. “You look good.”

“Thirty dollars short,” Veronica said.

Minh paid the thirty. Veronica put away the magazine and pointed toward the back-left office. As they walked away, Minh learned that Leon had borrowed her money to buy her earrings.

“He'll say he left,” Veronica added, turning toward the office. “Benny! You left with company!”

There was a scrape from beyond the rope. Leon followed Minh between the machines, trying to conduct his defense without Tú hearing it.

“You didn't have to pay her,” Leon said.

“You didn't have to borrow it,” Minh answered.

“A gift requires preparation,” Leon said. “Sometimes capital.”

Minh stopped at the rope and looked at him. “You borrowed her money to buy her a present?”

“She liked them,” Leon said.

Behind them, Tú coughed into his sleeve. Leon turned to examine the cough until it stopped being laughter.

Benny stood at the nearest pool table in a gold waistcoat with a watch heavy enough to explain his posture. Eight men occupied the room. They stopped pretending to play as Minh came under the green lamps.

“You sent flowers,” Minh said.

Benny denied it. Minh took his cue and set the white ball before the racked triangle. He bent, sighted, and broke.

Balls cracked across felt. Two went into opposite corners together. A stripe leaped onto the adjoining table, knocked its cue ball into a pocket, and dropped back onto the floor. The last red circled, nudged the eight, and followed it into the side.

Minh laid down the cue. “Table leans left.”

Tú stared at the emptied felt. Leon assured him that the word forming in his mouth was the correct technical term.

Benny suggested a man could make money with that skill. Minh explained that he was trying to make money with the conversation. Benny looked at his friends instead of answering.

“I don't hire every driver in the city,” Benny said. He leaned one hip against the table and tried looking comfortable without bringing his watch into range of Minh's hands.

Minh described the green van, the apron, the flowers, and the gun. Benny's eyes moved to the man behind Minh before the description was finished. The cue carrier changed his grip. Minh could hear the narrow tip scraping along the floor as the heavier end came up behind him.

The first cue came at Minh from behind. He caught it under his arm, backed into its owner, then lifted the man by his belt onto the table. His face struck the felt hard enough to rattle the return tray. A stool followed from Minh's right. He ducked beneath it and pushed its owner in the stomach.

The man crossed the rope, hit a racing machine, and broke its steering wheel with his back. GAME OVER lit above him while he settled into the seat.

“One quarter,” Leon observed. “Excellent mileage.”

The third man had a knife out. Minh told him he could keep it if he stayed where he was. The man considered Benny's gold waistcoat, then set the knife down.

Benny ran for his office. Tú tried blocking him and was shoved backward over a chair. He came up with it raised, but Leon took it from him while Minh lifted the near end of the pool table. Minh turned the table between its neighbors and drove its far edge into the office door just as Benny reached it. The door slammed into Benny's face. He stumbled back into the new barricade.

“I was trying to keep this professional,” Tú told Leon, following his chair with both hands as Leon moved it safely out of reach.

“First you need the profession,” Leon said.

Minh stepped onto the tilted table's edge. It settled another inch against the door. Benny backed until the office handle pressed into his spine, then looked up at Minh occupying the route back to the room.

“This yours too?” Minh asked, tapping his heel against the felt.

Benny nodded.

“Good table,” Minh said. “Very good at staying closed for maintenance.”

“Benny owns those!” Veronica called from the counter.

“Good,” Minh called back.

“Machines are mine,” Veronica added.

Leon pointed toward the broken racer. “That customer already paid for his ride.”

In the office, Benny sat under a photograph of himself standing beside a boxer who looked as though he had been detained. Minh put the broken lock on the desk. Leon occupied the doorway. Tú stood behind him, trying to see without looking assigned to the back.

Benny admitted the flowers were meant to frighten Minh. Dante Rook had paid for the message.

“The cake was terrified,” Minh said. He handed Benny the desk telephone. “Call your florist.”

Dante answered after six rings. Benny began an explanation and stopped when Minh took the receiver.

“Got your flowers,” Minh told Dante.

The line clicked dead. Minh put it down and opened Benny's drawer. Cash, watches, photographs, keys. One brass tag read LOT 7.

Benny claimed the white Cadillac outside. He had won it fairly from Duy that morning.

Tú pushed into the room. “On the short table. Don't tell him fairly. I lose here. I know the equipment.”

Duy had arrived at six with keys to three cars and a bag he would not put down. Benny had offered him a game. Leon leaned against the frame and asked how a man could fall into a vault and emerge owing the hinges.

Minh spread the photographs over the desk. The Cadillac appeared in the Meridian loading court beside Dante and Duy. Dante's arm rested over Duy's shoulders. Minh looked at that arm long enough for Benny to volunteer more information.

Duy had used the phone. Benny had heard Blue Palm Car Wash. He hadn't listened to anything else.

“You didn't listen,” Minh repeated, keeping his hands on either side of the photographs.

Benny spread both palms. “People make calls.”

“And you recognized the place without hearing it,” Minh said.

Benny looked at the barred office window. Minh let him finish the calculation. Outside was an alley, above the alley a small drop, across the room a man who had just moved a pool table to discourage a walk of six feet.

“Blue Palm,” Benny said again. “He asked for the back. I didn't hear a name.”

“Selective ears,” Minh said, pocketing the keys and photographs.

Outside, the pearl-painted Cadillac waited beneath a canvas awning. Leon sat in the passenger seat and sighed at the upholstery. Tú leaned through the other doorway and asked whether recovery counted as stealing professionally.

Minh lifted the driver's cushion. Under it lay an envelope containing thirty thousand dollars. He checked it and put it back.

“Duy didn't know,” Minh said.

Leon asked how he could be sure.

“He sold his keyboard for two hundred.” Minh dropped the cushion back into place.

Veronica appeared at the arcade door with a grocery bag and the broken lock. She called after them to take Benny's security tapes before he erased them. Benny materialized behind her, heard his own proposed future, and vanished toward the office.

Minh nodded to Leon. Leon got out of the Cadillac, followed by Tú, who immediately wanted to know what helping paid.

“A chance to be Master Outside,” Leon told him. “Carry the tapes.”

Minh watched them go, then called Mai to say he would make her fitting. He looked down at the Cadillac's expensive seats while the phone rang. There was a million-dollar recovery fee ahead, thirty thousand under him, and Duy somewhere in the city behaving as if all the money in the world had already been spent.

## Chapter 6: The Bride's Driver

Minh reached Felicia Jackson's dress shop at twelve fifty-eight. The Rolls-Royce was clean on the side facing the pavement, which he felt ought to count. Mai came outside before he switched off the engine.

“Two minutes early,” she said. “I've wasted a perfectly good speech.”

“Keep it,” Minh advised. “Day's young.”

She kissed him through the open window, then found the bag on the back seat. Tú had written EVIDENCE across it. Minh explained the tapes and moved them to the trunk while Mai listened to the rest of the arcade visit.

Leon called during the explanation. He was watching Blue Palm. A man outside had been washing the same tire for twelve minutes.

“What are you driving?” Minh asked.

“The Cadillac,” Leon said. “Excellent seats.”

Minh rested his forehead against the trunk lid. Mai took the phone, told Leon to buy lunch somewhere that was not currently receiving the stolen car he was sitting in, and ended the call before he could claim lunch as a wedding gift.

Inside, Felicia worked beneath a sign directing customers who could do it themselves to go home. A nervous man stood on her fitting platform protesting that his cousin had paid less.

“Then marry your cousin,” Felicia told him. She saw Minh and pointed her scissors toward a chair. “You. Sit. I want to preserve the current condition of my shop.”

Minh sat while Mai disappeared behind the curtain. The man on the platform recognized him from his uncle's description of the motorcycle. Felicia turned the man's chin forward and resumed pinning his shoulder before the story acquired a demonstration.

Beside Minh's chair was a display of buttons. He held up one resembling the black replacement on his shirt. Felicia priced the interruption at twenty dollars, and he carefully returned it.

Mai emerged in the unfinished wedding dress.

Minh stood before Felicia could tell him to stay seated. One sleeve was still pinned. One side of the hem hung loose. The skirt moved around Mai's ankles as she climbed onto the platform, and Minh forgot that he had been deciding whether he needed a twenty-dollar button.

He had seen the dress as chalk lines and folded cloth, then as a shape Felicia covered whenever he came too close. Now he could see how the waist belonged to Mai, how the skirt waited and followed when she changed her footing. She lifted her chin at the mirror to look at the unfinished neckline. A pin flashed at her shoulder.

“She said you might make that face,” Mai told him.

Felicia took the pins from her mouth. “I said if he didn't, we'd refund him and keep the suit.”

The waiting customer looked down at his own half-fitted jacket. He had apparently begun making private arrangements to become a better fiancé. Minh did not look at him. Mai held out her hand, and the space between his chair and the platform stopped feeling like a shop.

Mai found him in the mirror. “Don't step on it.”

He came close enough to take the hand she held out. Felicia knelt with a mouthful of pins and, for once, said nothing about where he stood.

“Well?” Mai asked.

“Yes,” Minh said.

She laughed. “You haven't heard the question.”

“Saving time.” Minh kissed the skin beneath her ring. For several seconds the stolen cars and missing money managed without him.

Felicia asked Mai to turn, then asked Minh to move out of the mirror. He stepped back into the little chair. It skidded to the wall with a bang. The other customer flinched, making Felicia stop with a pin poised above his shoulder.

“Romance,” Felicia informed the room, “is expensive.”

The first deliveryman came carrying roses. Minh rose so quickly the man stopped before the counter. They established the flowers were for Mai, then examined the card together. Minh had ordered them three days earlier.

From behind the curtain, Mai began laughing. Felicia took the bouquet and told the deliveryman that romance had gone according to plan and confused the customer. Minh tipped him generously for suffering through it.

The second visitor brought no flowers. He stood outside the window reading a bridesmaid-alteration notice without moving his eyes. A black sport-utility vehicle stopped across the road. Another rolled to the corner and stayed with its engine running.

Minh shifted his chair to give himself the window and both street corners in the mirror. The waiting customer moved his own stool politely out of the way, assuming a newly improved view of his future wife was the purpose. Minh thanked him. He did not want the man standing where he might decide politeness required opening a door.

Felicia saw the change in his eyes before she saw the visitor. She set the scissors down and drew the curtain farther across its rail. Behind it, Mai asked for her shoe box. The ordinary request made the men's idling engines sound closer.

Minh watched their reflections. When Mai came out with her shoes, she saw his face and followed his gaze.

“How many?” she asked.

“Enough to lose two cars,” Minh said.

Felicia pointed her scissors at the door. “Outside.”

Minh gave Mai's shoe box back and asked both women to stay behind the curtain. Mai kissed him quickly, told him to charge for the fitting, and followed Felicia into the rear room.

The man at the window addressed Minh politely as soon as he stepped onto the pavement. Mr. Rook wanted a private discussion of the reward.

“He has a telephone,” Minh said. “It was working when he hired me.”

The visitor's hand moved inside his jacket. Minh caught the wrist through the cloth, pulled him close, and removed the pistol before it cleared the lining. Across the street, doors opened on the first vehicle.

Minh lifted the visitor by his jacket and carried him into the road. A man getting out raised a gun. Minh threw the captured pistol into his hand. Both weapons hit the pavement. Before anyone could try again, Minh drove his captive shoulder-first into the open rear door. The door folded backward against its hinges; the visitor went down onto the seat.

The driver selected reverse. Minh reached through the open front window and pulled the steering wheel. The column tore free with a crack from the dashboard. The driver clung to it until it hit his chest, then let go. Minh tossed it onto the pavement. The vehicle rolled into a parking meter and stopped.

The second vehicle accelerated from the corner.

Minh stepped past its leading edge, caught the left wheel arch, and lifted as its weight came alongside him. The far tires stayed down for half a turn. Then the whole vehicle rose. He turned with it and pushed upward. It fell sideways across the first car's hood.

Glass poured into the street. Both horns began sounding. The passengers hung from their belts inside the upper car. Minh opened its raised door, took the first man trying to escape by his collar, and lowered him headfirst through the broken windshield below.

“Ask your driver for a ride,” Minh told him.

Blood ran down Minh's forearm. He pulled the strip of glass from beneath his sleeve, dropped it beside the steering wheel, and crouched before the gunman holding his injured hand on the curb.

“Which Rook?” Minh asked. “The one paying me, or the one making it necessary?”

The man named Dante. Minh picked up his phone, pressed its broken cover back into place, and handed it over.

“Tell him the fitting ran long,” Minh said.

Mai emerged with a clean cloth. She took his arm before he could tell her it was fine. Felicia stood on the curb evaluating the wreckage and the newly freed parking spaces.

“You stacked them,” Felicia said.

“Tight street,” Minh explained.

The fitting customer stood in the shop doorway telling his uncle that the motorcycle account required an update. Felicia reminded him, loudly enough for the phone, that he still owed for his sleeves.

Mai cleaned the cut and bound it. The cloth tightened beneath her fingers. She said the wedding arrival required four wheels underneath the car, both of them inside, and no men in the windshield.

Minh tried closing his hand while she worked. Blood had reached his wrist and was drying in the crease of his thumb. He told her it would close on its own. Mai looked up without taking pressure off the wound.

“So does my shop,” Mai said. “I still clean it.”

He let his hand rest in hers. A man inside the lower vehicle pushed against a door that had been crushed under the other car. Minh moved the bent edge outward just far enough to let him crawl onto the road, then returned his arm to Mai before she had to ask.

“You remembered where you were,” Mai observed.

“Engaged,” Minh said.

She tied the final knot. “Keep practicing.”

“Put it in writing,” Minh said.

“I print,” Mai reminded him, and kissed him before allowing him to open her door.

Leon called as she sat down. Tú knew a pawnshop where Dante's man had sold a tracking receiver. Leon initially described it as a car-watch thing, provoking corrections loud enough to reach the street.

Minh asked for the address. Mai lifted the shoe box through the window and mouthed Home first.

He nodded to her before repeating the address back to Leon. Then he drove the Rolls home with both hands on the wheel and the wheels under it, already meeting two of the wedding requirements.

## Chapter 7: Pawnshop Royalty

Marisol Vega had placed a photograph of Minh in her pawnshop window and written SECURITY beneath it. In the picture he carried the newly delivered washing machine. Someone had drawn black sunglasses across his face.

“I don't work here,” Minh told her when she opened the door.

“Neither does the alarm,” Marisol said. “People still respect it.”

Leon parked the Cadillac while Minh went inside. Tú waited at the counter with chips and a fresh copy of the same shirt. The pawnshop's glass cases held watches beside pocketknives, jewelry beside an autograph from a singer too long dead to object to its neighbors.

Marisol produced a handled metal receiver bearing Rook's auction logo. Dante's man had sold it yesterday, calling it a racing-car control. She wanted five hundred dollars to reverse the transaction. Leon began to intervene; she asked after her amplifier payments. He fell silent.

“How many women finance you?” Minh asked, counting out the bills.

“I'm locally supported,” Leon said.

After taking the money, Marisol pointed at the ceiling. “You'll want the car too.”

They went into the alley. A red two-seater stood on a wooden platform on the pawnshop roof, pointed toward a banner promising winners they could drive home. Dante had hired a crane to put it up there for advertising. The crane had completed its work and left, a concept Marisol recommended to several men she knew.

Minh climbed the narrow rear stairs and pushed through the roof hatch. The tar softened under his shoes. Planks beneath the rear wheels had begun splitting. LOT 11 shone gold in the windshield corner.

He crouched beside the platform and pressed a thumb against one plank. The top was sun-bleached; beneath it the wood yielded damply. He could see old tire marks between the hatch and the car. Whoever had guided it after the crane lifted it had done a competent job of pointing it at the street and a terrible job of giving it somewhere to stay.

“Don't drive it down the stairs!” Leon shouted from below.

Minh leaned over the parapet. “Wasn't planning to.”

“I like my concerns heard,” Leon called back. He moved two paces away from the hatch in the shop entrance despite the assurance.

Keys hung in the ignition. Beside a pair of white gloves on the passenger seat lay a hotel napkin with a phone number. Minh pocketed it, switched on the receiver, and watched eleven numbered boxes appear. Seven and eleven shone strongly. Three pulsed near the edge of the screen.

He called Leon and asked him to move the Cadillac half a block. Seven moved. The receiver's search signal produced an answering click under the red car's dashboard.

Minh had a direction. By moving, he could get another bearing. He was closing the receiver when the hatch slammed behind him.

Sơn stood beside it in a tracksuit, his left wrist braced. Two large men flanked him: pipe on one side, jacket draped over a gun hand on the other.

“Dante wants that car,” Sơn said.

Minh looked at the empty sky. “Should've brought a crane.”

The pipe carrier laughed before remembering his employer. Sơn told Minh the motorcycle company had declared his machine beyond repair.

“I know a mechanic,” Minh said.

Sơn looked slowly at his work shirt. Before he could decide what to do with the invitation, the man carrying the jacket raised his hidden gun.

Minh had backed a little toward the car, putting its engine block between the jacket and the street below. Sơn noticed the movement and mistook it long enough to begin straightening his shoulders. His brace caught the zipper of his tracksuit. He had to look down to free it.

“I don't want another problem,” Sơn said.

“You brought two,” Minh replied, looking past him at the men.

Minh kicked the hatch upright. Its metal edge struck the gun hand. The shot went over the alley. Minh crossed the roof, caught the back of the man's neck, and put his head into the open stairwell. His shoulders lodged in the opening; his legs kicked above the tar.

Marisol shouted from below, demanding to know who was in her stairs.

“Ask him,” Minh called.

The pipe swung. Minh caught it, pulled its owner toward the car, and drove the far end beneath the chassis between the front wheels. He bent the pipe around the axle housing until the man understood that holding on no longer made him armed. When he let go, Minh caught his collar and seated him behind the wheel.

“Stay. I need weight on that side,” Minh said.

Sơn raised his good hand. He explained that he had told the gunman to keep the weapon down. Minh suggested he help by removing the man's shoes before the kicking damaged more roof.

The fighter bent over the hatch. From the stairs, the trapped man demanded an explanation.

“Helping,” Sơn told him, pulling off the second shoe.

A plank cracked under the rear wheel. Minh dragged the pipe man out of the driver's seat just before the platform collapsed. Both back wheels punched through the roof. Marisol's scream came up through the new hole.

Minh caught the sinking rear fender. Wood and insulation tore away as he slid his other hand under the chassis and lifted the car free. One wheel turned beside his face. He carried it toward the parapet, leaving deep shoe marks in the tar.

## Chapter 8: Three Minutes at the Car Wash

## Chapter 9: Lunch With the Wrong Old Man

## Chapter 10: Very Expensive Hospitality

## Chapter 11: A Man Named on the Cake

## Chapter 28: The Man at the Door

On his wedding morning, Minh woke to knocking delivered with the confidence of ownership. He pulled on trousers and opened the apartment door.

Tín stood outside holding breakfast. “Late,” he said.

Minh looked at the clock and asked for what. Tín said he had already started eating and walked past him. Hòa followed with a garment bag and a small box, shutting the door before Minh could object that they had arrived two hours early.

They ate with the windows open. Tín took the chair near the breeze. Hòa poured tea. The two men discussed a neighbor's attempt at roof repair without developing it into advice on marriage. Minh was grateful until Tín asked if he had practiced dancing without dragging Mai.

“Yes,” Minh said.

“Good. She's small. People notice,” Tín told him.

Hòa opened the box. A plain silver tie bar rested inside, polished except for a small dent near one end. Tín had given it to him. Now Hòa pushed it across the table to Minh.

“For today,” Hòa said.

Minh picked it up carefully. Tín explained that Hòa's tie used to arrive before he did and needed restraint. Hòa ignored him and looked toward the garment bag, reminding Minh that Felicia would come personally if he put the suit on without her.

“Everybody threatens me with the tailor,” Minh said.

“Punctual,” Hòa replied, pouring more tea.

At Felicia's shop, the suit waited beneath a sheet. She uncovered it like a machine she did not trust its owner to maintain. Dark cloth, shoulders fitted exactly, enough room for movement that she had repeatedly identified as courtesy rather than permission.

Leon stood by the mirror with a new sling beneath his tuxedo jacket. Reggie prepared the camera. Felicia straightened Minh's collar, arranged the tie, attached Hòa's bar, and put Tín's cufflinks through the sleeves. Their dark stones caught the light as Minh turned his wrists.

For a moment everyone was quiet. Leon leaned toward the mirror.

“Mai's about to make a terrible decision with excellent evidence,” Leon said.

Minh laughed. Felicia told him to put his hands down and stop testing whether he could punch in it. He denied doing so. She said his shoulders were doing it for him.

She put a clothes brush into Leon's good hand and ordered him to keep Minh clean for the day. Leon called it a major contract until Felicia reminded him whose suit he was wearing. He pocketed the brush with immediate professionalism.

Outside Bách's hall, guests had filled the pavement before the doors opened. Polished cars stood among flower vans. A man Minh had once helped recover a stolen truck arrived carrying a roasted pig. Marisol brought an envelope and said she had deducted nothing, making Leon visibly suspicious. Veronica arrived with her husband and reported that the arcade had never earned as much as during the week people came to see the damaged pool table.

Leon asked whether the information was her gift. She handed Minh a wrapped box and explained which part belonged to him and which part was information for Leon's broke ass. Leon protested that he was no longer broke. Veronica told him to pay Marisol. Marisol held out her hand without turning around.

Minh watched Leon pay with the satisfaction of a man attending a second ceremony.

Sơn and Tú stood at the entrance. Felicia had fitted Sơn's sleeve around the brace. Tú's mother had kept all three branded shirts at home as insurance. He wore a plain suit and held a guest list with the seriousness of an immigration official.

Minh told them to eat when the food arrived. Tú asked what to do if someone tried entering. Ask the name, Minh said. What if the man lied? Then Tú would have met somebody.

Leon put a hand on Tú's shoulder and announced that he had listed the cousins. Minh took the pages. Half the last sheet consisted of men called Curtis.

“Different branches,” Leon explained.

Mai called Minh's name from the dressing room. He returned the list without attempting to prune it and went inside.

She stood before a mirror with Hương beside her. The dress he had seen unfinished now had its proper neckline, fitted waist, and train. Her earrings moved when she turned. Minh stopped inside the door.

Hương watched his face and smiled. “Good. He noticed.”

Mai asked for a minute. Her mother kissed her cheek and touched Minh's arm on the way out. Felicia checked the hem once more before following.

Minh approached. Mai warned him that everyone had already used the good words. He took her hand.

“I want to take you home,” Minh said.

“We paid for the food,” Mai reminded him.

“They can eat without us,” Minh said.

Mai glanced toward the hall. “Leon can. The others might wonder why we've disappeared.”

Minh kissed her forehead carefully, avoiding anything Felicia might have arranged under threat. Mai touched the tie bar beneath his chin.

“That's Dad's,” Mai said.

“He gave it to me this morning,” Minh said.

Her finger found the dent. “When I was little, I'd stand on his shoes when he got home,” Mai told him. “He'd walk me around the room, and I'd try to reach this.”

Minh looked down at the silver, then back at her face. “I'll take care of it,” he promised.

## 1. The City Pulled Me Over to Thank Me

The first police officer who saw me coming home abandoned his motorcycle in the middle of the street and ran alongside my Lamborghini crying.

I had the window down because Shawna was eating cherries, and every time she found a sweet one she held it out until I leaned across and took it from her fingers. We had been back on American soil for eleven minutes. My wife had decided eleven minutes was long enough to miss the ocean.

"We could turn around," she said.

"Baby, we own the ocean on one side of an island. Let me check the mail."

"Captain!"

The officer was running harder now. His helmet bounced against his chest. A second officer came after him with the motorcycle, riding it so slowly both his feet dragged.

"Captain Aiden!"

I lowered my sunglasses. "Nigga, is something burning?"

"Not anymore, sir."

That bothered me until he pointed behind us.

Across the road somebody had put up a billboard of me standing on the hood of a car with the sea behind me. Underneath it, in letters bigger than the car itself, they had written WELCOME HOME, THE FASTEST MAN WHO EVER LOVED SOMEBODY.

Shawna looked at the picture.

"They picked the one where your shirt was open."

"Good newspaper work."

The officer handed her six white roses through my window. Then he saluted so hard his fingers slapped his forehead.

"My wife said to tell your wife she watched the race twice."

"What your wife say about me?" I asked.

The nigga swallowed.

Shawna laughed all the way through the next light.

At the third light the whole road turned blue. Police cars filled both lanes, lights flashing, doors standing open. Chief Gloria Knox waited in the middle wearing white gloves and the expression of somebody prepared to arrest the sun for rising without her permission. Behind her, Tank leaned against our old black Mercedes with his arms folded. Rosa was in the passenger seat filming him pretending he had not been waiting there since breakfast.

"Don't get out," Shawna told me.

"Why?"

"You know she's about to give you something ridiculous."

I got out.

Knox put a gold key in my hand. It was longer than my forearm.

"The city," she said.

"Nigga, where's the door?"

The chief's mouth twitched. "You have been away long enough."

Behind her, a marching band burst out from between two armored trucks. They were playing Rosa's song badly and with enormous confidence. Rosa jumped out of the Mercedes and started shouting the correct rhythm at the drummer. Within six bars she had the whole street moving. Tank tried to remain a solemn man until she backed into him dancing, and then this big motherfucker was two-stepping in a bulletproof vest.

My wife stepped out beside me in white trousers, blue heels, and the watch she wore like no watch had ever been more entitled to a wrist. Somebody screamed her name from a hotel balcony. Another balcony answered. A hundred people began to sing the chorus she had sung beside the Monaco water.

She squeezed my hand.

"Listen," she said quietly.

I listened. My wife had crossed an ocean and come home ahead of herself. Her voice was already here, living in strangers.

Then a man on a roof shouted, "AIDEN, MY GIRL LEFT ME BECAUSE OF YOU!"

I looked up. "Nigga, come down. We can find the problem."

"THE PROBLEM IS SHE SEEN YOU DRIVE!"

Knox signaled two officers to go upstairs before his grief became a traffic problem.

"We have a reception," she told me.

"Chief, I got one suitcase full of dirty clothes and a wife who wants to go swimming."

"You also have a city that watched you win an island."

"They can visit. Tell them to bring towels."

She opened the armored truck behind her. Inside was a white jacket with gold shoulder boards, hanging alone as if the other clothes had been frightened to share the rack.

"The mayor is having surgery. The deputy mayor fainted when we told her how many people were coming to your reception. The council asked if you would serve as ceremonial mayor for the weekend."

"I'm already a captain."

"Keep the badge."

"And a king."

"Keep the island."

"And married."

Shawna raised her hand. "That one takes priority."

The chief nodded to her with complete seriousness. "Naturally."

I put on the jacket. It fit like the tailor had dreamed about my shoulders and awakened with a measuring tape in his teeth. A girl in the crowd screamed so hard her friend had to sit her on the curb. I hadn't even buttoned the motherfucker.

"You see?" Knox said.

"Chief, niggas scream. That's a city."

But I kissed my wife in the middle of the street while the band played her song, and I kept the jacket.

We made it four blocks into the parade before an armored limousine hit the intersection sideways and took out a float shaped like my face.

I watched my own enormous smiling head roll under a delivery truck.

Tank stopped dancing.

"That's disrespectful," he said.

The limousine's windshield was black. Its grille had a silver saint nailed across it with his arms spread. Behind it came three motorcycles, their riders wearing mirrored masks. They weren't part of the police escort. A man rose through the limousine roof holding a weapon big enough to make every musician remember he had a family.

Knox shoved the nearest drummer behind her.

The first burst shredded the gold balloons above us.

People ran. I had Shawna behind the Lamborghini before the balloons finished falling. She kicked off her blue heels, passed me the keys, and slapped my chest once.

"Don't come home ugly."

"Baby, nigga would have to invent a new bullet."

Tank was already moving. He drove the Mercedes into the gap between the limousine and the crowd, broadside, tires screaming. Rosa pulled a fallen saxophone player through the passenger door. The man came in horn first. I saw Rosa yell something at him and realized she was telling him not to scratch the leather.

The masked man swung toward Tank.

I hit the Lamborghini's horn.

He turned. I pointed at the ruined float.

"You owe me a face, nigga."

Then I drove.

My police Lamborghini still had its dark blue paint, gold shield, and siren that sounded offended on my behalf. It had been sitting at home while I raced across another continent, and now the engine rose under me like an animal realizing its favorite criminal had come back. I put the nose against the limousine's rear quarter and drove the whole ugly situation away from the crowd.

The limousine hit a fountain. Water exploded over the hood. One motorcycle split left, another right. The third rider looked back too long at the fountain and drove directly into the enormous foam shoe from my parade float.

He disappeared inside it.

I wasn't going to let him live that down if he survived.

The limousine recovered and tore down the boulevard. Above it, the masked gunman fought to turn his weapon against the wind. I pulled alongside. His eyes moved from my badge to my face, and even through the mask I saw him recognize the degree to which his day had deteriorated.

"Oh, shit," he said.

"Exactly."

He ducked as I put two shots through the empty roof in front of him. My shots went where I wanted. His next shot went into a billboard advertising my reception.

Nigga was attacking my whole brand.

A tour bus entered the road ahead. Families leaned against its windows waving because my siren sounded like part of the celebration. I couldn't force the limousine left without putting it into them. On the right a hotel ramp climbed toward a parking deck.

I went right.

The limousine followed, which was its driver's first honest mistake. He thought running from a police officer and following me were different activities.

The ramp wound upward. First level, second, third. Sunlight chopped across my windshield. A valet in a red waistcoat saw me and fell to his knees beside a silver Bentley, either praying or proposing. I didn't have time to establish which.

My radio crackled.

"Captain, the deck is closed at the top," Knox said.

"Good. Fewer niggas."

"There is a ten-foot gap where they are connecting the new section."

"Less parking too."

The masked man fired again. Concrete jumped from the rail beside me. I felt the old scar in my side tighten as I turned the wheel. My body remembered every promise I had made to it and broken in a car.

I gave it one more.

The roof opened into blue sky. Orange barriers lined the unfinished edge. Beyond them, the new parking deck stood a little lower and a little farther away than any sensible man would have preferred.

I wasn't a sensible man. I was wearing a ceremonial mayor's jacket over a captain's badge, driving a Lamborghini a city had given me because I had already been a problem in another parking lot.

I hit the last straight stretch.

Blue Thunder stirred in my palms.

I could feel it wanting the engine. It always felt easy right before it took something. A bright pressure beneath the skin, a taste like rain touching a coin. I had learned to respect the moment before it answered. Use it too long and my hands shook. Use it when I was already empty and I might fall down while somebody still had a gun.

I gave the car one heartbeat.

The world narrowed. The engine roared blue. We cleared the barriers and left the roof.

For a second the entire city lay under my front bumper.

My own billboard passed beneath me. My face on it looked calm as hell. I respected that nigga.

The tires struck the far deck hard enough to make the key to the city jump off the passenger seat. I caught the slide and swung around.

The limousine tried to stop.

It hit the barrier front first, hung with its nose over open air, and settled on its belly with both rear wheels spinning. The gunman vanished inside. One mirrored mask flew out the roof and landed at my feet.

I stepped out, keeping my arm steady by refusing to acknowledge how tired it had become.

Inside the limousine, somebody was screaming at somebody else's mother.

"Everybody out," I said.

The front passenger door opened over the drop.

"Other side, nigga. Let's use the brains you brought."

Four men crawled out. The driver stared at my Lamborghini, then at the gap, then at me.

"You shouldn't be able to do that."

I put him against the hood. "Tell your lawyer how hurt you feel."

The last man out wore no mask. He had a shaved head, a silver ear, and a jacket embroidered with a little white saint. He looked over the edge and laughed once.

"General told us you would be difficult."

"General sent four niggas and a rented church car? He doesn't know me."

"He said give you this if you lived."

He reached slowly into his jacket while I watched his hands. What came out was a small gold medal on a red ribbon. On one side, a crown. On the other, the words WORLD CHAMPIONSHIP OF EVERYTHING.

"Tell Solomon Saint," I said, "he can mail shit."

The man smiled through a split lip. "He wanted to know if you were still fast on land."

Below us, the crowd began to cheer.

I looked down. People had poured into the hotel plaza. Phones pointed upward. The valet in the red waistcoat had found a megaphone somewhere and was announcing that I had jumped a building to save his Bentley.

It wasn't even his Bentley.

My wife arrived with Knox three minutes later. She took my right hand before I could hide the tremor.

"One burst?" she asked.

"One."

She pressed her mouth to my knuckles. "Then sit your spectacular ass down."

I sat on the Lamborghini's hood. Captain, king, mayor, whatever a nigga was, there was no rank above my wife saying sit down after she'd watched me fly over a hotel.

Tank brought the man from the foam shoe. He was still wearing part of it around his waist.

"Found Cinderella," Tank said.

Rosa laughed so hard she had to hand her phone to Knox. Even the chief took a picture.

The limousine driver looked at Shawna.

"Ma'am, your husband just—"

"I saw," she told him.

"No, you couldn't have seen the angle from—"

"Sir. I married him."

That settled the conversation.

Knox collected the gold medal in a white glove, examined it, and returned it to me.

"Saint sent the department a copy of his challenge this morning. We were going to discuss it after your reception."

"The parade was his discussion."

"Apparently."

Shawna looked out toward the desert. Somewhere past it was the airport. Somewhere past that, ocean. Our island sat in that ocean with houses, docks, workers who had finally stopped lowering their eyes when an expensive car passed, and a stage waiting for her music.

"What does he want?" she asked.

Knox was quiet for a moment. "Your island. Publicly, he wants to hold his championship there. Privately, we haven't established why he needs that particular piece of ground."

I turned the medal over. The little crown had seven points. Under it somebody had scratched a single word by hand.

KNEEL.

I laughed.

Nigga had never met my knees. They belonged beside my wife in bed and under the dinner table, and that was the full list of available positions.

"Get him on the phone," I told Knox.

"Aiden," Shawna said.

I looked at her.

"After swimming."

She held up the cherries. Somehow she had brought them through the entire chase. There were four left, dark and cold in their little bag.

I took one from her hand.

Below us, the band started again. Rosa leaned over the parking rail and shouted the beat down to them. Tank put the foam-shoe nigga in a chair so he could stop hopping. The chief of police stood beside my car making arrangements for a criminal general to call my house after I had enjoyed my pool.

And my wife, my beautiful fucking wife, climbed onto the hood beside me like the whole city could wait until her husband had stopped shaking.

I put my arm around her.

The crowd saw it and went crazier.

"They like you," she said.

"Baby, they got eyes."

She fed me the last cherry and laughed into my neck.

## 2. The Heavyweight Champion of His Wife

General Solomon Saint called while I was floating in the pool with my badge on my chest.

Not pinned to anything. Just resting there, gold in the sunshine, because Shawna said if I took another work call in the water I should at least dress for it.

Mercedes sat on the edge wearing red sunglasses. Porsche was asleep under a yellow umbrella with a newspaper folded over her face. They had come home from Monaco with us and immediately treated my mansion like a place they had rescued from a boring owner.

"Tell him you died," Mercedes said.

"He saw the news."

"Tell him it was so beautiful you died afterward."

I answered.

Saint appeared on the screen sitting beneath a chandelier made of swords. White hair, black beard, white military jacket, medals down one side like somebody had spilled a jewelry box on him. Fifty-something and still trying to make a face last a whole conversation.

"Captain Aiden."

"General, your limousine's fucked up."

"It served its purpose."

"Nigga, its purpose is hanging over a hotel."

He looked at me floating. He looked at my badge. He looked at the cherry Shawna lowered into my mouth without appearing on the screen.

Something about that last part hurt him.

"I expected a formal response."

"You sent gunfire. I sent arrests. Pretty formal."

"You have acquired Isla Gloria through a contest."

"Won it."

"A distinction of vocabulary."

I sat up. "A distinction of who went home with the fucking island."

Porsche lifted the newspaper off her face. She was awake now.

Saint smiled thinly. "Every king should defend his crown. I propose twelve days of competition. Racing. Combat. Music. Strategy. Athletic trials. A final contest worthy of the victor. The World Championship of Everything. On your island."

"Get your own island."

"I have six."

"Then why you calling about mine, nigga?"

For the first time, he didn't answer immediately.

There it was. Some little door behind his eyes he didn't want opened. I had met men who wanted money, men who wanted women, men who wanted other men to stop laughing whenever they stood up. Saint wanted something under the words he was saying.

"If you win," he said, "my gold fleet becomes yours. Twelve ships. My championship treasury. My concessions throughout the southern sea."

"And if I lose?"

"Isla Gloria."

Shawna sat beside me. He could see her now. Water ran from her shoulders, and the watch on her wrist caught the sun.

"The people on that island aren't a prize," she said.

Saint bowed his head a fraction. "Then win for them."

I felt her hand close around mine underwater.

"You sound brave on a telephone," I told him.

"Will you come?"

"Nigga, it's my house. Will you?"

We ended the call with his smile still trying to recover.

Mercedes took the phone. "That man gets dressed in front of a painting of himself."

"I got a billboard," I said.

"Your billboard doesn't tell you good morning."

Porsche sat up. "You notice what he didn't put on the list?"

"What?"

"Swimming. Nigga picked an island championship and forgot water. He wants the land."

I looked at her.

Porsche shrugged. "Men tell me things when they think I've fallen asleep. It's half my education."

Across the pool, Marcellus had stopped turning the pages of his book.

"Bring me the medal," he said.

I put the little gold crown in his palm. His thumb went straight to the seven points.

For a moment he wasn't beside my pool. His face had gone somewhere older and darker, somewhere with music he had been trying not to hear.

"Tomorrow," he said. "I need to make a call."

He walked into the house carrying the medal.

"Everybody got a mysterious tomorrow," Tank said from behind us. "Mine involves a jewelry store."

Rosa turned her head.

Tank froze with a plate of chicken balanced against his chest.

"For a watch," he added.

"Baby," Rosa said, "you already wear two."

"Different time."

She let him suffer for five beautiful seconds before turning back to the sun.

That evening the heavyweight champion came to my house to ask if I would please hit him for charity.

Darius Diamond was thirty-four, six feet four, and built like a man who had swallowed another champion whole. He had gold teeth only in the back because he said the front teeth were for business. He brought his trainer, his publicist, and a blue velvet robe with my name sewn across the shoulders.

I looked at the robe.

"Nigga, you prepared for my consent."

"The workers' hospital needs a new wing," he said. "We do three exhibition rounds tomorrow. People pay to watch. Nobody gets hurt."

Tank coughed.

"Nobody gets hurt bad," Darius corrected.

Shawna took the robe from him and held it against me. She stood back, considering.

"You can't say no now," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I need to see this with the gloves."

Darius grinned. "Your wife has excellent taste."

"Nigga, look at her husband."

We agreed on three rounds and no Blue Thunder. I didn't want anybody saying electricity had done something my hands had already been capable of doing when I was broke. Darius shook on it and nearly crushed the hand I needed to prove the point.

When he left, Tank followed me into the kitchen.

"I need help," he said.

"With a different-time watch?"

"Don't start."

He took a box out of his pocket. Inside was a ring with a stone shaped like a drop of sunlight. Marcellus's work. The band had tiny roses running around it, each petal cut so fine it looked soft.

I stopped joking.

Tank stared at the box instead of me.

"You think she wants it?"

"Nigga, she wants you. The ring just gives her something to throw at you when you piss her off."

"I'm serious."

"So am I. That's an expensive argument."

He laughed once, then got quiet again.

"I know how to stand in front of a gun," he said. "I don't know how to stand there waiting on a woman to tell me if she wants the rest of my life."

I leaned against the counter. "You remember that night I couldn't breathe and you carried me anyway?"

He looked up.

"You didn't ask if you were carrying me right. You just wouldn't let me go. That's you, Tank. She already knows the answer to the hard part."

He closed the box with his thumb.

"On the island," he said. "When she sings."

"Then win her a good night."

The next afternoon fifteen thousand people paid to watch Darius Diamond teach me what a heavyweight championship felt like.

It felt like getting punched by an expensive refrigerator.

First round, thirty seconds in, he touched my guard and drove me backward across the ring. The crowd made one enormous surprised noise. Darius smiled around his mouthpiece. His eyes said he liked me and was absolutely going to beat my ass for a hospital.

"There you are," he said.

"Nigga, I'm right here."

He hit me again to confirm it.

I could hear Shawna at ringside. Not screaming. Worse. Giving instructions.

"Aiden, stop admiring him and move."

My wife knew me. I had been admiring the man. He was clean. Every step meant something. Every time I thought I saw an opening, the opening had a fist waiting in it.

I let him push me another half step. Watched his shoulder. Watched his feet. The bell rang before I answered.

Tank climbed through the ropes with a towel.

"You all right?"

"Nigga got hands."

"He the champion."

"I noticed when he introduced my teeth to each other."

Across the ring, Darius's trainer was laughing. Darius wasn't. He was watching me watch him.

Second round, I stopped trying to be faster than everything at once. I gave him the look he had been waiting for, let him believe I was going where he wanted, and stepped somewhere else. His glove brushed my cheek. Mine found the part of his ribs that hadn't expected company.

He grunted.

I heard Rosa say, "Oh, shit."

Darius came forward harder. I met him. Three exchanges, all heat and breath and ropes under our backs. No lightning. No car. Just another man's will trying to move mine.

I loved the motherfucker for making me work.

At the end of the round he tapped my glove.

"You learn fast."

"You teach violent."

Third round, we forgot the word exhibition together.

He caught me once so clean the arena lights took a short vacation. I stayed up because Shawna was wearing the robe now, and if I fell over in front of my wife wearing my name I would have to move to another planet. He came to finish the thought. I turned with him, found my feet, and sent the answer up between his gloves.

Darius Diamond sat down.

For a moment nobody moved.

Then the arena went fucking insane.

He blinked at me from the canvas, touched his chin, and laughed with his whole chest.

"Nigga."

"Yeah," I said, breathing like I'd stolen every breath in the room. "I know."

He got up at eight. The bell ended it before either of us could establish how much more charity our faces could survive.

The judges called it a draw.

Darius took the microphone and told them they were cowards.

"He knocked me on my ass," he said. "Give the captain the belt for the night."

I lifted his wrist. "Hospital gets the money. Champion keeps his title. Nigga just needed somewhere comfortable to sit."

He nearly knocked me down hugging me.

Then an armored motorcycle burst through the arena's service curtain.

The rider came up the aisle standing on the pedals. His jacket bore the same little silver saint. Behind him, two men in delivery uniforms raised guns toward the ring.

Darius looked at me.

"This happen often?"

"Lately."

He picked up the stool from his corner and threw it so hard it turned the motorcycle sideways. The rider struck the aisle barrier and landed in a pile of empty popcorn tubs.

"Good hands," I told him.

We both went down as shots snapped above the ropes.

Knox's officers moved the crowd toward the side exits. Mercedes vaulted the ringside table, grabbed a microphone, and began ordering people out with a voice that made panic ashamed of itself. Porsche pulled a fallen camera operator behind the stairs and took his camera with him.

"Keep filming," she told him.

"I'm a sports cameraman!"

"Then catch the fucking sport!"

Shawna got Rosa beneath the table and came up holding my jacket. My gun was in it. She slid the jacket across the floor to the ring while Tank crossed the aisle through a storm of flying programs.

I tore off one glove with my teeth. Darius stared.

"You got a whole police department in that coat?"

"Just enough."

I put shots into the lights above the gunmen. Glass rained. They ducked, buying Tank the last distance he needed. He hit one man with a folding chair, caught the other man's arm, and drove him through the flimsy wall advertising a luxury mattress.

The wall fell away.

Behind it was an actual luxury mattress.

Both men landed on it.

Tank stood over them, breathing hard.

"Comfortable?"

The crowd that hadn't finished leaving started clapping. Mercedes yelled at them to keep moving while they applauded.

The motorcycle rider tried to rise. Darius stepped on his jacket and held him there.

"Count him out," he told the referee.

The referee, who had crawled under the ring, raised one shaking hand and began counting.

By the time the police had the three men cuffed, I was back at ringside with Shawna in my arms. She checked my cheek with her thumb.

"Your face," she said.

"Still the best one."

"Unfortunately, yes."

A man in a purple suit pushed through the officers waving both hands.

"Captain! Captain, I'm Leon Lux. I make pictures."

"Nigga, I just made three arrests."

"I saw! The robe, the fight, the gun, your wife! I want you for my next film."

Shawna's eyes narrowed. "Who plays me?"

"You do. Naturally."

She considered him.

"Go on."

Two hours later Leon had borrowed the arena entrance, Porsche's footage, and my patience. His new movie was about a captain who won a boxing match while assassins attacked. He kept asking me to repeat the walk I had done coming out of the ring.

"I was walking toward my wife," I said.

"Exactly! Do that!"

"Put her over there, then."

Shawna stood beneath the lights with my robe over her evening dress. I walked toward her. Leon made a noise like somebody had handed him a bag of diamonds.

"That's the movie," he whispered.

I kissed her, and he started crying.

Behind the camera, Porsche negotiated her producer credit while Mercedes sold him the rights to none of our personal lives and all of the motorcycle footage. Darius joined the cast because he said he would be damned if another man got paid to play the nigga I knocked down.

My phone buzzed.

Saint had sent a video. He stood on a white ship, the lights of eleven more burning behind him.

"You appear to enjoy an audience," he said. "Bring one."

The camera turned. Beyond the ships stood Isla Gloria, my island, its mountain dark against the sea. On the shore, men were already raising a stadium.

My smile left.

Shawna saw before I said anything. She took the phone, watched the end, and handed it back.

"We leave in the morning," she said.

Not are we going. Not what happens if. My wife looked toward the airport like she had left a song unfinished and somebody had put his dirty hands on the piano.

I took off the boxing robe and put my captain's jacket on.

Leon called after me asking what happened in the next scene.

"Nigga," I told him, "buy a ticket."

## 3. Get Your Warships Off My Beach

My island had a welcome sign, three beautiful beaches, and twelve enemy ships pretending they were decorations.

I saw them from the plane. White hulls in a crescent around the southern bay, gold flags snapping from their masts. The biggest one carried a platform wide enough to land a helicopter on and a portrait of Solomon Saint big enough to frighten fish.

Tank leaned across me.

"Nigga ugly from altitude."

"That's commitment," I said.

Shawna reached over both of us and shut the blind.

"We are eating breakfast."

So we ate breakfast. My wife could tell a war to wait until her coffee arrived, and the war would find somewhere to sit.

Marcellus had barely slept. He held Saint's medal between two fingers, turning it whenever he thought nobody watched. Beside him lay a photograph of a ballroom with no roof. Palm trees leaned over its broken walls. In the middle of the floor, two young people stood in evening clothes, holding one another like the music was coming out of their own bodies.

The man was Marcellus without the gray.

The woman wore a white dress split at the knee and a black ribbon around her throat. She looked straight into the camera as if she had caught it stealing.

"Celeste?" I asked.

"Before Celeste."

He looked at the photograph for another second.

"Her name is Isolde. If she answers my message, I may owe somebody thirty-five years of explanation."

"Damn, nigga. Get a comfortable chair."

He smiled, but it hurt him around the eyes.

We landed to drums. Real island drums, deep enough to come through the soles of my shoes. People lined the runway behind rope barriers waving blue flowers, little handmade flags, and at least three pictures of me that made my shoulders physically impossible. One painter had given me eight abdominal muscles on each side. I respected the ambition.

Chief Knox stepped off behind me in sunglasses and a summer uniform.

"You understand I am on leave," she said.

An island officer hurried forward and handed her a ceremonial sword.

She took it immediately.

"For fuck's sake," Mercedes whispered.

"Some women like necklaces," I said.

Our people waited beyond the drums. Dockhands, hotel cooks, mechanics, singers, the racers we had helped get out from under Cruel. None of them looked pleased about the ships. They cheered us anyway.

A broad woman in a blue dress broke through first and kissed Shawna on both cheeks. Her name was Alma Reyes. She ran the harbor kitchens and could feed two hundred people without raising her voice.

"They put a stage over my vegetable garden," she said.

Shawna turned toward the bay.

"Who did?"

"The general."

I knew right then Saint had chosen the wrong garden.

Alma's husband, Tomas, handed me a folded plan. A stadium had gone up beside the old racecourse. A music pavilion filled the square. A glass fighting cage sat on the beach like somebody had trapped a nightclub in an aquarium. Roads had been painted gold. Saint's men had marked off the ruined hillside palace as a private competitors' residence.

"Nobody hurt?" I asked.

"Not yet," Tomas said. "They pay for what they take. They tell us that means they are welcome."

I looked down at the plan. The whole fucking island was beautiful, and the nigga had somehow found a way to draw himself on all of it.

"Keep your wages," I told him. "Keep your houses. If they tell you anything different, tell them the captain's home."

The crowd heard captain and answered with king.

I wasn't about to interrupt good conversation.

Solomon Saint met us at the palace gate with two champions on either side. The first man wore a gray uniform and polished boots, a chest like a steel door, and a look that said he'd never been wrong unless another man had failed him. The woman beside him had short dark hair, a yellow suit, and a tiny chess knight pinned at her throat.

"Captain Lionel Graves," Saint said, indicating the man. "Champion of pursuit, combat shooting, and the continental police games."

Graves looked at my badge.

"You wear that casually."

"Nigga, I wear everything well."

Knox made a little sound behind me that might have been agreement or an old knee.

"Doctor Mina Quill," Saint continued. "Three-time winner of the Seven Sciences Crown."

Mina held out her hand to Shawna first.

"I know your music," she said.

"I hope that's good news."

"Very. I used your last performance to get through a miserable train journey beside a man who explained trains to me."

Mercedes liked her immediately.

Saint led us through the gate. In the courtyard, mechanics worked beneath canvas awnings. Cameras hung from balconies. Waiters carried iced drinks around a marble statue with its face covered by a cloth.

I stopped beside it.

"Who is that?"

"The future champion," Saint said.

I lifted the cloth.

It was Saint.

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

"Nigga, you better hope marble comes with an eraser."

He got in.

He pulled us both out.

He cried harder.

"Go," I said.

He jumped.

Everybody stayed above the water.

I counted until I believed it.

Forty.

All forty.

Graves turned toward me. His immaculate uniform was soaked. A bloody scrape ran down his forearm.

"You didn't use the lightning," he said.

"There were niggas in the water."

He looked at the passengers. Looked back at me.

Something moved in his face. Not friendship yet. Recognition of a thing his general had neglected to explain.

We came into the harbor with three boats full of rescued people and Tank holding a soaked cook who refused to let go of his neck.

The stadium was on its feet.

Saint waited at the dock with cameras behind him. He opened his arms as if the rescue had been an entertainment he had generously arranged.

"A remarkable beginning."

I got out of the boat and walked straight into his open arms.

He wasn't expecting that.

I hugged him hard enough to put seawater all over his white jacket and spoke against his ear.

"Forty people, nigga. If one had gone under and stayed there, I'd be carrying your ass out instead of talking."

His body went stiff.

I released him smiling for the cameras.

"Wonderful host," I said.

Behind him, Mina Quill had brought blankets. She wrapped one around the cook and gave another to Tank. Marcellus stood with Alma, helping the frightened passengers name the people they needed called.

Shawna came ashore barefoot. Her dress clung to her knees. She took one look at the untouched stage and climbed onto it.

Saint started to speak.

Rosa took his microphone.

"No," she said.

Then my wife sang.

She didn't sing the big triumphant number everybody expected. She sang something slow enough that the people still shaking could breathe with it. The first line went across the harbor almost bare. By the second, Rosa was beside her. By the chorus, Alma's cooks were singing from the dock, and the men we had just pulled from the sea were holding each other's hands instead of the boat rails.

I stood there dripping beside my enemy while the whole island belonged to my wife.

Saint couldn't interrupt it. Couldn't buy it. Couldn't even clap until everybody else did, because he needed to know what admiration looked like before he copied it.

Graves stood on my other side.

"She's extraordinary," he said.

"Nigga, now you're catching up."

When the song ended, the island didn't stop cheering for so long that Saint's opening fireworks went off without anybody watching them.

Shawna came down the steps and put her wet arms around my neck.

"Did you win the boat competition?" she asked.

"Wasn't one."

"There is now."

She kissed me in front of every ship the general owned.

That night, a small envelope appeared beneath our door. No threat. No crest. Just Marcellus's name written in a woman's steady hand.

Inside was a black ribbon and a line of writing.

Tell the boy with the impossible car that the general is digging in the wrong room.

Underneath, one more sentence.

And tell Marcellus I have not forgotten how to lead.

## 4. Every Cop in the World Wanted My Autograph

"Nigga, justice could at least have windows."

Saint had put me at the far end in a uniform two sizes too small.

I came in my own.

"Souvenir."

## 5. Marcellus Had Been a Dangerous Nigga Too

Marcellus put on a white dinner jacket to meet a woman who had threatened him with a ribbon.

I watched the old nigga adjust his cuffs for the fourth time.

"You nervous?"

"No."

"Nigga, your left wrist been dressed for ten minutes."

He lowered his hands.

"I would prefer not to look like time has won."

Tank leaned in the doorway. "Time won against everybody, Marcellus. You just got better shoes than most niggas."

"Thank you, Tank. That is almost encouraging."

We found Isolde in the roofless ballroom from the photograph.

Moonlight lay across the old floor. Somebody had cleared the weeds from between its marble squares and hung lanterns in the broken windows. The mountain rose behind the wall. Below it, the general's stadium shone gold, but up here all we could hear was the sea and a woman turning the pages of a piece of music.

She wore a red dress and silver shoes. Sixty years old, Marcellus told me, and standing like nobody had ever been allowed to make her hurry. Her hair was white at the temples, black everywhere else. The black ribbon around her throat looked new.

"Isolde."

"You found the door this time."

Nigga took that one straight in the chest.

Shawna stepped forward first.

"I'm Shawna."

"I know. I heard you sing to the harbor."

Isolde took her hands. "You made a frightened room breathe. That is harder than making a happy room clap."

My wife smiled, and I watched admiration pass between two women without either one getting smaller. I liked that shit. I liked seeing Shawna recognized by somebody who knew what a thing cost before it looked easy.

Marcellus stood looking down the steps. The last time he'd seen them, he'd been twenty-seven, carrying a lamp, expecting a whole different life to meet him at the harbor.

Isolde took his hand.

"This time," she said, "we leave together."

He nodded.

I went first with Graves behind me. Tank followed, then the others. The stair smelled of old stone and sea air. Water had found its way into the walls, leaving pale lines like the room had been counting the tides.

The antechamber was almost exactly as Marcellus had described it. Cabinets. A stool. The remains of a lamp blackened on one side. A conductor's baton lay in a cracked velvet case on the table.

Marcellus picked it up.

"He came back," he said softly.

"Who?" I asked.

"The conductor. Bellanotte must have had him return to the palace after we left."

Isolde opened the case. Beneath the baton lay a folded sheet in an old man's handwriting.

Marcellus read it aloud.

"To the two young fools who gave an old musician one excellent night. The king is dead. His son sold the coast. The men who bought it have no patience for music, so they will never find what his father hid. I have kept the last path for people who know how to share a room. If you return, follow the river beneath the lion. And Marcellus, the jacket was worth more than twenty dollars."

Tank laughed quietly.

The old man wiped his eyes with the back of his hand.

There was a drawing on the other side. A river. A lion. Three little bridges leading toward a pair of enormous doors.

The Vault of Kings.

Not a metaphor. Not a man selling an expensive story about an empty hole. Beyond the cabinets, the far wall held a carved lion large enough for a man to stand in its shadow. Beneath its paws, a narrow passage sloped downward toward running water.

I shone my light into it.

The floor glittered.

Gold coins lay in the shallow water like somebody had dropped a handful and decided he was rich enough not to bend over.

"Nigga," Tank said.

"Yeah."

"No, nigga."

"I see it."

"I don't think you seeing the amount of nigga I am currently experiencing."

Porsche crouched and lifted a coin. One side bore the crowned lion. The other showed a ship sailing under seven stars.

"Pretty," she said.

Marcellus took it carefully. "Old."

Mercedes smiled. "Those words get along beautifully."

We followed the passage to the first bridge.

An underground river crossed the cavern ahead. Above it, three stone arches climbed toward a dark platform on the opposite side. On that platform stood the vault doors, taller than the yacht's mast, made of black metal veined with dull gold.

Between the doors sat a round red stone.

Except it wasn't a stone.

It was an empty setting.

Isolde drew in a breath.

"The Lion's Heart."

"Where's the heart?" I asked.

Graves answered from beside me.

"Around Saint's neck. He wears it beneath his medals. I saw it once when he was injured during a tournament."

The general had the last piece.

Of course the nigga did. A man couldn't own twelve ships without finding something smaller to be insecure about.

I looked at the bridges. Two were broken halfway across. The third stood intact, narrow and wet, rising in a clean curve toward the doors.

"We know where," I said. "Now we know what. That's more than he knows."

A light appeared at the far end of the cavern.

Then another.

Graves pushed me sideways as the first shot struck the wall.

Saint's digging crew had found a side passage.

They came through it in helmets and dark uniforms, guns flashing over the river. I pulled Shawna behind a stone column. She took Isolde with her. Tank shoved Marcellus down behind the bridge rail and returned fire.

The chamber roared.

"Nigga brought drills and guns," Tank shouted.

"Still can't dance," I said.

I moved along the near bank while Graves kept the gunmen from crossing. Water splashed around my shoes. The stone was slick. My light rolled from my hand and shone upward, catching the gold veins on the doors like lightning trapped in a wall.

One gunman reached the intact bridge.

I met him halfway.

He swung the butt of his rifle. I caught the blow on my shoulder, drove into him, and felt the bridge rail give beneath our weight. For one second both of us leaned over the river with nothing but my hand on a broken stone between us and the water.

He looked at my face.

"You," he said.

"Nigga, who were you hoping for?"

I dragged him back onto the bridge and hit him hard enough to end the conversation.

Behind him a second man raised his gun.

Shawna's shot broke the lantern above his head. Darkness swallowed his aim. Graves took him down before he found it again.

My wife looked across the chamber at me.

"One piece," she called.

"Still counting right," I called back.

The gunmen retreated into their passage, dragging two wounded men with them. I could have followed. I wanted to follow. Every part of my body that enjoyed an argument wanted to put Saint's whole digging crew against a wall.

Then I heard Isolde cry out.

A fallen stone had struck Marcellus's leg. He was awake, furious, and unable to stand.

I went back.

Tank lifted the stone. I took Marcellus under the shoulders. The old nigga tried to object until Isolde said his name in a voice that had apparently survived thirty-five years without losing any authority.

He shut up.

We carried him out together.

At the top of the stair, the sky had begun to pale. Rosa helped Shawna close the hidden door while Knox's officers spread across the garden. The ballroom returned to looking like a ruin somebody might photograph and misunderstand.

Marcellus sat on the low wall with his trouser leg rolled up. Bruised, bleeding a little, still arguing that he could walk. Isolde knelt beside him and tied a clean cloth around the cut.

"You came back for me," she said.

He looked at her.

"I was always coming back."

She kissed him before he could say anything else.

Tank turned away, grinning like a nigga trying to give two old people privacy while needing every detail.

My phone vibrated.

Saint, on video, aboard his ship. A red stone hung against his bare throat. The Lion's Heart. He had taken off his medals so I would be certain to see it.

"You have found my door," he said.

"Nigga, the deed says mine."

"Then earn the key. Continue winning. Reach the final challenge. Face me before the whole world."

"Or I come out there and take your necklace."

He moved the camera toward the decks behind him. Armed men stood beside the ship's rail. Beyond them, the other eleven ships held their places around the island.

"You may try."

I looked past his face at the ships.

At the launch hanging from the nearest crane.

At the sailor beside it who was listening instead of looking at the general.

I smiled.

"Oh, nigga," I said. "I intend to."

I ended the call and turned toward my people.

Shawna stood barefoot on the old dance floor, my gun in one hand, the seven stones wrapped safely in the other. Tank had an arm around Rosa. Mercedes and Porsche leaned together beneath the broken arch. Knox and Graves watched the sea. Marcellus had Isolde's lipstick on his mouth and thirty-five years less loneliness in his face.

The general thought he had an army.

Nigga, I had everybody I wanted beside me.

And the morning hadn't even started yet.

## 1. The Ocean Was Waiting on Me

The first thing I saw in Monaco was a man falling off a yacht with my name on it.

Not his name. Mine. AIDEN, in gold letters so big the helicopter had to swing around to get all of them in the window. Under that, somebody had written THE FASTEST KING ALIVE.

I hadn't even raced these niggas yet.

Shawna leaned across me. Her hair brushed my mouth, she smelled like the expensive part of a flower, and below us this grown man in a white dinner jacket was descending through the Mediterranean afternoon with a bottle of champagne still in his hand.

“Is that part of the welcome?” she said.

“Better not be the captain.”

Tank pressed his face against the opposite window. “Nigga, the captain got his own boat.”

“He better get back on it.”

The falling man caught a striped awning. It tore. He dropped another six feet, landed on a lower deck, bounced against a table and stood up holding the bottle over his head. A hundred people cheered. He bowed with the deeply relaxed expression of a man who had never paid a hospital bill himself.

Rosa put her sunglasses back on. “I'm singing for these people at nine. If they're dead by eight, I'm keeping the deposit.”

We had come down from Sky High two days earlier. Nigga, I had saved an entire school out of the sky and graduated so hard they played my arrival at the graduation before they played the graduation. I was supposed to take a vacation. Dean Storm had told me to rest, Chief Knox had told me to represent the department, and my wife had asked for an ocean.

An ocean sounded easy.

Then a duke sent an invitation saying the winner of his monthlong Grand Prix of Kings would receive a fleet of cars, a fleet of yachts and an island called Isla Gloria. The island had a palace, an opera house, a private harbor and twelve beaches. Twelve, nigga. I figured Shawna could have one for each month, and I'd keep the water between them.

So we came to race.

My police Lamborghini arrived before I did. Dark blue, gold badge on the doors, blue lights tucked above the windshield. I could see it sitting on the yacht's upper platform now, strapped beside a swimming pool full of women in shining swimsuits and men too rich to admit they couldn't swim. Beside the car stood Mercedes in white and Porsche in yellow, both arguing with a man whose hat had a feather three feet long.

They had flown ahead with Marcellus to make sure the rooms were right. Apparently the rooms had started a fight.

Our helicopter settled onto the rear deck. Before its blades stopped, the man in the feathered hat marched toward us carrying a silver tray. He had a mustache thin enough to have been signed by a jeweler.

“Captain Aiden,” he announced. “By special instruction of His Grace, Duke Aurelio Cruel—”

“Nigga, somebody really put Cruel on the birth certificate?”

“An ancient name.”

“That don't improve it.”

Shawna squeezed my arm. The man tried again.

“I am Bellario, master of ceremonies and chief marshal of the Grand Prix. His Grace regrets that he cannot personally greet every invited contestant.”

He gestured toward the harbor. At the far end stood a black yacht with four decks and a gold statue of a horse kicking another gold horse in the face. A man in a pale green suit stood on its balcony beneath a huge umbrella. Even from three hundred yards away I could tell he was looking directly at me.

“He's right there,” I said.

“His Grace is at luncheon.”

“Tell him chew faster.”

Bellario's mustache moved without the rest of his face. Mercedes came over carrying the hat's missing feather.

“This nigga said we can't be on the drivers' floor,” she said.

Porsche followed with a room key between two fingers. “Then he offered to upgrade us if we had dinner with him.”

Bellario straightened. “A misunderstanding.”

“Then misunderstand this.” Porsche dropped the key onto his tray. “We already bought the floor.”

I looked at her.

“With your money,” she added.

“Good.”

“Both floors.”

“Better.”

Bellario took a breath. On his tray lay a blue velvet box. He opened it, revealing a little bronze steering wheel on a chain.

“Each contestant must earn the gold wheel before participating in the Grand Prix. The qualification exhibition begins in one hour. An unfortunate late arrival may find the entry list already closed.”

“Nigga, I'm on time.”

“For your arrival, certainly.”

He turned the tray slightly, like that was going to make his sentence less stupid.

Tank stepped out of the helicopter behind me. The rear of the helicopter lifted when his weight came off it. Bellario saw him and briefly reconsidered everything his family had done to bring him into the world.

“The course,” Bellario continued, “is a ceremonial run from the harbor to the Royal Meridian Hotel. Contestants depart from the eastern quay. We are, regrettably, moored at the western quay.”

“How far?”

“Eleven minutes by tender. Forty by road.”

“And when do they close the list?”

“In seven.”

Shawna turned toward the duke's yacht. The man in green raised a glass.

Oh, this nigga wanted to play before I unpacked.

I took the little bronze wheel and hung it around Bellario's neck.

“You wear that, my nigga. It'll remind you of what almost happened.”

Then I walked toward my car.

There are people who think because you own a helicopter you have to use the helicopter. These are the same people who look at a Lamborghini on a yacht and see a transportation problem. I saw my car. My car saw me. Its doors came up before I touched them, and one of the women in the pool screamed like the machine had proposed marriage.

Shawna got in beside me.

“Baby,” she said, “tell me this yacht has a road.”

“It got a front.”

“Aiden.”

“Trust the front.”

Tank climbed onto a nearby launch with Rosa. Mercedes and Porsche followed him, still carrying the feather. Marcellus emerged from the cabin holding a little cup of coffee, saw me putting the car in motion, and sat back down very carefully.

“I'll meet you at the hotel,” he said.

Smart nigga.

Ahead, a service ramp reached from our yacht to a broad floating stage. The stage held a piano, six flower towers and a singer who had started warming up. Beyond it, a smaller yacht rocked gently at its moorings. Beyond that, the eastern quay glittered in the sun.

A reasonable person would have stopped at the word beyond.

I kissed my wife.

“You ready to see the ocean?”

“I was hoping from a chair.”

The engine rose under us. People turned. The singer held one note so long it became fear. I took us down the service ramp, across the stage and past the piano with maybe an inch on each side. The pianist kept playing. That nigga respected his craft.

We hit the edge of the stage with enough speed to make the sea disappear below the windshield.

For a second, all I could see was sky.

Shawna grabbed my arm and laughed one huge terrified laugh directly into my ear.

“Aiden, you stupid beautiful motherfucker!”

Then the tires landed on the smaller yacht's sun deck.

A table collapsed behind us. A man in a silk robe threw both hands up, either praying or awarding me points. I kept the wheel straight. The front of his yacht dipped under our weight; its bow lifted into a ramp. Nigga, the boat helped me. Some things understand greatness immediately.

We launched again.

The car hit the quay sideways, tires shouting, and I pulled it into a turn so clean a line of photographers all stepped backward at the same time. My blue lights flashed across their faces. Behind us, the smaller yacht's owner stood on his deck cheering with the broken table leg over his head.

We had six minutes left.

At the entry gate a woman in a silver racing suit looked from my badge to my car to the two yachts we had just crossed.

“You were on the wrong side,” she said.

“I corrected it.”

She held out a clipboard.

“Name?”

“Nigga, look at the yacht.”

She did. Then she wrote AIDEN in letters large enough to be seen from the yacht.

The starting area was already full of cars. A red machine with white stripes sat low as a threat beside a silver coupe. Behind them waited a black car wearing the number one, its nose shaped like a shark that had married money. The driver leaned on its door in a chrome jacket, thirty-one years old, handsome and aware of it.

He watched the torn flowers falling out of my grille.

“Interesting route,” he said.

“You should see the parking.”

“Cassius Chrome.”

“Aiden.”

“I know.”

He said it without smiling. I liked that. A nigga who knew my name and still wanted to beat me was at least taking his life seriously.

The driver of the red car came over pulling off her gloves. Inez Inferno, the woman whose face appeared on half the racing posters between the airport and the harbor. She had dark hair braided tight, a narrow scar on her chin and eyes that assessed me like she was deciding which part of my car to pass first.

“You brought a passenger,” she said.

“My wife.”

“This isn't a scenic tour.”

“It is for her.”

Shawna leaned out. “I'm Shawna.”

“Inez.”

They shook hands through the window.

“If he scares you,” Inez told her, “you can ride with me.”

Shawna glanced at the sea behind us. “I think we passed scared at the piano.”

Inez laughed. Not polite, either. A real laugh that changed her whole face. Then she looked at me again.

“Keep up, Captain.”

Bellario's voice crackled over the speakers. Somehow the nigga had reached his microphone before his dignity. He announced that the exhibition would follow the Royal Corniche, climb through the old town and finish at the hotel. The first three would receive gold wheels. Everybody else could enjoy the parties and explain to their children why they had become spectators.

I didn't need a gold wheel. I had four black ones and a wife wearing diamonds. But sometimes you let the occasion catch up to your standards.

The light changed.

Cassius left like the devil had called him personally. Inez went beside him, red car snapping through the first bend. I let them get out together. Then I put my foot down.

The Lamborghini roared between two white cars whose drivers both looked over at once. I could see one man's mouth shape the word police. Nigga, yes. Put your seat belt on emotionally.

We swept beside the harbor. Yachts leaned in their ropes. On the balconies, people waved flags. At the first sharp turn, Cassius slipped inside Inez, and she answered by driving so close to his rear bumper their reflections touched. They were good. I could see it immediately. Neither wasted a movement. Neither needed to look back to know the other was there.

I liked them even more.

Then a green car came out of a side street where no race car should have been.

It had no number.

Its driver wore a pale mask.

He aimed straight at Cassius.

“Nigga, what—”

Cassius swerved. The green car struck the wall and spun across the road. Inez shot through the gap. Cassius's front wheel caught the stone curb. His black car lifted, struck a low barrier and hung there with the right side over a hundred feet of open air.

The crowd stopped sounding like a crowd.

Shawna's hand closed on my wrist.

“Aiden.”

“I see him.”

The road was still full of racing cars. I pulled across it, blocked the next two and jumped out before mine finished rocking. Cassius was fighting his belt inside the black car. Every movement lowered its nose farther over the drop.

“Stay still!” I shouted.

“Get me out!”

“Nigga, that is the plan. Help the plan by not rolling into the sea.”

I climbed onto the barrier. The metal under my hands was hot. Below me the harbor flashed between buildings, and I could hear a woman's voice screaming Cassius's name over and over.

He looked up at me through the open side window.

“You can't hold the car.”

“I can hold you.”

The wheel slipped.

Blue light cracked across my fingers.

It came fast, the way it had at Sky High, a sharp electric pressure that seemed to start behind my teeth and run down my bones. I could use it. I could not ask it to last forever. The first time I had tried that, my knees had explained the limits to the rest of me.

So I spent it on one thing.

I caught Cassius by the front of his jacket, pulled him through the window and threw both of us backward onto the road just as the car went over.

The black machine dropped out of sight.

A beat later something huge struck the water below.

Cassius lay across me, breathing like he had stolen the air.

“Nigga,” I said, “you got to get off my chain.”

He looked down. Dante's gold cross was caught beneath his sleeve. He freed it with shaking fingers.

I sat up. The road tilted once. Shawna was already beside me, her hands on my face, checking my eyes.

“How much?” she asked quietly.

“One burst.”

“Tell me the truth.”

“One burst, baby.”

She nodded. Then she kissed me hard enough to make the whole street remember there were other reasons to lose your breath.

By the time she pulled away, the crowd had found its voice.

They were screaming my name.

People on the balconies screamed it. Drivers standing beside their stopped cars screamed it. The silver-suited woman from the entry gate had apparently run half the course and now stood on a staircase shouting, “That's the yacht man!” like it was a title passed through generations.

Cassius got to his feet slowly. He stared at the broken barrier, then at me.

“You stopped your race.”

“You was in it.”

He shook his head.

“Nobody stops here.”

I stood. My legs were steady again, mostly. I dusted off my jacket, straightened the cross and looked toward the hill.

“Nigga, I didn't say I quit.”

We put Cassius in the back of a marshal's car. I got behind my wheel. Shawna tightened her belt and gave me the look she used when she wanted to call me an idiot but was too proud of me to make it sound convincing.

“Three minutes behind,” she said.

“Then we should go.”

The Lamborghini climbed.

I didn't use the blue thunder again. I used my eyes, my hands and the fact that I had not come all this way to lose a little wheel to men who wore driving gloves at breakfast. We passed four cars through the old town. We passed two more beneath a railway bridge. At a bend lined with white flowers, I saw Inez ahead and a yellow car beside her, fighting for first.

Shawna started laughing.

“What?” I said.

“You really think you're going to do it.”

“Nigga, I know I'm going to do it.”

The hotel rose at the top of the hill, all marble balconies and flags. The final straight narrowed between fountains. Inez moved left. The yellow car moved right. Neither had left room for me.

So I drove through the fountain.

Water hit the windshield like applause with weight. Shawna shrieked. Stone dolphins vanished on either side. We came out in a shining wall of spray, hit the finish line half a hood ahead of Inez and stopped beneath the hotel's canopy with a live ornamental fish on the windshield.

For two seconds nobody moved.

Then the doorman took off his hat.

“Welcome, Your Majesty.”

I looked at Shawna.

She was soaked, gorgeous and laughing so hard she couldn't answer.

Inez rolled up beside us. She looked at the fish. She looked at me. Finally she slapped both hands against her steering wheel and laughed too.

“You ridiculous motherfucker.”

I picked the fish off the glass and handed it to the doorman.

“Put my nigga back in his house.”

Above us, on the grand balcony, Duke Aurelio Cruel stood beside a table laid for lunch. His green suit shone. His glass had stopped halfway to his mouth.

Bellario arrived carrying a gold wheel.

This time he didn't make a speech.

He put it around my neck with both hands.

Shawna stepped out of the car. The crowd moved toward us in a wave, shouting, filming, holding out hats and champagne and one man's marriage certificate for reasons he was going to have to explain to his wife. I pulled Shawna against me before they could separate us.

“Got your ocean,” I said.

She brushed a drop of fountain water off my cheek.

“Now get me a towel, fastest man alive.”

I looked up at the duke.

He set his glass down.

The month had begun.

## 2. The Woman Who Drove Through Fire

The Royal Meridian gave me a bedroom with a ceiling painted like heaven and a bathtub big enough to baptize a basketball team.

I put Shawna in the bathtub before anybody had a chance to ask me another question.

She sat among the bubbles with her hair pinned up, holding the gold wheel I had won and turning it between her fingers. Through the open balcony doors we could see the harbor, the yacht with my name, and the smaller yacht whose owner had already renamed it AIDEN LANDED HERE.

“Nigga got a gift shop open,” I said.

Shawna looked over my shoulder. On the smaller boat, somebody was carrying boxes of shirts onto the deck.

“You owe that man a table.”

“I gave him a business.”

“You gave him tire tracks.”

“Limited edition.”

She tried to stay serious. Failed. I took that as permission to kiss her again.

For a while the whole Grand Prix could do whatever the fuck it wanted outside. I had warm water, my wife and a door that locked. When we finally got dressed, her lipstick was on my shoulder and a bell was ringing somewhere in the suite with the desperate persistence of a man who didn't know how close he was to getting fired.

Tank stood at the door holding the room-service bell between two fingers.

“Why didn't you knock?” I asked.

“I did. The door didn't answer.”

Behind him, Rosa had a plate of fruit. Mercedes wore a silk robe over her clothes because, according to her, the robe was included and she was getting her money's worth. Porsche had three phones laid out on a tray and was talking into the fourth.

“No,” she told somebody. “He saved your favorite driver and beat your other favorite driver. The price changed twice.”

I pointed at her.

“What's that?”

“Appearances.”

“I ain't said yes to appearances.”

“That's why they cost extra.”

She covered the phone with her hand. “You want six hundred thousand to eat dinner near a watch?”

“Does the watch eat?”

“No.”

“Make it eight.”

She nodded and went back to the call like this was exactly the kind of business judgment she had expected.

Marcellus sat beside the balcony with a little screwdriver and my gold wheel. He had taken the chain off because the clasp was cheap.

“Gold plate,” he said.

“Nigga.”

“Very thick plate.”

“That ain't the point.”

“No,” he agreed. “It rarely is.”

He fitted a better clasp, one of his own, then held the wheel to the light.

“Beautiful work on the face. Someone talented made the original design. Someone cheap ordered the copies.”

Rosa stopped eating her grapes.

“The musicians say nobody's been paid in three weeks.”

Porsche lowered her phone.

“They told the dancers tomorrow.”

Mercedes leaned against the doorway. “They told the drivers' hosts next week. One girl asked to leave, and the hotel said her passport was at the island office.”

The room changed. Not dramatically. Nobody stood up with a speech. But Tank put the bell down, and Shawna stopped turning the wheel.

“How many?” I asked.

“Don't know yet,” Mercedes said. “Enough that they're scared to count in front of anybody.”

There are lies rich niggas tell because they want more money, and lies they tell because they think other people belong to them. The second kind always pisses me off faster. I had bought enough things to know the difference between owning a building and owning the woman standing in it.

Porsche's phone started ringing again. She silenced it.

“There's a mechanic downstairs who wants to see you. She says you picked up something in your grille.”

“The fish?”

“No. A piece of the green car.”

We went downstairs together.

The hotel's underground garage looked like somebody had threatened a museum until it learned to accelerate. Red Ferraris, silver machines with doors like wings, a white car sitting under its own chandelier. My Lamborghini stood in the middle with half the harbor still drying on it.

A woman with close-cut hair and grease on one cheek waited beside the front wheel. She wore blue overalls tied at the waist and a pair of gold hoops so large I respected her confidence around machinery.

“Lark,” she said. “Thirty-two, chief mechanic for the Silver Arrow team until somebody sold the team without telling the people inside it.”

She gave me a handshake strong enough to be honest.

“Aiden.”

“I watched.”

She held up a bent piece of green metal.

“This was lodged below your light. The car that hit Chrome wasn't part of the field.”

“I noticed the mask.”

“I mean it belongs to the harbor service. It should have been carrying luggage.”

Tank took the fragment and turned it over.

“That's some violent luggage.”

Lark didn't smile.

“My partner drove one of those cars. Mateo. He's been missing nine days.”

Shawna stepped closer. “Your partner on the team?”

“My partner in my house.”

She said it firmly, like she was tired of people trying to make the missing part smaller.

“He's thirty-four. Laughs with his whole mouth. Puts salt on pineapple. Buys me flowers even though I keep telling him a plant would survive longer. His last message said they wanted extra crew for the island. Then nothing.”

Mercedes folded her arms.

“They keep sending people there.”

Lark looked at her. “You heard it too?”

“I hear things men think women ain't listening to.”

I took the green fragment from Tank.

“What do you need?” I asked.

“To know whether you really stopped for Cassius. Or whether it was a show.”

“Nigga was hanging off a cliff. That's an expensive show.”

She studied me. Then she nodded once.

“I need somebody who can get through a door they won't open for me.”

“Show me the door.”

Before she could answer, an engine shouted from the garage entrance.

Inez's red car came down the ramp and stopped close enough for its heat to reach my shoes. She climbed out in black jeans and a red sleeveless shirt, braid down her back, the gold wheel she had won hanging against her chest.

“I've been looking for you,” she said.

“Everybody has.”

“I found you first.”

She looked at Shawna. “You hungry?”

“Yes.”

“Good. My father cooks. He lives on the mountain. I brought two cars.”

I looked past her. A second red car waited at the top of the ramp.

“Nigga, that sounds like a race wearing a dinner jacket.”

“It can be both.”

Her eyes dropped to the green metal in my hand. For the first time since I'd met her, her smile went away completely.

“Where did you get that?”

“Out of my fish tank.”

“Aiden,” Shawna said.

“Out of the car.”

Inez reached for the fragment, then stopped before touching it.

“Bring Lark,” she said. “And don't let a hotel car follow you.”

The mountain road started above the city and climbed into terraces of olive trees, old stone walls and houses whose balconies seemed to be standing on faith. Inez led in her red machine. Shawna sat beside me, Lark in the back of a car Porsche had obtained by pointing at it and saying, “That one belongs with us now.” Tank followed in a black SUV so large it made the little stone villages look recently built around it.

At the first overlook, Inez stopped and got out.

“There's a restaurant at the top,” she said. “Nine turns. Two narrow bridges. No traffic this afternoon; they close it for race practice.”

“Your father live at the restaurant?”

“No. We eat there first, so he's got time to stop pretending he isn't excited.”

Shawna laughed.

Inez rested her arms on my open window.

“The exhibition was one thing. You went through a fountain.”

“It was in the way.”

“You're fast. But this road is mine.”

I looked at the road curling up behind her. Beautiful road. Lots of opportunity to be wrong in public.

“What we racing for?”

“An honest answer.”

“To what?”

“Why you're here.”

“Shawna wanted the ocean.”

Inez waited.

“Nigga, that is the honest answer.”

She looked at my wife. Shawna shrugged.

“I did.”

For a moment Inez seemed almost disappointed. She had probably expected a secret revenge, a lost fortune, a hidden father. People see a man driving a police Lamborghini across yachts and assume his life requires explanation. Sometimes a nigga just loves his wife.

“Then race for dinner,” she said.

I glanced at Shawna.

“Baby?”

She tightened her belt.

“Win me something with potatoes.”

Inez went first.

She knew every inch of that mountain. I could tell by the way she entered a turn before the turn seemed to exist, the way her red car moved from sunlight to shadow without any hesitation. She wasn't performing for me. She was speaking a language she'd learned before anybody told her it was difficult.

I answered with my right foot.

We climbed past a chapel and two empty market squares. At the first bridge she gained half a car. At the second I took it back. Shawna watched the road with one hand on the dashboard and the other on my thigh, not holding me down, just staying connected to the nigga who had decided dinner should happen at a hundred impossible miles an hour.

Inez's car flicked left around a stone fountain.

I stayed on the road this time. Growth.

Then we hit the smoke.

It rolled across the upper bend, black and thick, carrying the bitter smell of burning rubber. Inez braked. I pulled beside her. Through the smoke, a delivery truck sat sideways against the wall, its cab burning. Beyond it, a little open-top car had stopped with its driver trapped between the truck and the drop.

A man in a chef's jacket stood on the far side waving both arms.

“Papa,” Inez said.

She was out of her car before the word finished.

I caught her wrist as she passed.

“Together.”

“That's my father.”

“I heard you.”

Her eyes hit mine, hot with the kind of fear that wants to turn itself into violence. Then she nodded, and we ran into the smoke.

The heat slapped us. Inez pulled her shirt over her mouth and reached the small car. Her father was outside it, trying to free the truck's driver from the burning cab. Another man lay on the road, dazed, one arm over his face.

Inez went to the man on the road. I went to her father.

“Move back,” I said.

“He's caught.”

“I see him.”

The truck driver was conscious, terrified, his belt jammed beneath a twisted piece of door. I didn't need a lesson in trucks to know we had very little time. I grabbed what was in the way and pulled.

It moved an inch.

Fire climbed the dashboard.

“Nigga,” I told the truck, “don't make this complicated.”

I pulled again.

This time the metal gave enough. Inez's father reached in beside me, and between us we dragged the driver out. His shoes hit the road. He tried to stand. Couldn't. I threw his arm over my shoulder and carried him away while Inez helped the other man toward our cars.

Shawna was already there with water and a cloth from the back seat. She took the driver's face gently between her hands.

“Look at me. You're out. Stay with me.”

Behind us the truck's front tire burst with a sound like a shot.

Tank's SUV came around the bend. He stopped, took in the fire, the men, Inez and her father, then looked at me.

“Nigga, we left you alone for six minutes.”

“Dinner got delayed.”

He lifted the second injured man into the SUV like the man weighed the same as a suit jacket. Lark got in beside him. Porsche called ahead to the mountain clinic. Mercedes flagged the practice marshals arriving from below, and within a minute everybody had a job except the fire, which continued being a disrespectful motherfucker until a crew put it out.

Inez stood beside her father. He had gray at his temples, a burn on one sleeve and hands that wouldn't stop shaking. She held both of them.

“Why were you up here?” she asked.

“The restaurant called. They said you wanted—”

He stopped.

I saw her face change.

“I didn't call the restaurant.”

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Then her father looked at me.

“You are Aiden.”

“Yes, sir.”

“My daughter said you were very annoying.”

“Papa.”

He held out his shaking hand.

“She was smiling when she said it.”

I shook it carefully.

“Esteban Inferno,” he said. “You saved my life, and my daughter's pride will never recover.”

“Nigga, her pride looked healthy in them turns.”

Inez let out a breath that almost became a laugh. She brushed soot off her father's cheek, then turned toward the burned truck.

Its rear doors had fallen open. Inside were cases of fruit, a few crates of flowers and a green service-car panel stacked behind them.

The same green as the fragment in my pocket.

Lark saw it too.

“They didn't want you at his house,” she said.

Esteban closed his eyes.

For the first time, he looked old.

We ate at his house anyway.

It stood above the road, white walls, blue shutters, a courtyard full of lemon trees. The dining table was long enough for all of us. Esteban insisted on cooking, even with the burn on his sleeve, and Inez argued with him until Shawna quietly handed her a knife and a bowl of vegetables.

“Help him do it,” she said.

So Inez helped.

I stood at the courtyard wall watching the sea below. Cassius arrived in a borrowed silver car with one arm in a sling and a bottle of wine under the other.

“Nigga, you drive that yourself?” I asked.

“With one good arm I'm still quicker than most people with two.”

“Most people ain't me.”

“No,” he said. “They aren't.”

He put the bottle on the table and held out his good hand. This handshake felt different from the one we had never actually had at the starting line. His fingers closed hard around mine.

“I watched the harbor footage,” he said. “You jumped a Lamborghini across two yachts, pulled me out of my car and still won.”

“Three things.”

“What?”

“You forgot the fish.”

He stared at me. Then he started laughing. It hurt his shoulder, so he held it and kept laughing anyway.

At dinner Esteban served lamb, potatoes crisp at the edges, bread warm enough to steam when Tank tore it. Shawna held up her fork toward me.

“You won the potatoes.”

Inez shook her head. “We didn't finish.”

“My wife eating them, ain't she?”

Everybody laughed except Esteban, who was looking at the green fragment beside my plate.

Finally he picked it up.

“Seven years ago,” he said, “a car this color followed my wife to the last race she ever attended.”

Inez put her fork down.

“Papa.”

“You brought him here.”

“Because he saved Cassius.”

“And now he has saved me.” Esteban turned the piece of metal in his hand. “Perhaps I have been waiting for a man who would stop.”

The courtyard grew quiet. Beyond the wall, the sun touched the sea.

Esteban reached into his shirt and drew out a little folded photograph. He placed it beside the green metal. A woman stood in front of a racing car, smiling into the camera with one hand lifted to block the sun. She wore a plain blue dress. On the car behind her, someone had painted a white bird.

“My wife was Amalia,” he said. “She loved this race before the duke turned it into a kingdom.”

I looked at the photograph. Then at the mountains, the road and the impossible amount of money glittering below us.

“What happened?” I asked.

Esteban watched his daughter's face for a long moment.

“I believed the wrong man,” he said.

And underneath the table, Shawna took my hand.

## 3. Dinner with a Man Named Cruel

Esteban said the wrong man's name, and Cassius stood up so fast his chair fell over.

“Nero Chrome,” Esteban said.

“That was my father.”

“I know whose father he was.”

“Nigga,” I said, “everybody leave the potatoes out of this.”

Cassius wasn't looking at me. He had one hand flat on the table and the other held against his injured shoulder. Esteban sat very straight, his wife's picture beneath his palm. Inez stood between them before either man moved again.

“Papa, you don't know everything.”

“I know who was beside the car.”

“You know what Bellario told you.”

Cassius laughed once. No amusement in it.

“Of course. Bellario.”

He turned toward the courtyard gate.

I caught him there.

“Where you going?”

“Somewhere my father isn't being tried by a man who let the duke buy his mouth.”

Esteban's chair scraped behind us.

Tank put one hand on its back.

“Sit down, my nigga.”

“I will not—”

“Then stand still. Pick one.”

I kept my eyes on Cassius.

“You can hear him and still hate what he says.”

“I've heard it for seven years.”

“Then hear it once with me here.”

His jaw moved. He looked at Shawna, who had come to stand beside the table, holding Amalia's photograph carefully by the edges.

“Your father drove this car?” she asked.

Cassius looked back despite himself.

“The White Heron,” he said. “He built it with Esteban.”

“Then sit down and tell us something nobody else gets right.”

That reached him.

He picked his chair up. He didn't apologize, and Esteban didn't ask him to. Sometimes, nigga, the best you can get from two men is both their asses back at the table.

Esteban began again.

Seven years earlier, the Grand Prix had been smaller. There were rich men in it, but money wasn't the only invitation. Mechanics entered cars they had built behind their houses. Boat captains raced old engines down the coast. The drivers ate at the same tables as the people who changed their tires, and at the end of the month the winner sailed to Isla Gloria with everybody who had helped him get there.

“Amalia loved the last night,” Esteban said. “She would dance until the musicians begged her to stop.”

Rosa smiled faintly. “I know that kind of woman.”

“She collected people. If a waiter looked lonely, suddenly he was sitting with us. If a driver lost, she found something he had done well and made him talk about that. When Aurelio bought the race, she told him the prize was already here. He only needed to leave it alone.”

“What'd he say?” I asked.

“That a woman who enjoyed giving things away should marry a poorer man.”

“Nigga.”

Esteban nodded.

“He said it smiling.”

That year Esteban and Nero had entered the White Heron together. One drove the mountain stages, the other the coastal ones. Amalia kept their books, knew every worker by name and had discovered that the prize fleet included vessels the duke had never actually paid for. Their owners had been promised shares in the Grand Prix. Instead, crews stopped receiving wages, captains disappeared from the entry list and the harbor gates began closing at night.

“She went to the island,” Esteban said. “A captain named Celadon took her. She came back with a packet of papers and told me we had to finish the race. Win it in front of everybody. Then we could make him give back what he had taken.”

“Why didn't she show you the papers?” Shawna asked.

“She did. I looked at the first page, kissed her forehead and said I trusted her.”

His voice roughened.

“I thought that was the same as listening.”

I felt Shawna's thumb move against my hand.

Esteban told us about the final mountain stage. The White Heron led by eleven seconds. Nero had driven the morning. Esteban was due to take over at the ridge. Amalia traveled ahead in a little blue support car carrying the papers and the team's food, because even when a man was about to expose a duke she thought he ought to have lunch.

At the ridge, Nero never arrived.

Instead, Bellario brought news that the support car had gone over the road. Amalia was dead. Nero had vanished. A green harbor car had been seen leaving the scene.

“The duke showed me a signed withdrawal,” Esteban said. “Nero's name. Money paid. A place on a ship leaving that night. Bellario said he watched Nero get into the green car.”

Cassius leaned forward.

“My father couldn't write with his right hand that week. He'd broken two fingers.”

Esteban blinked.

“The signature was steady as a schoolmaster's,” Cassius said. “I told them. I was twenty-four, and I told every man in that room. They said grief was making me irrational.”

“Why didn't you tell me?”

“You wouldn't open your gate.”

The words landed harder than shouting would have.

Esteban stared at him. Inez looked away, and I understood this was a conversation they had been walking around for seven years, keeping it alive by never allowing it to finish.

Cassius took something out of his jacket. A little metal bird, white paint worn off one wing.

“My father gave me this the morning of the race. Said if he won, I'd drive the first lap next year.”

He set it beside Amalia's photograph.

“He never came home.”

Outside the courtyard a car horn sounded.

Nobody moved.

It sounded again, and Tank stood up with the expression of a man grateful for a fresh target. He opened the gate. Bellario stood beyond it beside a cream-colored limousine, dressed for dinner, feather restored.

“His Grace requests Captain Aiden's presence at the opening banquet.”

I looked at the nigga for a long time.

“Does he?”

“Attendance is traditional.”

“So is knocking.”

Bellario glanced past Tank and saw the table. Saw Cassius, Esteban, Inez. His face didn't change, but his eyes counted us twice.

“An intimate gathering,” he said.

“Getting less intimate.”

Shawna squeezed my hand and stood.

“We'll be there.”

Bellario bowed to her with the precise depth of a man who practiced alone.

“His Grace particularly hopes you will sing.”

“Then he should particularly hope he's paid my musicians.”

The bow ended early.

After he left, Esteban went inside and returned with a small lacquer box. He opened it to reveal a folded letter, a yellow ribbon and a photograph of Amalia laughing beside a white boat.

“This came three days after she died,” he said. “A woman delivered it. She wouldn't give her name. I read the first line so many times I almost wore through it.”

“What's it say?” I asked.

He unfolded the top edge.

“Esteban, if you are reading this without me, do not let a man who never loved either of us explain my last day.”

Cassius closed his eyes.

Esteban held the letter toward Inez.

“I wasn't brave enough to obey her.”

She took it without a word.

We left the table standing. Plates, bread, photograph, the little white bird. Nobody tried to make it look like a dinner that had ended normally.

At the hotel, Shawna changed into a blue dress that moved around her legs like water had learned her name. She wore Dante's watch on her wrist and earrings I had given her after Sky High. She stood before the mirror fastening one, and I came up behind her.

“You want to skip this?” I asked.

“No.”

“We can eat somewhere nobody named Cruel owns the forks.”

She met my eyes in the mirror.

“I want to sing. I want Rosa to get paid. I want that nigga to look at us across his own table while we find out what he's hiding.”

“That's my wife.”

She turned around.

“And you want to race him.”

“I want to beat him.”

“Then do it with your eyes open.”

I kissed her forehead.

She pushed my chest lightly.

“Don't Esteban me.”

Nigga, I laughed so hard she smiled before she could help it. Then I took her face in both hands.

“I hear you. You want to sing, Rosa gets paid, we watch the duke. I don't go running off because somebody waves a faster car.”

“And?”

“And if it's a really fast car, I bring you.”

She hit me with the earring box.

The banquet occupied a terrace above the sea. Three hundred people sat beneath chandeliers hanging from living trees. A string orchestra played on a platform over the swimming pool. Along the terrace stood twelve cars under silk covers, each with its own guard and a woman holding a silver card naming the race it belonged to.

I walked in with Shawna on my arm, my badge against my chest and Tank behind me in a white suit that made him look like he had personally purchased summer.

Conversations stopped in waves.

The first wave was people recognizing me. The second was people asking the first wave who I was. The third was everybody looking at Shawna and deciding the answer must be more impressive than they had heard.

The duke rose from a long table at the center.

Aurelio Cruel was forty-eight, tall, broad through the shoulders, handsome in the way a sword can be handsome before somebody pulls it out. His hair was black with two white streaks. He wore a green diamond on one hand and a watch whose face seemed larger than the gold wheel I'd won.

“Captain,” he said. “Your entrance this afternoon was costly.”

“For who?”

The people close enough to hear laughed before they looked at him. He let them finish.

“For the harbor's dignity.”

“Nigga, the harbor selling shirts.”

This time the laughter reached the next table.

He smiled. “Come. Sit beside me.”

There were two chairs waiting. One for me, one for Shawna. Farther down the terrace, Mercedes and Porsche had been directed toward a little table behind a pillar.

I stopped.

“Where's my people?”

“We've arranged seating by distinction.”

“Then you arranged it wrong.”

The duke rested his fingertips on the back of my chair.

“A captain. A celebrated singer. Naturally, some guests have qualifications others do not.”

Mercedes had heard him. She walked over before I could answer, yellow silk catching the light.

“Mine got you to call my agency three times last year,” she said. “Porsche's got your minister to pay double for dinner and still ask if she had a sister.”

Porsche appeared beside her.

“I told him no. He looked financially devastated.”

A man at the duke's table suddenly became fascinated by his soup.

The duke's smile thinned.

“There is no need for vulgarity.”

“Nigga, you invited me,” I said.

I took the chair beside him, lifted it and carried it to Mercedes's table. Tank picked up the table itself. Rosa brought the flowers. Within twenty seconds we had joined two tables together directly in front of the orchestra, and half the terrace was standing to help because nobody wanted to be the last person on the wrong side of the evening.

Cassius arrived with Inez. He saw what we were doing and dragged his own chair over one-handed.

“Careful,” I told him.

“It's my good arm.”

“Keep one.”

The duke stood alone beside his empty place settings.

Then he laughed.

It was a good laugh. Full, generous, almost convincing.

“A king makes his own table,” he said.

I sat down and pulled Shawna into the chair beside me.

“Now you catching on.”

Dinner came. Lobster, lamb, fish served whole on silver platters. Tank looked at the fish and said, “This better not be the nigga you saved.” Rosa nearly dropped her wine.

The duke joined us after the second course. He had accepted that if he wanted the most important table at his party, he would have to sit at mine.

He told stories about princes losing fortunes. He described a driver who had bet his castle and left in a taxi. He named the prize fleet: forty road cars, twelve racing cars, six yachts and a flagship called the Sovereign Sun. At the end of the month, the winner would sail that flagship into Isla Gloria's harbor and receive the island before every camera in the world.

“And if you lose?” I asked.

“I do not compete.”

“Convenient.”

“I own the competition.”

“For now.”

He watched me over his glass.

Shawna's hand settled on my knee.

The orchestra quieted. Rosa rose first, went to the little stage and sang a song so rich and warm the sea itself seemed to lean closer. Tank sat still through the whole thing. Nigga, the man could lift a table with dinner on it, but that woman's voice pinned him to a chair.

When she finished, he stood up before anybody else.

“That's Rosa!” he shouted.

Everybody knew. He told them anyway.

Then Shawna joined her.

My wife began without an introduction. One clear note, then another, rising over the water. I had heard her sing in rooms where I was the only person listening. I'd heard her angry, laughing, half asleep, making up words because she couldn't remember the real ones. This was different. She stood in the light with her shoulders bare and let the whole terrace hear how large she was inside.

The duke stopped drinking.

A woman at the next table began to cry.

I sat forward, both hands flat on my knees, because if I leaned back I felt like my chest might open.

Rosa answered her. Their voices wound together. The orchestra followed, and for once in my life, nigga, I didn't need to be doing the most impressive thing in the room. My wife was doing it, and she kept looking at me like the song had an address.

When they finished, the terrace stood.

Shawna came back through hands reaching for hers. A conductor kissed her fingers. One old man said something in Italian so emotional his wife nodded like he had finally gotten a point right after forty years.

The duke rose to greet her.

“You must perform on Isla Gloria,” he said. “The opera house was built for a voice like yours.”

Shawna smiled.

“Then make sure the people who clean it get paid.”

His hand stopped halfway toward hers.

At the edge of the terrace, a waiter dropped a glass.

Bellario was beside him instantly. Too quickly. He caught the man's arm and began steering him toward a service door. The waiter looked back once, directly at our table.

Lark stood up.

“That man knows Mateo.”

I rose with her.

The duke stayed between us and the door.

“Captain, dessert—”

“Nigga, move.”

His eyes hardened.

Then he did.

By the time we reached the corridor, Bellario was gone. The waiter stood alone beside a linen cart, shaking, blood on one finger where the glass had cut him.

Mercedes took a clean napkin and wrapped his hand.

“What's your name?” she asked.

“Lucien.”

“How old are you?”

“Twenty-nine.”

“Okay, Lucien. Breathe.”

He looked past us toward the terrace.

“They said if I spoke to the drivers—”

Tank shut the corridor door.

“Now you're speaking to us.”

Lucien swallowed. Then he pulled a folded service menu from his pocket. On the back, in hurried pencil, was a list of names. Thirty-two of them.

Mateo was halfway down.

Beside the list were two words.

CINDER DOCK.

Below that, a time.

MIDNIGHT FRIDAY.

Lark covered her mouth with one hand.

Lucien looked at me.

“Are you really a policeman?”

I took the gold badge off my chain and put it in his good hand.

“Nigga,” I said, “tonight I am whatever you need me to be.”

## 4. I Arrested the Finish Line

By morning, the city had put my face on a bus.

Not an advertisement. The whole front of the bus was my face. The driver looked out through my sunglasses, and when it turned a corner my left cheek opened to let people off.

Tank watched it pass beneath our balcony.

“Nigga, they made your head public transportation.”

“About time the people rode in something great.”

Rosa was eating breakfast in his lap despite there being twelve empty chairs. She leaned forward to see the bus, then looked back at me.

“You charging for that?”

Porsche answered from the next chair.

“Now we are.”

It was the first official race day. The Grand Prix of Kings lasted a month, with seven major races, exhibitions, sea events and enough parties to make a man forget why he had brought his passport. The ceremonial qualifier had put me first on the grid. Today's Crown Circuit would take us through the city three times, then climb to the glass palace above the harbor.

Winner received the first of seven jeweled keys. All seven together opened the prize ceremony. The points decided who held them at the end.

I liked a competition that gave a nigga jewelry for doing his job.

Lark had spent the night in the garage with my car. When I went down, she stood beside it holding a mug that read WORLD'S WORST PASSENGER.

“Your wife bought me this,” she said.

“She knows talent.”

Shawna came down behind me carrying a little bag of breakfast. She put it on Lark's workbench.

“Eat.”

“I will.”

“Now.”

Lark took a piece of bread out without arguing. I respected how quickly Shawna had become the chief of all this.

Cassius waited near the far wall in a fresh racing jacket, arm still in the sling. His replacement car sat under a black cover.

“You racing?” I asked.

“No. Doctor said another impact could tear the shoulder worse.”

“Nigga, listen to the doctor.”

“I am. I hate it.”

He walked with me around the Lamborghini.

“Watch the third descent. There's a blind rise before the old customs house. Yesterday's practice showed a dip after it.”

“Anything else?”

He looked toward Lark. She nodded.

“The green harbor cars have disappeared. Every one of them. The service company says they changed the fleet overnight.”

“Fast workers.”

“Frightened workers.”

Lucien had slept in one of our extra rooms, with Tank's jacket over a chair outside the door as a warning anybody with survival instincts could understand. He had told us the staff were being moved to Cinder Dock on Friday, four nights away. From there, boats took them to the island's old shipyard. Nobody who went there came back to work the mainland hotels.

He didn't know what happened to them. That was bad enough without inventing worse.

Chief Knox called while I was putting on my gloves.

“Captain,” she said, “why is there footage of your patrol vehicle on a boat?”

“International waters.”

“It was on two boats.”

“International boats.”

There was a pause.

“Did you save that driver?”

“Yes, ma'am.”

“Good. The department has received eleven marriage proposals, three requests for transfer and a yacht club membership.”

“Keep the membership.”

“Already did.”

I smiled.

Then I told her about Lucien, the missing staff and the duke's fleet. She went quiet in a different way.

“Keep your people close,” she said. “Your badge gets attention. Attention can open a door or tell the wrong man which door you're standing behind.”

“I know.”

“And Aiden?”

“Yes, ma'am?”

“Win the race.”

Nigga, that was leadership.

The grid sat beneath an arch covered in blue flowers. The street beyond it climbed between grand hotels, then fell toward the port. Thousands of people pressed behind the barriers. More watched from balconies, windows, rooftops. A choir had been hired to sing the race anthem, but half of them started chanting my name when I got out of the car.

The conductor tried to recover the key.

Lost it completely.

Shawna kissed me beside the driver's door. She wore a blue team jacket with AIDEN on the back in gold. Under my name she had added HUSBAND, because apparently the women of Monaco required a second line of clarification.

Inez walked over in her red racing suit.

“You running the police car all month?” she asked.

“Why?”

“Because I want to know whether being arrested is going to count as losing.”

“Depends how fast you confess.”

She grinned. Then her expression turned serious.

“My father read the rest of Amalia's letter last night. He wants to show you after the race.”

“What'd he find?”

“A reason to hate himself.”

“Tell him wait till we know where to put it.”

She looked toward the duke's balcony. Aurelio stood beside Bellario under an awning, both dressed in white, like innocence was a team color.

“I told my father I'm winning today,” Inez said.

“Nigga, you can tell him you drove beautifully.”

She punched my shoulder lightly.

“Try to keep your car out of the architecture.”

The anthem finally ended. Drivers climbed in. The crowd grew tight and loud around us.

I put both hands on the wheel.

For a moment I thought of Vegas. Big T's lot. My first night. The heat of the gun, the hurt in my side, Shawna looking at me like she couldn't decide whether I was rescue or a different kind of disaster. Now she stood behind a race barrier across an ocean wearing my name, and a city was waiting for me to do something impossible before lunch.

Nigga, life had finally started acting like it knew who I was.

The lights went out.

Inez launched beside me. A yellow car took the outside, its driver a narrow man called Prince Bastian who had introduced himself three times at dinner and seemed offended that no one had introduced him a fourth. He swept ahead into the first turn.

I let him enjoy it until the second.

Then I passed him so close he looked over like I had entered his living room without knocking.

The city became motion. White stone, blue water, red banners. We passed the opera steps with Inez half a car behind. She came alongside on the descent, and for two turns we ran together, close enough that I could see her eyes through the visor.

She was smiling.

I smiled back.

The nigga in the yellow car tried to fit between us.

There was no between us.

He backed off at the last second and nearly lost his rear wheels to the curb. Inez shook her head inside her helmet. I laughed once, sharp and happy, and we dropped into the tunnel beneath the old city.

Inside, the engines multiplied. Mine roared off the walls. Hers answered. Lights ran across the hood in long white bars. At the exit she took the lead by a nose.

I could have called the blue thunder.

I didn't.

That mattered to me. I liked knowing the difference between what I could do and what the power could add. The thunder was a burst, a loan against the strength in my body. I wasn't spending it because a beautiful woman had better timing through one tunnel.

I caught her at the harbor bend.

The first lap ended with us side by side.

Above the crowd I heard Tank's voice. I couldn't make out the words, but the volume suggested he had physically challenged the entire spectator area to doubt me.

On the second lap, things got ugly.

A white car came out of the service lane ahead of us. It belonged to the race, had a marshal's stripe on the side and should never have been moving across the active road. It stopped sideways at the entrance to the third descent.

Inez swore over the open race channel.

“Obstacle!”

She turned sharply, barely missing it. Her red car hit a loose section of barrier and spun toward a staircase descending into the market below.

I pulled left.

The yellow car behind me hit the stopped marshal's car. Metal folded. Smoke burst across the road. Somebody's horn started and didn't stop.

Inez's car slid backward down the first three steps.

People in the market scattered.

“Nigga, no.”

I took my car onto the staircase beside her.

The first impact drove the breath out of me. The second made my teeth meet. I kept the Lamborghini straight while her car bounced against the stone rail, scraping sparks from the wall. We descended side by side, two beautiful machines doing something no manufacturer had put in the brochure.

At the bottom stood a flower stall, an old stone well and a crowd trapped between them.

I hit the horn.

People moved.

Inez's car wasn't going to stop in time.

I drove across her path and caught her front corner against the rear of mine.

The impact spun us both. My shoulder hit the door. Flowers exploded across the windshield. We came to rest around the stone well, her car on one side, mine on the other, with a wreath hanging from my mirror like I had won a funeral.

For one second all I heard was ticking metal.

“Inez!”

“I'm here.”

“You hurt?”

“My pride.”

“Nigga, it needed room anyway.”

She laughed once, then groaned.

I was out before she opened her door. Her ankle had twisted beneath the pedals. She could move it, but not without pain. I helped her onto the low stone wall around the well.

A woman from the flower stall came over holding two huge bouquets.

“For you,” she said breathlessly.

“Ma'am, I just destroyed your business.”

“You saved my sister.”

Behind her, another woman was crying and pointing at the place where Inez's car would have gone.

The flower seller pushed the bouquets into my hands.

“Take them.”

Nigga, what was I supposed to do? Refuse flowers while standing in a car crash shaped like a love letter?

I handed one bouquet to Inez.

“First date on the mountain, second in a well. You moving fast.”

“Tell your wife I want a refund.”

The crowd laughed. It helped. Fear loosened enough for people to start moving again.

My radio crackled. Shawna's voice came through, low and urgent.

“Talk to me.”

“I'm good. Inez twisted her ankle. We're both out.”

“Your chest?”

“Fine.”

“Your side?”

“Fine, baby.”

She took one breath I could hear all the way from the grid.

“Then get her help.”

The marshals reached us two minutes later. One tried to tell me to stand aside. I showed him my badge, pointed to Inez and told him to be useful in that direction.

Above us, at the top of the staircase, Bellario appeared.

He was speaking into a radio.

Smiling.

Not at me. At somebody I couldn't see.

I looked down at the white marshal's stripe reflected in the broken glass on my hood. Then up at his radio.

Something in me went still.

Inez followed my eyes.

“You see it too,” she said.

“I'm starting.”

The race continued around us. The leaders had gone. The announcer's voice drifted over the buildings, saying Captain Aiden had made another courageous sacrifice and would surely receive the admiration of the entire principality.

Admiration was nice.

Nigga, I wanted my key.

Lark arrived with Tank and a service crew. She went straight to the Lamborghini, checked the wheels, looked beneath the front and stood back with both hands on her hips.

“You are a violent owner.”

“She still run?”

“She is offended. She still runs.”

I got in.

Tank leaned through the window.

“Aiden.”

“What?”

“You serious?”

I looked at him.

He stepped back and slapped the roof.

“Go get them niggas.”

There was no easy road out of the market. There was, however, an arcade through the old customs building, a broad set of doors and a courtyard that opened onto the last climb. Lark looked at the route. Looked at me. Then she moved her tools out of the way.

I drove through the customs house with both bouquets on the passenger seat.

A guard dropped his sandwich.

I shouted an apology through the window because I respected lunch.

We came out on the climb half a lap behind the leaders. My car's rear panel flapped. The steering pulled a little. There were petals in every vent. Nigga, we looked like the world's most expensive prom had escaped from a hurricane.

I drove anyway.

One car at the old palace. Another beside the railway. Two through the harbor tunnel. The crowd saw the broken blue Lamborghini and rose in sections, pointing, shouting, spreading my return faster than the announcer could understand it.

By the final lap I was fourth.

By the glass palace I was third.

Ahead, Prince Bastian's yellow car chased a silver coupe toward the line. Behind them rolled the white pace car that should have withdrawn at the final corner.

Instead, it slowed across the road.

Bellario stood at the finish with the jeweled key on a cushion.

Nigga wanted me stopped one more time.

I put on my siren.

The crowd screamed.

The pace car didn't move.

I leaned out the window and pointed straight at its driver.

“You are under arrest, motherfucker!”

He looked back.

That was his mistake.

The silver coupe went left. Bastian went right. I went straight, not into the pace car but across the low marble divider beside it, the Lamborghini lifting just enough to make every camera on the finish line tilt upward.

For a breath I was above all three of them.

Then I landed beyond the pace car, the steering wheel kicking against my hands, and crossed the line first with my siren singing louder than the anthem.

The palace windows shook with the crowd.

I stopped, climbed out and walked back toward the pace car. Its driver had both hands up already. Bellario came toward me with his radio against his chest.

“Captain, the result requires review.”

“Nigga, review this.”

I pointed to the driver.

“Who told him to stay on the track?”

“A communication error.”

“Who told the other car to block the descent?”

“An unfortunate sequence—”

“Keep talking. You might confess by accident.”

The cameras moved closer. Bellario saw them. Saw the crowd. Saw the duke on the glass balcony, watching him with a face that offered no rescue.

He lifted the cushion.

“The first key,” he announced, voice suddenly enormous, “to Captain Aiden.”

I took the key.

Then I took his radio.

“Evidence,” I said.

He looked like he wanted to bite me.

Shawna came through the crowd at a run. I caught her with my good arm, lifted her off the ground and kissed her while the city shouted around us.

“You brought me flowers,” she said when she saw the car.

“Whole market.”

“You're bleeding.”

“Small amount.”

“Nigga.”

I loved when she said it like that. Like my entire government name had disappointed her in one syllable.

She pressed a cloth to the scrape above my eyebrow. Behind her, Inez arrived in the medical cart with her ankle wrapped and both bouquets in her lap. She had kept mine safe.

“You won in that?” she asked, looking at my car.

“Car wanted it bad.”

She shook her head slowly.

“I've raced the best drivers in the world.”

“Now you have.”

She laughed, then raised her bouquet toward the crowd.

“Aiden!” she shouted.

The crowd answered so loudly the duke stepped back from his balcony.

But Lucien recognized the little silver mark etched on the back.

A white bird.

“The Sovereign Sun uses that mark,” he said. “The old crew put it on their equipment.”

Cassius reached into his pocket and put his father's little metal bird beside it.

Same wings.

Same lowered head.

Inez unfolded Amalia's letter.

“Now,” she said, “we read the whole thing.”

## 1. The Ocean Was Waiting on Me

The first thing I saw in Monaco was a man falling off a yacht with my name on it.

Not his name. Mine. AIDEN, in gold letters so big the helicopter had to swing around to get all of them in the window. Under that, somebody had written THE FASTEST KING ALIVE.

I hadn't even raced these niggas yet.

Shawna leaned across me. Her hair brushed my mouth, she smelled like the expensive part of a flower, and below us this grown man in a white dinner jacket was descending through the Mediterranean afternoon with a bottle of champagne still in his hand.

“Is that part of the welcome?” she said.

“Better not be the captain.”

Tank pressed his face against the opposite window. “Nigga, the captain got his own boat.”

“He better get back on it.”

The falling man caught a striped awning. It tore. He dropped another six feet, landed on a lower deck, bounced against a table and stood up holding the bottle over his head. A hundred people cheered. He bowed with the deeply relaxed expression of a man who had never paid a hospital bill himself.

Rosa put her sunglasses back on. “I'm singing for these people at nine. If they're dead by eight, I'm keeping the deposit.”

We had come down from Sky High two days earlier. Nigga, I had saved an entire school out of the sky and graduated so hard they played my arrival at the graduation before they played the graduation. I was supposed to take a vacation. Dean Storm had told me to rest, Chief Knox had told me to represent the department, and my wife had asked for an ocean.

An ocean sounded easy.

Then a duke sent an invitation saying the winner of his monthlong Grand Prix of Kings would receive a fleet of cars, a fleet of yachts and an island called Isla Gloria. The island had a palace, an opera house, a private harbor and twelve beaches. Twelve, nigga. I figured Shawna could have one for each month, and I'd keep the water between them.

So we came to race.

My police Lamborghini arrived before I did. Dark blue, gold badge on the doors, blue lights tucked above the windshield. I could see it sitting on the yacht's upper platform now, strapped beside a swimming pool full of women in shining swimsuits and men too rich to admit they couldn't swim. Beside the car stood Mercedes in white and Porsche in yellow, both arguing with a man whose hat had a feather three feet long.

They had flown ahead with Marcellus to make sure the rooms were right. Apparently the rooms had started a fight.

Our helicopter settled onto the rear deck. Before its blades stopped, the man in the feathered hat marched toward us carrying a silver tray. He had a mustache thin enough to have been signed by a jeweler.

“Captain Aiden,” he announced. “By special instruction of His Grace, Duke Aurelio Cruel—”

“Nigga, somebody really put Cruel on the birth certificate?”

“An ancient name.”

“That don't improve it.”

Shawna squeezed my arm. The man tried again.

“I am Bellario, master of ceremonies and chief marshal of the Grand Prix. His Grace regrets that he cannot personally greet every invited contestant.”

He gestured toward the harbor. At the far end stood a black yacht with four decks and a gold statue of a horse kicking another gold horse in the face. A man in a pale green suit stood on its balcony beneath a huge umbrella. Even from three hundred yards away I could tell he was looking directly at me.

“He's right there,” I said.

“His Grace is at luncheon.”

“Tell him chew faster.”

Bellario's mustache moved without the rest of his face. Mercedes came over carrying the hat's missing feather.

“This nigga said we can't be on the drivers' floor,” she said.

Porsche followed with a room key between two fingers. “Then he offered to upgrade us if we had dinner with him.”

Bellario straightened. “A misunderstanding.”

“Then misunderstand this.” Porsche dropped the key onto his tray. “We already bought the floor.”

I looked at her.

“With your money,” she added.

“Good.”

“Both floors.”

“Better.”

Bellario took a breath. On his tray lay a blue velvet box. He opened it, revealing a little bronze steering wheel on a chain.

“Each contestant must earn the gold wheel before participating in the Grand Prix. The qualification exhibition begins in one hour. An unfortunate late arrival may find the entry list already closed.”

“Nigga, I'm on time.”

“For your arrival, certainly.”

He turned the tray slightly, like that was going to make his sentence less stupid.

Tank stepped out of the helicopter behind me. The rear of the helicopter lifted when his weight came off it. Bellario saw him and briefly reconsidered everything his family had done to bring him into the world.

“The course,” Bellario continued, “is a ceremonial run from the harbor to the Royal Meridian Hotel. Contestants depart from the eastern quay. We are, regrettably, moored at the western quay.”

“How far?”

“Eleven minutes by tender. Forty by road.”

“And when do they close the list?”

“In seven.”

Shawna turned toward the duke's yacht. The man in green raised a glass.

Oh, this nigga wanted to play before I unpacked.

I took the little bronze wheel and hung it around Bellario's neck.

“You wear that, my nigga. It'll remind you of what almost happened.”

Then I walked toward my car.

There are people who think because you own a helicopter you have to use the helicopter. These are the same people who look at a Lamborghini on a yacht and see a transportation problem. I saw my car. My car saw me. Its doors came up before I touched them, and one of the women in the pool screamed like the machine had proposed marriage.

Shawna got in beside me.

“Baby,” she said, “tell me this yacht has a road.”

“It got a front.”

“Aiden.”

“Trust the front.”

Tank climbed onto a nearby launch with Rosa. Mercedes and Porsche followed him, still carrying the feather. Marcellus emerged from the cabin holding a little cup of coffee, saw me putting the car in motion, and sat back down very carefully.

“I'll meet you at the hotel,” he said.

Smart nigga.

Ahead, a service ramp reached from our yacht to a broad floating stage. The stage held a piano, six flower towers and a singer who had started warming up. Beyond it, a smaller yacht rocked gently at its moorings. Beyond that, the eastern quay glittered in the sun.

A reasonable person would have stopped at the word beyond.

I kissed my wife.

“You ready to see the ocean?”

“I was hoping from a chair.”

The engine rose under us. People turned. The singer held one note so long it became fear. I took us down the service ramp, across the stage and past the piano with maybe an inch on each side. The pianist kept playing. That nigga respected his craft.

We hit the edge of the stage with enough speed to make the sea disappear below the windshield.

For a second, all I could see was sky.

Shawna grabbed my arm and laughed one huge terrified laugh directly into my ear.

“Aiden, you stupid beautiful motherfucker!”

Then the tires landed on the smaller yacht's sun deck.

A table collapsed behind us. A man in a silk robe threw both hands up, either praying or awarding me points. I kept the wheel straight. The front of his yacht dipped under our weight; its bow lifted into a ramp. Nigga, the boat helped me. Some things understand greatness immediately.

We launched again.

The car hit the quay sideways, tires shouting, and I pulled it into a turn so clean a line of photographers all stepped backward at the same time. My blue lights flashed across their faces. Behind us, the smaller yacht's owner stood on his deck cheering with the broken table leg over his head.

We had six minutes left.

At the entry gate a woman in a silver racing suit looked from my badge to my car to the two yachts we had just crossed.

“You were on the wrong side,” she said.

“I corrected it.”

She held out a clipboard.

“Name?”

“Nigga, look at the yacht.”

She did. Then she wrote AIDEN in letters large enough to be seen from the yacht.

The starting area was already full of cars. A red machine with white stripes sat low as a threat beside a silver coupe. Behind them waited a black car wearing the number one, its nose shaped like a shark that had married money. The driver leaned on its door in a chrome jacket, thirty-one years old, handsome and aware of it.

He watched the torn flowers falling out of my grille.

“Interesting route,” he said.

“You should see the parking.”

“Cassius Chrome.”

“Aiden.”

“I know.”

He said it without smiling. I liked that. A nigga who knew my name and still wanted to beat me was at least taking his life seriously.

The driver of the red car came over pulling off her gloves. Inez Inferno, the woman whose face appeared on half the racing posters between the airport and the harbor. She had dark hair braided tight, a narrow scar on her chin and eyes that assessed me like she was deciding which part of my car to pass first.

“You brought a passenger,” she said.

“My wife.”

“This isn't a scenic tour.”

“It is for her.”

Shawna leaned out. “I'm Shawna.”

“Inez.”

They shook hands through the window.

“If he scares you,” Inez told her, “you can ride with me.”

Shawna glanced at the sea behind us. “I think we passed scared at the piano.”

Inez laughed. Not polite, either. A real laugh that changed her whole face. Then she looked at me again.

“Keep up, Captain.”

Bellario's voice crackled over the speakers. Somehow the nigga had reached his microphone before his dignity. He announced that the exhibition would follow the Royal Corniche, climb through the old town and finish at the hotel. The first three would receive gold wheels. Everybody else could enjoy the parties and explain to their children why they had become spectators.

I didn't need a gold wheel. I had four black ones and a wife wearing diamonds. But sometimes you let the occasion catch up to your standards.

The light changed.

Cassius left like the devil had called him personally. Inez went beside him, red car snapping through the first bend. I let them get out together. Then I put my foot down.

The Lamborghini roared between two white cars whose drivers both looked over at once. I could see one man's mouth shape the word police. Nigga, yes. Put your seat belt on emotionally.

We swept beside the harbor. Yachts leaned in their ropes. On the balconies, people waved flags. At the first sharp turn, Cassius slipped inside Inez, and she answered by driving so close to his rear bumper their reflections touched. They were good. I could see it immediately. Neither wasted a movement. Neither needed to look back to know the other was there.

I liked them even more.

Then a green car came out of a side street where no race car should have been.

It had no number.

Its driver wore a pale mask.

He aimed straight at Cassius.

“Nigga, what—”

Cassius swerved. The green car struck the wall and spun across the road. Inez shot through the gap. Cassius's front wheel caught the stone curb. His black car lifted, struck a low barrier and hung there with the right side over a hundred feet of open air.

The crowd stopped sounding like a crowd.

Shawna's hand closed on my wrist.

“Aiden.”

“I see him.”

The road was still full of racing cars. I pulled across it, blocked the next two and jumped out before mine finished rocking. Cassius was fighting his belt inside the black car. Every movement lowered its nose farther over the drop.

“Stay still!” I shouted.

“Get me out!”

“Nigga, that is the plan. Help the plan by not rolling into the sea.”

I climbed onto the barrier. The metal under my hands was hot. Below me the harbor flashed between buildings, and I could hear a woman's voice screaming Cassius's name over and over.

He looked up at me through the open side window.

“You can't hold the car.”

“I can hold you.”

The wheel slipped.

Blue light cracked across my fingers.

It came fast, the way it had at Sky High, a sharp electric pressure that seemed to start behind my teeth and run down my bones. I could use it. I could not ask it to last forever. The first time I had tried that, my knees had explained the limits to the rest of me.

So I spent it on one thing.

I caught Cassius by the front of his jacket, pulled him through the window and threw both of us backward onto the road just as the car went over.

The black machine dropped out of sight.

A beat later something huge struck the water below.

Cassius lay across me, breathing like he had stolen the air.

“Nigga,” I said, “you got to get off my chain.”

He looked down. Dante's gold cross was caught beneath his sleeve. He freed it with shaking fingers.

I sat up. The road tilted once. Shawna was already beside me, her hands on my face, checking my eyes.

“How much?” she asked quietly.

“One burst.”

“Tell me the truth.”

“One burst, baby.”

She nodded. Then she kissed me hard enough to make the whole street remember there were other reasons to lose your breath.

By the time she pulled away, the crowd had found its voice.

They were screaming my name.

People on the balconies screamed it. Drivers standing beside their stopped cars screamed it. The silver-suited woman from the entry gate had apparently run half the course and now stood on a staircase shouting, “That's the yacht man!” like it was a title passed through generations.

Cassius got to his feet slowly. He stared at the broken barrier, then at me.

“You stopped your race.”

“You was in it.”

He shook his head.

“Nobody stops here.”

I stood. My legs were steady again, mostly. I dusted off my jacket, straightened the cross and looked toward the hill.

“Nigga, I didn't say I quit.”

We put Cassius in the back of a marshal's car. I got behind my wheel. Shawna tightened her belt and gave me the look she used when she wanted to call me an idiot but was too proud of me to make it sound convincing.

“Three minutes behind,” she said.

“Then we should go.”

The Lamborghini climbed.

I didn't use the blue thunder again. I used my eyes, my hands and the fact that I had not come all this way to lose a little wheel to men who wore driving gloves at breakfast. We passed four cars through the old town. We passed two more beneath a railway bridge. At a bend lined with white flowers, I saw Inez ahead and a yellow car beside her, fighting for first.

Shawna started laughing.

“What?” I said.

“You really think you're going to do it.”

“Nigga, I know I'm going to do it.”

The hotel rose at the top of the hill, all marble balconies and flags. The final straight narrowed between fountains. Inez moved left. The yellow car moved right. Neither had left room for me.

So I drove through the fountain.

Water hit the windshield like applause with weight. Shawna shrieked. Stone dolphins vanished on either side. We came out in a shining wall of spray, hit the finish line half a hood ahead of Inez and stopped beneath the hotel's canopy with a live ornamental fish on the windshield.

For two seconds nobody moved.

Then the doorman took off his hat.

“Welcome, Your Majesty.”

I looked at Shawna.

She was soaked, gorgeous and laughing so hard she couldn't answer.

Inez rolled up beside us. She looked at the fish. She looked at me. Finally she slapped both hands against her steering wheel and laughed too.

“You ridiculous motherfucker.”

I picked the fish off the glass and handed it to the doorman.

“Put my nigga back in his house.”

Above us, on the grand balcony, Duke Aurelio Cruel stood beside a table laid for lunch. His green suit shone. His glass had stopped halfway to his mouth.

Bellario arrived carrying a gold wheel.

This time he didn't make a speech.

He put it around my neck with both hands.

Shawna stepped out of the car. The crowd moved toward us in a wave, shouting, filming, holding out hats and champagne and one man's marriage certificate for reasons he was going to have to explain to his wife. I pulled Shawna against me before they could separate us.

“Got your ocean,” I said.

She brushed a drop of fountain water off my cheek.

“Now get me a towel, fastest man alive.”

I looked up at the duke.

He set his glass down.

The month had begun.

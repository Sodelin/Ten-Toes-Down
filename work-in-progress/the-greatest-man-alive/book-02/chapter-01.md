## 1. They Put My Parking Space in Heaven

The first time I drove my police Lamborghini into the clouds, the sky opened up and a woman told me I was already late for my own award.

I had not enrolled yet.

That was the kind of pressure greatness put on a nigga.

Shawna sat beside me with her bare feet on the dashboard and a pair of white sunglasses bigger than the sins we had committed on our honeymoon. She had a new diamond on her wedding finger and Dante's old watch on her wrist. I had my badge in my pocket, his cross under my shirt, and a twelve-cylinder reason to believe the universe had finally gotten serious about respecting me.

The invitation said Sky High Advanced Hero Academy. Adult postgraduate division. Every student twenty-two or older. It said my graduation rescue in Vegas had demonstrated exceptional aptitude.

Exceptional aptitude was when you parallel parked without touching the curb.

I had driven through a burning auditorium and still delivered my valedictorian speech before the principal finished crying.

They should have put exceptional motherfucker.

“Aiden,” Shawna said. “Cloud.”

“Baby, I see it.”

“Inside the car.”

A little piece of weather rolled through the open window and rested on her lap. She pushed her sunglasses down and stared at it. The cloud drifted over her wedding ring, turned pink, and dissolved.

“Your diamonds doing shit to the climate now,” I said.

“Keep looking at the road.”

There was no road. That was the first issue.

There had been a road until thirty seconds ago. It had climbed a mountain, passed between two stone lions, and ended at a gate reading WELCOME, HEROES. Then a guard in silver armor had glanced at my gold badge, looked at my face, and saluted so hard his helmet fell off.

“Captain Aiden,” he'd said. “It's an honor.”

“Nigga, which way?”

“Up.”

I had appreciated his confidence.

Now the Lamborghini was climbing an invisible ramp through the clouds at a speed Shawna described as “why the fuck.” The wheel vibrated in my hands. Beneath us, the mountains shrank until they looked like somebody's pile of dirty clothes.

Behind us Tank's black Mercedes rose through the mist. Rosa sat beside him, singing into a hairbrush. Marcellus was in the back wearing a gray suit, carrying a small case, and looking as if he had already decided heaven charged too much for lunch. Dr. Vega had come for a three-day visit and was currently holding Tank's headrest like she intended to remove it.

Mercedes and Porsche followed in a red convertible I had bought them after they pointed out that I owned an island's worth of cars and kept asking them to share an Uber. The women were arguing over whose turn it was to meet a man who could fly.

All my people were ascending.

And at the top of the clouds waited a whole fucking city.

Not a building. A city.

White towers rose around a stadium shaped like a golden shield. A glass train moved between them without touching anything. Gardens hung off stone balconies. An enormous waterfall poured from one edge of the island and disappeared into blue air. Little vehicles streamed across bridges suspended between floating blocks of land. Farther out, a ring of separate islands carried hotels, stores, and a casino with a flashing sign visible from what appeared to be several religions.

SKY HIGH stood over the front gate in letters taller than my mansion.

I slowed down just enough for the Lamborghini to make its noise.

Shawna smiled.

“There he go.”

“Who?”

“My husband about to introduce himself to the atmosphere.”

“Baby, the atmosphere been waiting.”

The invisible ramp became a solid white drive. Students stopped where they were. A man with transparent wings folded them and turned. A woman carrying three refrigerators over her head looked at me and forgot she was carrying refrigerators. Somebody in a crimson cape walked straight into a fountain.

I parked in front of the main staircase.

There was a gold sign.

DEAN.

I read it carefully and remained parked.

A tall woman descended the steps wearing a white suit with silver lightning along the collar. Her hair fell black to her shoulders except for one white streak. She had the kind of face that made a man stand up straighter before he remembered he had a wife beside him. The air sharpened around her. Her eyes were gray, and behind the gray something bright moved.

“Captain Aiden,” she said.

I stepped out. “Dean.”

“Seraphine Storm.”

“That's a hell of a name.”

“I earned it.”

“So did I.”

We shook hands. A tiny spark jumped between us.

I did not let go.

Her eyebrow rose.

“Most visitors flinch.”

“Most visitors ain't me.”

Shawna came around the car. Storm's attention moved to her ring, then to her face.

“Mrs. Aiden.”

“Shawna.”

They shook hands without sparks. I respected the weather for knowing when to behave.

Tank parked behind me. “Nigga, you in her space.”

“I know where I'm at.”

“We can arrange another space,” Storm said.

Tank looked from her to me and slowly took off his sunglasses.

“We just got here.”

“I make a strong first impression.”

From the staircase a blond man in a blue uniform laughed once. He was broad through the shoulders, clean-shaven, with a silver emblem on his chest and a jaw he plainly expected other people to remember.

“That's the famous graduate?” he asked.

Storm didn't look away from me. “Victor Voltage. Twenty-seven. First in his division until this morning.”

Victor's smile stopped.

My smile began.

“Nigga, don't look at me. I ain't touched your division.”

“Your provisional placement puts you first overall,” Storm told me.

“Based on what?” Victor demanded.

She gestured toward the Lamborghini.

He looked at the car as if it had personally stolen his childhood.

“That's a vehicle.”

“The entrance ramp was closed,” Storm said.

Everybody got quiet.

I looked back at the sky.

Tank looked back at the sky.

Shawna took off her sunglasses very slowly.

“Aiden.”

“Now hold on.”

Storm's smile grew. “We were testing our emergency magnetic ascent field. You drove through its narrowest boundary at exactly the velocity required to remain inside it. Then three civilian cars followed your path.”

Dr. Vega got out of the Mercedes.

“I would like to go home.”

“You can use the open ramp,” Storm said.

“There was an open ramp?”

I put my arm around Shawna. “Baby, I brought us the scenic way.”

She pinched my side. The old wound still had opinions about people touching it.

“The scenic way to fucking death.”

“And look how beautiful we survived.”

Then the sky train exploded.

Not the whole train. The front coupling burst in orange flame as it came around the stadium. A sharp crack punched across the courtyard. Heads turned. A hundred windows flashed.

The train tilted.

Forty people screamed at once.

Victor was moving before the sound finished. Blue-white energy wrapped his fists. He launched himself upward, fast enough to knock spray out of the fountain. Storm raised both hands. Wind slammed through the courtyard.

The train's front carriage lurched back toward its rail.

The rear two kept falling.

I saw a woman pressed to the back window. Saw a man drag another man off the floor. Saw the distance between that train and the edge of the island closing.

Nigga, I was supposed to be receiving something.

Instead I had to go earn it again.

I jumped into the Lamborghini.

“Tank!”

He already had the Mercedes door open.

Shawna slid into my passenger seat.

“You staying here,” I said.

“Drive.”

“Baby—”

“Those people do not have time for you to win this argument.”

I hit the gas.

The car shot between the fountain and the steps. Somebody tried to tell me that was a pedestrian path. He said the first half to the windshield and the second half to the exhaust.

The rear carriages struck a maintenance platform beyond the stadium. Metal shrieked. The platform bent toward the open air. Victor hovered above it, both arms spread, electricity coursing into the damaged suspension rings. Sweat ran down his face. His boots flickered.

He was powerful.

He was also holding forty people over a drop nobody had bothered measuring for survivability.

I understood his problem immediately.

Too many niggas. Not enough ground.

I took a turn so hard Shawna's hair whipped across my mouth. The Lamborghini climbed a service stair, hit the landing, and flew over a strip of garden. We landed on the maintenance platform with a crash that made my teeth feel individually expensive.

The platform dipped.

“Get that car off!” Victor shouted.

I looked through the windshield at the hanging carriage.

“Shawna. Door.”

She unbuckled. “Which?”

“All of them.”

I shoved the Lamborghini nose beneath a sloping support beam. The hood buckled. A warning screamed from the dashboard. I kept going until the car wedged between the broken platform and the stone service wall.

The platform stopped dropping.

Victor stared at me.

“Nigga,” I shouted, “keep doing the light shit!”

Shawna was already at the carriage. She hit its emergency release, hauled the door open, and caught the first woman through it by both arms.

“Walk toward the wall,” she ordered. “Keep your hands on the rail. Don't look down.”

Somebody looked down and froze.

My wife slapped him.

“Look at me instead.”

He did. He moved.

Tank brought the Mercedes onto solid ground and jumped out. Rosa followed him. She took one look at the gap and began singing, loud and steady, a beat people could move to. Not a sad song. One of the nasty ones she performed in Vegas when men forgot how much cash they had brought.

Forty terrified adult heroes began crossing a ruined platform to a song about getting paid before breakfast.

I climbed onto my hood.

It was an expensive hood. I would mourn it when niggas stopped falling out of the sky.

Inside the last carriage, a man had his leg trapped beneath a fallen seat. A silver-haired woman knelt beside him. One whole side of the carriage was opening over empty blue air.

I jumped through the door.

The woman looked at my badge.

“Are you rescue?”

“Today.”

I grabbed the seat. It didn't move.

The trapped man said, “My power's density. I can't turn it off when I'm scared.”

“How heavy?”

“A lot.”

“Nigga, that is a terrible number.”

The train shifted. Outside, Victor yelled. A burst of blue light turned the windows white.

I braced my shoes against the opposite seat and pulled. The metal bit my palms. Nothing. The man shook so hard the floor rattled.

I got close to his face.

“What's your name?”

“Elias.”

“Elias, listen to me. My wife standing outside this train.”

He blinked.

“That woman thinks I'm the greatest man alive. I happen to agree. Now you gonna make both of us look stupid?”

“No.”

“Then stop trying to weigh more than my reputation.”

He laughed. Just once. A frightened little cough of a laugh.

The floor lifted.

I tore the seat aside.

“There we fucking go.”

The woman helped me drag him. We reached the door as the back window broke away. Wind hit us. My cross slapped against my chest.

Below, the waterfall hung like a white ribbon all the way into the clouds.

Victor's left hand went dark.

The carriage dropped six inches.

I shoved Elias through the door into Tank's arms. The woman followed. I grabbed the rail.

Then the platform tore loose.

My Lamborghini went with it.

For one ugly second I saw my whole future reflected in that dark blue roof: car gone, wife watching, nigga floating down to Earth with a gold badge and an unpaid insurance argument.

Shawna caught my wrist.

Tank caught her waist.

Storm's wind caught the car.

Everything stopped.

Not gently. My shoulder made a noise. Shawna swore. Tank planted his feet and shouted, “I got all you motherfuckers!”

The dean stood at the end of the broken walkway with lightning in her hair. The entire Lamborghini hung sideways beneath us inside a spinning cradle of cloud.

“Captain,” she said through clenched teeth. “Would you please stop adding weight?”

I hauled myself up beside my wife.

“I'm trying to take some off.”

Victor brought the last carriage down into the grass. Storm lowered my car beside it. The engine ticked. The left headlight hung out like a tired eye.

Silence spread over the service yard.

Then Elias began clapping.

The silver-haired woman joined him.

By the time I stood up, everybody was doing it.

Shawna grabbed my face and kissed me hard enough to settle the disagreement we had not finished in the car. I held her against me. Around us forty rescued people cheered louder.

“That's my husband,” she said when she pulled back.

Nobody argued.

Victor landed beside the wrecked train. His uniform smoked at the wrists. He looked at the Lamborghini. Looked at my bleeding hands. Looked at Shawna holding me.

“You have no powers,” he said.

“I got several good qualities.”

“No. I mean you have no measurable power signature.”

“Nigga, measure the people who ain't dead.”

His mouth opened.

Then he looked at Elias sitting safely on the grass.

He closed it.

Storm came toward me. Her hair settled. Behind her, students poured around the corner, calling to the passengers, filming the wreckage, shouting my name even though I had introduced myself to approximately four people.

“Your award ceremony,” the dean said, “will now include an additional citation.”

I looked at my car.

“Will it include a body shop?”

“The best engineering faculty in the sky.”

“Good. That nigga need a doctor.”

Dr. Vega stepped around Tank carrying a medical bag.

“The car can wait. You are bleeding.”

“Little bit.”

“That is also a terrible number.”

She took my hand. Shawna stood against my other side. Tank looked at the shattered platform and shook his head with a smile he couldn't hide.

“You been here twelve minutes.”

“Thirteen,” Storm said.

“See?” I told him. “I gave the school an extra minute.”

At the edge of the crowd stood a thin man in a charcoal suit. He had silver hair combed straight back, a black cane, and eyes that did not seem interested in blinking. Two guards stood behind him in uniforms darker than the others.

Storm noticed him and straightened.

“Chancellor Voss.”

He approached the broken coupling first. Bent his head. Touched a scorched piece of metal with one gloved finger.

Then he turned to me.

“An extraordinary arrival.”

“We try.”

His smile showed no teeth.

“You saved a number of people whose gifts are important to this institution.”

I glanced at Elias. He was holding the silver-haired woman's hand. His whole body shook now that it was allowed to.

“Saved the rest too.”

“Naturally.”

Voss's eyes moved to my badge, then my cross, then Shawna. He lingered on none of them long enough for me to call it a problem.

But a nigga can feel a problem dressing itself.

“We will investigate the equipment failure,” he said.

“Do that.”

His guards collected the broken coupling before anybody else touched it.

Storm watched them go. Her face had changed for one second, then changed back.

She offered us the grand staircase, the open doors, the city of people waiting to meet me.

I put my good arm around Shawna. Tank lifted my overnight bag from the ruined car. Rosa took his other arm. Marcellus appeared carrying the case he had somehow never set down.

“First impression?” he asked.

“Strong,” I said.

He looked at the wrecked train, then at the whole school applauding.

“I should have brought a larger safe.”

We went up those steps together.

Above the entrance hung a banner they had apparently prepared before I arrived.

WELCOME, EXCEPTIONAL HEROES.

Somebody was already climbing a ladder to change it.

I didn't tell them to.

Nigga, excellence makes suggestions of its own.

Our rooms occupied the top floor of a tower overlooking the western edge. There were two balconies, a bath large enough to qualify for boat ownership, and a bedroom ceiling that showed the actual stars above the clouds. The bellman set down our bags and waited to be tipped.

I gave him enough money to change the direction of his afternoon.

He looked at it. Looked at me.

“Sir, I have worked here eleven years.”

“Then you was overdue.”

He left walking backward.

Shawna closed the door and leaned against it. Her smile had gone soft. That was the one that always made me feel more dangerous and less prepared at the same time.

“You scared me,” she said.

“I had it.”

“I know you had it. That's not the same thing.”

I crossed the room. She put her hands beneath my open jacket and rested them against my ribs, careful of the old hurt. Outside the balcony a woman flew past carrying a bouquet larger than herself. Somewhere below us people were still shouting about the train.

“You came with me,” I said.

“Always.”

“Even when I'm parking wrong.”

“Especially. Apparently that's when you become first in your class.”

I kissed her forehead. Then her mouth. She pulled me closer, and for a little while the biggest thing happening in the sky was our door staying shut.

Later, when we stood on the balcony wearing the school's expensive robes, I saw a second city beneath the first.

It wasn't really a city. More a collection of black buildings suspended under the island's lower rock. Bridges joined them. Red lights burned along their roofs. One tower descended farther than the rest and ended in a circular chamber that glowed through its windows like a covered furnace.

“What's that?” Shawna asked.

“Basement with ambition.”

A train moved along a track beneath it. The cars had no windows.

She stopped smiling.

The train entered the glowing chamber. For a moment the windows turned a strange yellow-white. Then every light went out together.

Above us, music began on the school lawn. People laughed. Somebody sent a ribbon of purple fire curling between two towers.

Shawna rested her head against my shoulder.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “I'm finding somebody who'll tell me what that is.”

I looked at the dark place under all our beautiful rooms.

“Tomorrow,” I said, “I'm getting my fucking car back.”

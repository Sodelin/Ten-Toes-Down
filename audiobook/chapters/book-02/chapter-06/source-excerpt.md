I returned to Sky High wearing a torn dinner jacket, carrying another man's gun, and smelling like champagne somebody had used as a weapon.

The night porter stood when he saw me.

“Good evening, Captain.”

“Debatable.”

He saw Shawna beside me, saw Tank behind her, saw Victor carrying a broken chair leg because he apparently had developed an attachment to doing things without powers.

The porter straightened even farther.

“Would you like your room prepared?”

“Nigga, prepare the basement.”

He had no idea what that meant.

Neither did the basement yet.

Storm met us in the music hall. Pike had brought six officers he trusted. Bell stood beside a hatch in the stage with all four hands occupied by tools. Dr. Vega had traded her evening bag for her medical one. Marcellus held the black access seal from Sable's case.

Rosa knelt at the brass grille.

She sang the four lines Lena had sung before.

Nothing answered.

She sang them again, softer.

The empty air below the stage remained empty.

Tank knelt beside her. Put his hand on her back.

“We going get her.”

Rosa nodded without looking up.

Then she stood, took off her earrings, and put them in his pocket.

That was the moment the evening changed for him. A nigga can hear a hundred plans. Then the woman he loves puts something small and precious in his hand, and suddenly the plan has a heartbeat.

Storm drew a line through the air. A pale image of the lower annex appeared above the piano. The prison hung beneath the island in three connected buildings. One housed the cells. One held the laboratories. The lowest contained the circular chamber we had seen from our balcony.

“Pike's people will secure the upper service lift,” she said. “Bell will keep the secondary support field alive. I have to remain near the foundation controls. If Voss cuts the main current, somebody must catch the load.”

“You catching a whole school?” Tank asked.

“For a little while.”

He looked at me.

“Nigga, your teachers serious.”

Victor would come with us, but with his power restricted until Vega cleared him. He looked ready to object. The doctor lifted one finger. He closed his mouth.

Shawna tucked her hair back and checked the little light Pike had given her. No grand speech. No asking permission. My wife had heard a woman singing through a prison wall and had been heading toward her ever since.

I touched her wrist.

“Beside me.”

She touched my badge.

“Keep up.”

Nigga, that woman.

Bell opened the hatch.

Cold air rose through the stage.

We went down.

The first ladder led into a maintenance room full of old theater machinery. Beneath that, stairs followed the inside of the island's rock. The walls sweated. The music above us faded until all I heard was shoes, breathing, and the distant beat of some enormous machine.

Halfway down, a narrow window opened onto the night.

We were outside the island now, walking through a bridge bolted to its underside. Above us hung black stone. Below us spread stars reflected in a sea of clouds.

Tank looked through the window.

“Nigga, the floor got sky.”

“Don't study it.”

“I'm trying not to major in falling.”

At the first locked door, Marcellus used Sable's seal. The panel flashed green. Beyond it stood a guard with a cup of coffee.

He saw me.

Saw the badge.

Saw the gun.

He carefully placed the cup on the floor.

I appreciated a man who could read a room before it became his last room.

“Hands,” I said.

He raised them.

Pike cuffed him to a rail and asked where the prisoners had been taken. The guard looked toward the lower stairs.

“Transfer chamber.”

“Who ordered it?”

The nigga looked genuinely confused by the possibility of more than one answer.

“The chancellor.”

We kept moving.

The corridor beyond his post narrowed around a little electric freight cart. Its driver had abandoned it sideways with a stack of clean blankets tied to the back. One blanket had fallen over the wheel. A mug sat in the little holder beside the seat.

Tank looked at the mug.

“Everybody downstairs got coffee except the niggas doing the work.”

I moved the blanket. Beneath it, on the lower shelf, lay a bundle of ordinary clothes: a blue blouse, a man's belt, a pair of red shoes with one broken heel.

Shawna touched the blouse. There was a little paper label tied through a buttonhole.

LENA MARCH.

She looked up at me.

“We close.”

A noise came from behind the service door beside the cart.

Not footsteps.

Scraping.

Tank opened it.

A man in green overalls stood on a stool inside a supply closet, his hands tied above him to a pipe. The stool wobbled every time he tried to turn around. A roll of packing tape lay at his feet. He had been trying to scrape a nail against the binding.

“Don't move,” Tank said.

The man looked at his gun and immediately began moving more.

“Nigga, I mean don't fall. I came to help.”

Tank held the stool while I freed him. He dropped onto his feet and grabbed the doorframe. Shawna gave him water from her little bottle.

“Your name?” she asked.

“Dennis.”

He drank, coughed, drank again.

“Dennis Vale. Laundry.”

He was forty-one, with a wedding ring hanging loose on a finger he'd been rubbing raw against the pipe. His left shoe was untied. That detail made me angrier than I expected. These niggas had tied a man up so carelessly he hadn't even had time to finish being dressed.

“You work here?” I asked.

“Upstairs. I came down because the collection wasn't arriving. Found the clothes. Found names on things. Asked where the people were.”

Tank looked back into the closet.

“That was their answer?”

Dennis nodded.

He saw the badge again. His eyes widened.

“You're the man from the race.”

“Yeah.”

“My wife said if you ever came to dinner she'd use the good dishes.”

“Tell her I'm hungry.”

Nigga started laughing and crying at the same time. He tried to stop both and managed neither. Shawna held his shoulder until his breathing settled.

Pike spoke to one of his officers, who took Dennis toward the stairs. Before he left, Dennis caught my sleeve.

“The bridge ahead is open from the control side. They rolled the walking section away. I heard them talking before they put me in there.”

I looked down the passage. A heavy door stood at the end. Light showed beneath it.

“How far?”

“Across? Maybe twelve feet. The cart rail still runs along the wall.”

Tank looked at the little freight cart.

Then at me.

“Nigga, no.”

“I ain't said nothing.”

“You looking at a vehicle. That's a sentence when you do it.”

Victor bent over the little cart. It had two seats and a flatbed. Its rubber wheels sat on the floor; beside it a narrow steel guide ran toward the door and vanished underneath. The laundry carts used it when the walkway moved away for maintenance. Dennis showed us the hinged little guide wheels tucked beneath the chassis.

“Empty loads only when the bridge is retracted,” he said. “Normally.”

Tank shut his eyes.

“He gave the nigga a normally.”

“Tank, take the rear.”

“Nigga, I can't take the rear. The rear a washcloth.”

He climbed on anyway. Victor stayed behind to help Pike bring the others through once we had the walkway back. Shawna got into the seat beside me.

“Where's my seat belt?” she asked.

I looked down.

There wasn't one.

She took a blanket from the stack and looped it around the front rail, holding the ends in her lap.

“Nigga, drive like you married.”

I pressed the little pedal.

The cart beeped.

It did not move.

Tank looked at me.

“Somebody finally said no.”

Dennis reached through the window and released the brake.

Then we moved.

Slowly.

Nigga, offensively slowly. I had driven through fire. I had outrun bullets in a Mercedes. This little bitch traveled at the speed of a man deciding whether he had forgotten something in the refrigerator.

Behind us Victor began walking.

Kept pace.

Passed us.

He reached the door first and held it open.

“Enjoy your journey.”

“Your doctor know you using sarcasm?”

“Brief use.”

Tank leaned over my shoulder to inspect the instrument panel. It had a green bulb, a red bulb, and a sticker showing a cartoon hand being crushed by a wheel.

“Nigga, where the speedometer?”

“We don't need one.”

“That's the first honest thing you ever said in a vehicle.”

I kept the pedal down. The cart crept another few feet. Somewhere inside it a motor complained like we'd asked for a favor during lunch.

Shawna tightened her grip on the blanket.

“It does feel married,” she said.

“See?”

“Thirty years. Arguing about a parking receipt.”

Tank put his fist against his mouth. The laugh escaped anyway.

“Nigga, she aged the whole transmission.”

I found a little toggle beneath the panel and pointed at it.

“What's this?”

“Don't,” both of them said.

I looked at my wife. Then my brother.

“Niggas got harmony now.”

Victor called back from the doorway. “Reverse warning buzzer.”

Tank slapped my shoulder gently.

“Go on, race nigga. Make us slow and loud.”

I left the toggle alone. The tires hummed beneath us. Shawna leaned forward, checking the little guide wheels against the rail. The jokes hadn't stopped her counting the fasteners, looking at the gap, seeing exactly what could fail.

“Left one's seated,” she said. “Other?”

I checked. “Seated.”

Tank shifted the blanket bundle away from the rear wheel.

“Clear back here.”

There. Three people doing the work, underneath all the shit we gave one another. That was how I liked us best.

“Ready?” I asked.

Shawna nodded. Tank held the rear rail.

“Nigga, at this speed I got time to become ready twice.”

I looked toward the open doorway. The little motor kept whining.

“Don't let him hear you,” I told the cart. “You doing excellent.”

“Now this nigga emotionally supporting laundry,” Tank whispered.

The door opened onto the gap.

The whole middle of the passage was gone. Through it I saw the black underside of the island and a dizzy stretch of stars. A narrow guide rail ran along the outer wall to the other side, where two armed men stood beside the bridge controls.

They saw our little cart.

One laughed.

The other brought up his gun.

Tank stood on the flatbed with the blanket stack in front of his chest. Shawna fired at the hanging lamp above the guards. It burst. They ducked. I held the pedal down as if the little cart might discover a second personality if I believed in it hard enough.

Our front wheels left the ordinary floor.

The guide caught.

We hung against the wall, rolling above the empty air.

Tank stopped joking.

So did I.

For about two seconds.

Then the laundry cart played a little musical chime.

A cheerful four-note warning.

“Nigga,” Tank said, “is it singing us to death?”

“Low battery,” Shawna read from the dash.

We were halfway across.

Of course we were. A less ambitious nigga would've encountered this problem beside a charging socket.

The cart slowed. The guide wheels clicked over a join. On the far side the guards recovered, one reaching for his weapon while the other tried to close the heavy door in front of us.

I took my foot off the pedal.

“What you doing?” Tank said.

“Letting it think.”

Then I pressed down again.

The cart lurched forward on its last little act of loyalty. Shawna flung the blanket ahead of us. It caught the closing door's edge and bunched under it. The door stopped a hand's width short of its frame.

Our front bumper hit the blanket.

I stood and pushed.

Tank pushed behind me.

The door opened with all three of us still attached to the cart. We rolled through into the control room like the worst laundry delivery these niggas had ever received.

One guard swung at me. I pulled his dinner plate-sized control clipboard out of his hand and hit him with the blank side. The other tried to grab Shawna. She drove her elbow into his face and shoved him into Tank.

Tank set him down.

On the desk.

The desk stopped being one desk and became several smaller opportunities for carpentry.

The freight cart gave a final beep.

Died.

I patted its steering wheel.

“Good nigga.”

Shawna looked at the wrecked desk, the two groaning guards, and the little cart's blinking red light.

“You gonna name this one too?”

“Laundry Day.”

Tank started laughing before he could help it.

“Nigga, we almost died on Laundry Day.”

I found the bridge control. The walkway slid back across the gap with a long metallic groan. Victor and Pike came through first. Behind them Dennis stopped beside the cart and stared at the collapsed furniture.

“It only goes eleven miles an hour,” he said.

I handed him his mug.

“That's all I needed.”

He took it with both hands.

“My wife is getting out the fucking dishes.”

The next door opened into a long cell gallery. Some doors stood open. Others remained locked. Men and women watched us from behind thick glass. All adults. Some wore plain gray clothing. Some still wore pieces of whatever lives they had been taken out of: a mechanic's shirt, a dancer's leggings, a suit jacket with the lining torn.

A tall man pressed his palm to the glass.

“Are we being moved?”

I stopped outside his cell.

“You being let out.”

He looked at the badge again.

“They said—”

“Nigga, they said a lot. I'm speaking now.”

Shawna opened the first door with the seal. Rosa opened the second. Petra took one look at a jammed third door, turned her hands to marble, and removed its disagreement from the frame.

Mercedes guided the people toward Pike's officers.

“Up the stairs. Stay together. If you can help somebody walk, do it. If you can't, say so. Nobody gotta perform for us.”

A woman with silver burns along her forearms stared at Petra.

“You could break the whole wall.”

Petra glanced at Mercedes.

Mercedes smiled.

“Girl, don't encourage her unless we need it.”

We needed it about fifteen seconds later.

The far security door slammed shut. Red light washed the gallery. A voice ordered everybody to remain where they were.

Everybody kept moving.

That voice had plainly not met my people.

Two guards appeared on the upper walkway. Their weapons came down toward the escaping prisoners. I fired first. One dropped out of sight. The other ducked behind the rail.

Tank lifted a steel bench and held it over three people trying to cross the open floor. Bullets struck it with flat, ugly bangs. He kept walking.

“Nigga, this bench better appreciate its promotion.”

Victor ran beside him carrying the chair leg from the casino.

I looked at him.

He looked at the chair leg.

“It's been useful.”

“I ain't judging.”

He threw it at the second guard.

It hit square across the man's hands.

Victor smiled for the first time since we came underground.

“Very useful.”

Petra punched the security door off its hinges. The noise traveled down the gallery like a cannon shot. Beyond it lay a control room with three startled technicians and a supervisor already halfway toward another exit.

Shawna pointed her gun at him.

“Sit.”

He sat.

“Where's Lena March?”

He looked at the technicians.

“Nigga,” she said, “they ain't going to answer for you.”

The supervisor pointed toward the lower stairs.

“She resisted the transfer.”

Rosa moved before he finished.

Tank followed her.

I left Pike with the controls and went down after them with Shawna and Victor. The stairs opened onto a medical corridor. Doors stood ajar. Beds rolled against the walls. In one room, a woman lay on her side humming through clenched teeth while a guard tried to fasten a restraint around her wrist.

Tank caught the guard by the back of his uniform.

The nigga left the room horizontally.

Rosa went to the bed.

“Lena?”

The woman opened her eyes.

She was thin, brown-haired, with a bruise beneath one eye and a look so fierce it reached us before her voice did.

“You're the singer.”

“So are you.”

Rosa undid the restraint. Shawna helped Lena sit. Tank returned from placing the guard in a condition where he would remain available for later questions.

Lena took one step and nearly fell.

He caught her.

“I can walk,” she said.

“Then walk with assistance. Ain't no extra prize for hurting.”

She studied his enormous arm, then nodded.

“Reiss,” she said. “They took Dr. Reiss downstairs. He tried to stop them.”

I looked at Shawna.

She looked back.

There was always another downstairs.

We took Lena to Vega and the evacuation group. Twenty-seven prisoners had come out of the cells. Two needed stretchers. The rest insisted on helping carry them. A man whose hands shook with uncontrolled sparks wrapped them in his own coat so he could support someone without hurting them.

I watched him for one second longer than I needed.

Then the upper lift stopped.

The lights over its doors went red. Pike spoke into his radio, listened, and swore. Voss's men had cut access from the annex to the service bridge. The people we had taken out of cells were now crowded into a corridor with one locked route home.

I could feel fear trying to turn them backward. Shoulders drew in. Eyes went to the open cells. A woman began shaking her head as if the whole rescue had been a particularly cruel rehearsal.

Lena pushed herself away from Vega's arm.

“Let me hear it,” she said.

Pike held out his radio.

Sable's voice came over the security channel, ordering the upper guards to keep the bridge sealed. Lena listened with her eyes closed. Her lips moved once, shaping the other woman's cadence.

Rosa looked at her.

“You can do that?”

“I can sing through walls. Words aren't harder.”

She held out her hand. Pike gave her the radio.

When she spoke, Sable came out of her mouth.

“Open the upper bridge. We are conducting an inspection. Anyone who obstructs this order will answer directly to me.”

I stared at her.

Tank stared too.

“This woman stole a whole bitch,” he whispered.

Lena nearly laughed. Caught herself. Spoke again in Sable's clipped voice.

“Now.”

The red lights turned green.

The lift doors opened.

People began moving, faster now. Not pushed. Choosing. The woman who had shaken her head helped lift a stretcher. The sparking man took its other end. Lena stood by the doorway and kept Sable's voice talking long enough to buy them the corridor beyond.

Then the real Sable shouted over her.

“That is not my order.”

Lena's own voice returned.

“I know.”

She gave Pike the radio with a smile so sharp it could have cut its cord.

We heard boots approaching along the bridge.

Petra stepped into the lift with Mercedes. “We'll hold the top.”

Pike joined them with two officers. The rest stayed beside the stretchers. Nobody asked which people were powerful enough to deserve first place. The people who could walk made space for the ones who couldn't. The ones with strength used it. The ones without still had hands.

A man in a torn suit stopped beside me as the lift filled.

He was maybe fifty, with gray in his beard and one lens missing from his glasses. He studied my badge as if he had been waiting for it longer than I had been alive.

“Who sent you?”

I looked at Shawna.

She stood at the stairwell, watching the lower door, ready for the next thing that needed doing.

“My wife heard a song.”

The man followed my gaze.

Then he took off his broken glasses and wiped his eyes with his sleeve.

“Tell her thank you.”

“Tell her yourself upstairs.”

He nodded. Put his glasses back on. Went into the lift carrying somebody else's bag.

Lena waited until the last stretcher cleared before she let Vega guide her inside. Rosa went with her, still holding her hand. Tank watched until the doors closed.

“That nigga in the gray suit,” he said quietly. “He look like my uncle.”

“Yeah?”

“My uncle would've been asking who stole his shoes the whole time.”

I looked at the lift doors. At the boots and shoes people had left behind in their cells because nobody had expected to need them quickly.

“We buying shoes,” I said.

Tank nodded.

“A lot of fucking shoes.”

Then we went toward the machine.

The transfer chamber was round, high-ceilinged, and bright with a cold yellow light. Rails crossed its floor into the metal circle at the far end. Above the circle, a clock displayed a date that belonged to somebody else's lifetime.

1944.

Snow blew through the open frame.

Real snow. It settled on the floor and melted around our shoes.

Beyond the circle stood a dim station building under a night sky. A train waited beside it. Men moved with lanterns. One wore a military coat with an emblem I recognized even before my brain caught up with what my eyes had been handed.

A Nazi.

Not a costume at a party. Not some present-day idiot trying to be provocative. A man standing in a different year, carrying a gun, waiting for human beings to be delivered to him.

My stomach went cold in a way the snow couldn't explain.

At the control dais, Sable held a pistol beside the head of an older man in a rumpled shirt.

Dr. Reiss.

He looked at us over his glasses.

“Do not damage the ring,” he said immediately.

“Nigga, we just got here,” Tank said.

Sable tightened her grip on him.

“Back away.”

I stopped.

Shawna stopped beside me.

Victor's hands glimmered faintly. He closed them into fists before the charge could grow.

“Let him go,” I said.

Sable laughed once.

“You keep arriving at the end of things you don't understand.”

“And somehow niggas keep getting free.”

Behind her, a black carriage rolled toward the circle. Three adults inside it pressed their faces to a barred opening. On the other side, a soldier raised his hand to receive them.

I could not shoot without risking Reiss.

Shawna saw the same thing.

She looked at the carriage, then at the hanging work light above the control dais.

I knew her face when a decision arrived.

“Baby,” I said.

She shot the light.

Darkness hit half the room.

Reiss dropped.

Sable fired where his head had been and stumbled back as Tank charged. I ran for the carriage. Victor went toward the controls. Shawna covered both of us from behind the rail support.

The carriage reached the circle.

Cold air swallowed my arms as I grabbed its rear handle.

The soldier on the other side saw me.

His face changed at the sight of my clothes, my badge, my skin, and the fact that none of those things had asked his permission to be there.

He raised his gun.

I shot first.

He fell behind the platform edge.

I hauled the carriage back.

The wheels fought me. Three people pushed from inside, trying to help through the same floor that trapped them. My shoes slid in melting snow.

“Tank!”

He came out of the dark, grabbed beside me, and planted his feet.

“This nigga train going the wrong way.”

We pulled together.

The carriage rolled clear of the circle.

Behind us, Sable disappeared through a side passage. Victor caught Reiss before he could strike the floor. Shawna broke the carriage latch with the control lever somebody had left beside it.

The three prisoners came out holding each other.

One man kept saying, “Thank you,” in a language I didn't know, then found the same words in English and said them again.

I touched his shoulder.

“Go with her.”

On the other side of the ring, a whistle shrieked. More soldiers appeared beside the train.

Reiss struggled upright.

“Close it.”

Victor looked at the controls.

“Which—”

“The black handle. Slowly.”

Victor moved it.

The circle narrowed from the edges inward. Snow became a thin white line. Through the shrinking opening I saw a woman standing near the station wall, one hand pressed against her mouth, watching us take people away from the men who owned her night.

Our eyes met across eighty years of machinery and somebody's monstrous ambition.

Then the door closed.

The room was only a room again.

I stood with both hands on the empty carriage handle.

Shawna came beside me. Her shoulder touched mine.

“We saw her,” she said.

“Yeah.”

“She saw us.”

I looked at the blank metal circle.

Behind me Tank swore softly. Reiss began to cry without making a sound. Upstairs, people we had freed were walking toward the music they had heard through a wall.

I let go of the handle.

“Then she know somebody coming back.”

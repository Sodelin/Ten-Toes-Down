Commander Pike offered me a patrol car. A regular patrol car. Nigga had watched me turn a Lamborghini into a rescue platform and decided what I needed next was a sedan with enough trunk space for cones.

I looked out his window at my Lamborghini waiting below.

He looked too.

“That was a stupid offer,” he said.

“Not stupid. Hopeful.”

Tank looked at the patrol car through the window. “That nigga got cloth seats.”

Pike cleared his throat. “They're durable.”

“Nigga, so is my aunt's couch. I ain't chasing nobody in it.”

“Your aunt got a very aggressive couch,” I reminded him.

“That's different. That nigga been through things.”

Pike looked between us, trying to decide whether he had hired two idiots or discovered a form of policing his academy had failed to teach. Then he glanced at my gold badge and chose faith.

Campus patrol occupied a stone building between a bakery and a store selling capes. The bakery smelled better than any police station I had visited. The cape store had a line outside. People apparently bought new identities the way niggas in Vegas bought belts.

Pike spread a map over his desk. The academy occupied the largest island. Six smaller districts floated around it, joined by marked lanes sustained by the school's field. If you stayed on those lanes with the right receivers, your car could drive through open air. Leave the field and gravity remembered your ass immediately.

The largest satellite was called the Crown District. Hotels, restaurants, showrooms, a marina for airships, and the Cloud Nine casino. Finn Cinder's car had headed there after leaving the academy.

“Can your people follow him?” I asked.

“They can. He knows our cars.”

I turned my badge over in my hand.

“Then let's introduce him to mine.”

Tank drove with me. Victor asked to come, and I told him there was no third seat.

“I can fly.”

“Nigga, why you asking for a ride?”

He looked at Pike. Pike found a fascinating place on his map to point at until Victor left.

Shawna had plans of her own. The dean had invited her and Rosa to rehearse with the academy's evening orchestra. My wife had heard “orchestra” and “doors ordinarily locked to visitors” in the same sentence and decided music deserved her immediate attention.

Mercedes went with Petra to see the lower training gardens. Porsche announced she was going shopping. She said it with such innocence I gave her a second look.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“Exactly. Mind your police business.”

That woman could sell rain to the clouds and make them apologize for being dry.

We left the patrol station under a bright afternoon sun. The Lamborghini rolled between white pillars and onto the first sky lane. Blue strips of light ran beneath the tires. Beyond either side was nothing but air.

Tank stared out his window.

“I miss curbs.”

“You used to hit every curb in Vegas.”

“That's how I knew they was there.”

He unfolded Pike's map across his knees. It covered the glove compartment, his right hand, and a substantial amount of faith.

“Nigga, why the map got a back?”

“Roads below roads.”

“That's stacking problems.”

Victor's voice came through the radio. “Take the next left.”

Tank looked up. Victor floated above the lane with the leisure of a nigga whose vehicle was himself.

“Which next left?” Tank asked.

“The turn beneath me.”

“Nigga, everything is beneath you. That's the whole issue.”

I laughed. Tank didn't. He flattened the map against the dash and found the blue line with his thumb.

“We need Crown. This left goes back to the stadium.”

There was a short silence.

“The following left,” Victor said.

“Look at that. This nigga just invented the next next left.”

“Thank you, Tank.”

My brother paused, pleased despite himself, then ruined the humility by nodding at me.

“You heard the airborne nigga. Valuable team member.”

“You've been valuable.”

“Say it with directional respect.”

I took his turn. The lane curved beneath a stone bridge and the Crown District opened ahead, windows stacked in sunlight. Tank folded the map down to one manageable square.

“I got you,” he said.

“I know.”

He settled back and adjusted the vent toward his face. The air smelled faintly of the bakery we'd left behind. My brother looked almost comfortable.

Then the lane dipped. He caught the door handle with his whole hand.

“Nigga, road got a basement.”

“You just explained roads below roads.”

“On paper. I approved that shit on paper.”

Victor laughed over the radio, finally too entertained to pretend he was above us in any useful sense.

Tank pointed upward through the windshield.

“Don't laugh, flying nigga. You still on navigation probation.”

Ahead of us the Crown District glittered. A red sports car passed on the other side of the lane, driven by a man with gold skin and a woman wearing a hat shaped like a small thunderstorm. Rain fell exclusively into her champagne glass.

Tank watched them go.

“Rosa gonna want that.”

“The hat?”

“The champagne weather.”

We spotted Finn outside a showroom called ASCENT. He stood between a black coupe and a salesman whose smile looked physically painful. Two guards waited near the door. None wore masks now. Finn had a strip of tape over the eyebrow I had helped him lose.

I parked across the road.

He saw the Lamborghini before he saw me.

That gave him half a second to enjoy life.

Then I stepped out.

“Finn.”

His face changed.

The salesman looked relieved someone else had become his problem.

“Captain,” Finn said. “We keep meeting.”

“That's because niggas keep letting you go.”

He looked up. Victor floated above the showroom roof, arms folded, electricity crawling over his shoulders.

Finn smiled at him.

“You get your bracelet off?”

Victor descended six feet.

I raised a hand.

“I got this.”

Finn's guards shifted.

Tank leaned against the Lamborghini and opened his jacket just enough for them to understand the local temperature.

“Y'all can keep standing,” he said. “Just stand peaceful.”

I approached Finn. “Who paid you to hit the hall?”

“You're assuming money.”

“Nigga, your shoes cost six thousand dollars and you look allergic to principles. I'm assuming correctly.”

The salesman made a little noise that might have been laughter and immediately pretended to cough.

Finn's eyes went flat.

“You don't know the city you're in.”

“Then show me.”

He did.

The black coupe leaped backward through the showroom window.

Glass burst into the street. Finn dove through the open passenger door as the car spun. One guard threw a smoke sphere. Tank caught it, looked offended, and threw it back.

The sphere burst under the man's feet.

His first cough sounded like regret.

The coupe shot onto the sky lane.

I was in the Lamborghini before the broken glass finished falling.

Tank landed beside me.

“Nigga, we arresting him or racing him?”

“He picking the format.”

I hit the siren.

Blue lights lit the clouds beneath us.

The Lamborghini lunged forward. Finn's car swerved around a delivery van and accelerated toward the Crown District's outer loop. I followed. The road curved around a hotel whose swimming pool hung over open air. People at the pool stood up when we passed. A woman waved. Her companion stared at the police lights and spilled an entire tray of drinks into his own lap.

Victor flew above us, keeping pace.

“He has two more vehicles joining,” he said through the patrol radio Pike had given me.

“Where?”

Two silver cars came out of a side lane.

“There.”

“Useful timing, nigga.”

The first silver car struck my rear quarter. The Lamborghini slid toward the lane's luminous edge. For one second Tank's window showed nothing below us at all.

He turned to me very calmly.

“Aiden.”

“I know.”

I steered into the slide, let the rear swing, and put us back between the blue strips. The silver car tried again. This time I braked. It shot past, clipped Finn's coupe, and bounced toward the outer barrier.

Victor caught it with a web of crackling light.

The driver stared through his windshield at the man holding his car over the clouds.

Victor smiled without warmth.

“Wait here.”

He set the car on a maintenance bay and welded its doors shut with a bright hiss. It wasn't going anywhere until somebody with tools and a better attitude arrived.

The second silver car pulled alongside us. A gun came through its window.

Tank leaned back as a shot shattered my mirror.

“Nigga shot the mirror.”

“I saw.”

“That's how we see other niggas.”

He returned fire. The attacking car veered off, sparks skipping from its door. I drove between it and a tourist coach full of adults in matching yellow jackets. The coach passengers flattened themselves against the opposite windows.

One of them gave me a thumbs-up.

I gave it back.

Customer service did not stop because somebody was trying to kill me.

Finn headed for a spiral lane climbing around a hotel tower. I followed him up. Faster. The engine climbed toward its beautiful, furious scream. Wind punched through the broken mirror housing. Tank laughed the way he always laughed when terror finally realized it had come to the wrong man.

We burst onto the hotel's roof.

A wedding reception was taking place there.

White chairs. Gold glasses. A fountain full of flowers. Two adult grooms stood beneath an arch, both in immaculate suits, both looking at Finn's car as it tore between their guests.

He knocked over the cake.

I will admit that made it personal in a new way.

“Hold tight.”

“Nigga, to what?”

I drove onto the narrow stone rim around the fountain. The Lamborghini rose sideways. Water flashed beneath Tank's door. We passed the fallen cake on two wheels, cleared a table, and landed in front of the arch.

Both grooms stared.

“Congratulations,” I shouted.

Then we were gone.

Behind us the wedding guests began cheering.

Ahead, Finn took the departure ramp toward the smaller northern island. The black coupe's rear lights flickered. Something unfolded from its sides: short angular wings, illegal enough that the patrol radio immediately began shouting about them.

“He's leaving the lane,” Pike warned.

Finn's car lifted.

My car did not have wings.

Bell had been very clear about that.

“Remember what the four-arm nigga said.”

“I remember.”

“You got the remembering face and the doing-it-anyway face. Right now they fighting.”

I took the service lane.

Darkness swallowed us. The engine thundered against stone. Ahead, daylight shrank to a small white rectangle.

Tank braced both feet.

We came out under Finn's car.

Not flying. Driving up the last steep piece of lane at exactly the moment he drifted over it.

The Lamborghini's roof struck the underside of his wing.

The wing snapped backward.

The black coupe rolled, skidded across the loading platform, and hit a stack of empty crates with a crash that sent wood flying all the way to a restaurant balcony.

I stopped beside it.

The Lamborghini's hood smoked.

Tank stared at me.

“Nigga.”

“What?”

“Efficient.”

He began laughing so hard he had to lean against the door before he got out.

Tank drew.

Finn stopped reaching.

Voss's guards stepped out.

Six of them. Weapons lowered but ready. At their center stood a woman with close-cropped red hair and a black uniform stitched with the downward wing. She looked about forty. Her voice could have broken a glass without raising its volume.

He began with a car, which was the first sensible thing the man had done all day.

Three months earlier, Voss's red-haired security commander had hired him to transport a sealed engine housing from the Crown District to the lower annex. Her name was Commander Sable. She paid well, asked no questions about his history, and made it clear she expected the same courtesy. Finn had driven the housing down the service ramp at midnight and waited while two technicians unloaded it beside a circular metal frame.

“A chronal aperture.”

He faced it.

“One,” he said.

# 19. Valedictorian Again, Because Niggas Keep Graduating Me

Single narrator: the funeral ticket man. Verbatim chapter body.

I woke up with Shawna asleep against my chest and Tank arguing with a nurse about fruit. Nigga, I knew immediately I had survived. Death might have had trumpets. It did not have my brother explaining that a pear was now an emotional subject. He can have one when he wakes, the nurse said. This nigga saved a school. And his stomach needs rest.

His greatness don't. His greatness can wait until breakfast. He got Blue Thunder, Tank said. He also has a chart. Nigga, you can't put thunder on a chart. I put his vomiting on it without difficulty. The room went quiet. Tank shifted in his chair. I kept my eyes closed a little longer, enjoying the rare sound of my brother losing at full strength.

I ain't saying give him a buffet, he said. Just have the pear ready. It is ready. Emotionally ready. The nurse must have looked at him. I could hear that look from the bed. Would you like to hold it? A pause. Yes. Nigga took the pear.

I knew because the bowl made a little scrape, then his whole voice became quieter, closer to his chest. Thank you. I had heard Tank negotiate with dangerous men using fewer words than he'd spent on that fruit. He'd sounded calmer then, too. My brother wanted a small thing he could have ready for me. A pear. A laugh.

Something he could give with those enormous hands when I opened my eyes. I let him have another second. Then the nurse said, Please stop polishing it on your shirt. Nigga, it's a clean shirt. It has been outside. So has the pear. I washed the pear. And Rosa washed the shirt. We both brought something to this, ma'am. She laughed despite herself.

A short sound, immediately recovered. Set it in the bowl. On top? Yes. Another small clink. The pear had received preferred placement. There, Tank said. Now when this nigga wakes up, his shit organized. My chest hurt. Laughing would make it worse. I was going to do it anyway, and this hospital deserved at least the courtesy of a warning. I opened one eye.

Nigga, let the woman work. Tank turned so fast his chair scraped across the floor. His face opened into a grin that told me how frightened he had been while I wasn't awake to make it easier. There he is. Shawna lifted her head. For one second she looked lost between sleep and the last thing she remembered fearing.

Then she saw my eyes open and put her hand against my cheek. Hey. Hey, baby. She kissed me carefully. That scared me more than a hard kiss would have. How bad I look? Like somebody hit a very expensive statue with a truck. Statue still handsome? Obnoxiously. I relaxed. Vega appeared at the door with Storm behind her.

The doctor checked me before she let anybody explain the outside world. My ribs were bruised, my shoulder strained, my hands burned, and my body thoroughly offended by being used as the temporary pathway for the Heart's charge. Nothing about the new ability had healed the old wounds. Nothing about the title people were shouting in the corridors changed the fact that I needed sleep.

No powers today, Vega said. Nigga, I just got them. Then you can enjoy knowing where they are. Storm laughed softly. The dean looked better. Tired, but no longer holding the sky up through force of will and a series of increasingly personal disagreements. She sat beside the bed and told me the school was stable. Bell and Miriam had severed the old tower's controls.

The real Heart remained inside the restored central housing. Voss's armor and the stolen coupling were secured. His surviving guards had surrendered. Sable and Finn remained in custody, separately, so neither could improve the other's story before Pike finished asking questions. The time door had been dismantled completely. Reiss had watched the final ring come off its mount.

Then he had gone upstairs and slept in a room with an unlocked door. I listened with Shawna's hand in mine. When Storm finished, I asked about the people. All sixty-two returnees were safe. The thirty rescued before the historical mission were safe too.

Dennis from the laundry had gone home with his own keys and had already returned to work long enough to tell his supervisor which locked storage rooms he would never enter again. Nobody had required gratitude as a condition of staying. People were calling families, arranging rooms, asking questions, refusing questions, resting.

The ones who had come from 1944 were taking the new world at the pace they could bear. That seemed right to me. Some surprises deserved more than an afternoon. Storm took my unbandaged wrist gently. You changed this place. I looked at the window. Beyond it the academy floated in bright morning air, scarred in places, beautiful in others, still very much a school.

It needed a little parking correction. She smiled. Yes. Apparently it did. For two days I was forbidden to leave the medical tower without somebody sensible beside me. Tank volunteered. Vega looked at him for so long he withdrew the application himself. Shawna took me walking instead. Slowly at first. Across the balcony. Down one corridor.

Eventually into the garden where Harlan Moss was repairing the damaged paths with roots coaxed carefully through the stone. He wore the polished brown shoes Tank had bought him. That afternoon his wife arrived. Anne came up the garden steps carrying a small overnight bag and stopped when she saw him.

She was a little shorter than Shawna, with graying curls and a face that seemed to lose five months of exhaustion in one breath. Harlan dropped the pruning shears. Neither of them ran at first. They walked toward each other, slowly enough to believe each step. Then she reached him and hit his chest with both hands before folding herself against it.

You still have those shoes, she said through tears. He looked down at the brown leather. New pair. Same fool wearing them. Your fool. She cried harder. Tank stood beside me with his mouth pressed into a line. Rosa slipped her hand into his. He squeezed it without looking away from the couple. Good shoes, he said quietly. I nodded. Excellent fucking shoes.

A week later Bell let me sit in the Lamborghini again. He had repaired every dent except one small mark near the rear fender, which he said he would preserve if I wanted a reminder of the train. Gold badge emblems now shone on the doors, and a discreet second strip of police lights sat above the windshield. Bell said a graduate deserved appropriate trim.

Nigga, the car had received formalwear. I looked at it. Leave that. Miriam stood beside him in a clean blue work coat. She had spent the week learning the academy's engines one careful question at a time. The floating roads still astonished her. The mechanical problems did not. You drive as if roads are suggestions, she told me. They been flexible.

The car has not always appreciated your confidence. Bell nodded so vigorously all four hands moved. Thank you. She looked at him. But it is a remarkable car. He nearly cried again. Nigga, I was going to start carrying tissues for that man. My new ability came with lessons, which I considered rude but useful.

Storm and Victor helped me learn the difference between feeling charge and having enough to spend. Bell watched what happened when I passed it through the car. Vega watched what happened to me afterward and ended every session before my pride could become an injury. One short burst could sharpen my reactions and send blue current through my hands.

A car could carry that charge into a brief surge of acceleration. Afterward I shook, sweated, and tired quickly. Trying to hold it longer made everything worse. I could not fly. I could not call up an endless storm. I could not fix a wound by admiring it.

The body beneath the light still needed food, rest, and a wife willing to say, Nigga, enough, with final authority. I had that wife. The power had no chance of making me completely unmanageable. Only impressively difficult. Two weeks after Voss fell, the academy held its graduation. They put my name first on the program. Then they put it in larger print than the title.

Tank unfolded the paper and stared at it. Nigga, this school's graduating from you. They learned a lot. You attended one class. Intensively. The ceremony took place in the restored stadium. Every seat was full. Helicopters and airships hovered beyond the upper rail, carrying guests who had decided a normal chair lacked ambition. The orchestra sat beneath a blue canopy. Rosa sang the opening number.

Lena joined her for the second verse, and their voices rose through the open air without a wall between them. Shawna sat in the front row wearing the ruby Claudine had given us, set now in a simple pendant Marcellus made to keep the stone close to her skin. The red flashed against her blue dress.

She caught me looking and smiled as if the whole stadium existed mainly to give us more room for that smile. I wore a dark suit, my gold badge, the cross, and an honors robe so heavy Tank said they had stitched my ego into the lining. He lifted one corner of it between two fingers. Nigga, this is upholstery. Academic distinction.

You got a sofa's worth. Porsche came along the row carrying a little sewing kit. She caught my sleeve, found a loose thread, and clipped it with the concentration she'd brought to stealing Sable's case. Hold still. Everybody been telling me that all week. And yet, nigga, here we are. Victor straightened his own robe. Mine's lighter. Tank looked him over. You took more classes.

They trusted you with less fabric. That almost makes sense. Don't encourage the nigga, I said. Porsche stepped back and inspected me. Turn. I turned. Other way. I turned again. Tank nodded solemnly. Nigga doing prerequisites. Mercedes came up from the front row to retrieve Porsche, saw the state of us, and fixed Victor's collar without asking.

He stood very still, plainly uncertain which institutional rule governed being fussed over. There, she said. Now you look like the nice one. I am the nice one. Nigga, let people discover it. He laughed. I did too. The orchestra tuned behind us, little fragments of sound finding where they belonged. No alarms. No metal tearing.

Just a hundred people briefly making an ugly noise so they could make a beautiful one together. Tank brushed the last bit of lint from my shoulder. You good? Yeah. He pressed his palm there once, firm and warm. Good, nigga. Then Rosa waved him toward his seat, and he went without a single argument. Victor watched him go. Does she have powers?

Nigga, I said, you keep asking that question. Victor stood beside me in blue. He had his own honors. He had earned them. When the dean called his name, I stood before anybody else and cheered loud enough to make him laugh on the way to the platform. That nigga had tried to beat me.

Then he had helped me save people who would never have known his name otherwise. I wanted the whole sky to know it now. Petra graduated with distinction in rescue strength. She accepted her award, turned toward Mercedes, and kissed her in front of the entire faculty.

Mercedes looked startled for one beautiful second, then put both arms around her and made the applause last longer. Porsche stood up cheering. Leon became three men beside her and cheered three times as loudly. Knox leaned toward Pike. Do all three need separate identification? Pike closed his eyes. Please let me have one peaceful afternoon.

Before Storm called my name, the great screen replayed our arrival: the broken train, my car beneath the platform, Shawna pulling people through the carriage door. Tank watched himself catch three people at once and nodded with satisfaction. Nigga, that man strong. That's you, Rosa said. Exactly. Camera telling the truth. Then Storm said my name. The stadium stood. All of it.

Nigga, even some people who had been sitting comfortably on air came down so they could stand properly. I walked onto the platform beneath the repaired finish arch. The dean held a diploma in one hand and a gold captain's insignia in the other. Aiden, she said, Top Graduate Captain of Sky High. The roar rolled across the clouds.

She waited for it to quiet, failed, and continued over it. For extraordinary command, ingenuity, courage, and devotion to those placed in his care. Tank shouted, And the driving! And the driving, Storm agreed. The engineering faculty stood and applauded as one wounded profession. She gave me the diploma. Then another. I looked at it. What's this one? An honorary distinction for your vehicle. Nigga.

My car had graduated. I turned toward Tank. He had both hands on his head. This nigga ride more educated than my cousins! I held up both diplomas. The stadium lost its fucking mind. Storm stepped aside for my speech. I looked down at the paper somebody had offered me backstage. Then folded it and put it in my pocket. Aight, I said.

The room settled into the kind of quiet a man felt in his chest. First, I want everybody to appreciate that I arrived here late, parked wrong, destroyed a platform, missed most of my classes, and still graduated first. Laughter broke across the stands. That's not advice. Most niggas try that and wind up walking home with a bill. More laughter. I looked at Shawna.

I came because somebody saw something I did in Vegas and figured I ought to try it higher up. I brought my people because everywhere I go gets better when they there. Then I met more people. Some tried to beat me. Some fed me. Some fixed the car after I made their profession nervous. Some were singing where nobody could hear them.

Lena looked up from beside Rosa. I hear you now, I said. She pressed her lips together and nodded. I raised the diploma. This got my name on it. Fine. Nigga, I like my name.

But every person who held a door, carried a stretcher, told the truth, brought a shoe, flew back for a friend, or stood beside somebody who couldn't stand alone—you're in it. I let the silence hold that. Then I smiled. My wife's still getting the good parking space. The stadium roared again. Shawna stood, laughing, and lifted both hands toward me.

I came down from the platform before anybody could announce another honor. Went straight to her. Put my arms around her waist. How I do? She looked at the diplomas, the badge, the cheering school, the car waiting beside the field with a blue ribbon tied around its mirror. Then she looked at me. Damn, boy. I kissed her before she finished.

Nigga, I knew the rest by heart.

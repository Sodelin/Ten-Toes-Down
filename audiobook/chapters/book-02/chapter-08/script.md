# 8. A Nigga Can't Be Late to Yesterday

Single narrator: the funeral ticket man. Verbatim chapter body.

They tried to give me a medal before breakfast. Nigga, I had blood on my cuff, no sleep in my eyes, and a wife leaning against my shoulder who had helped liberate a prison before most of the faculty remembered to put their slippers on. The medal could have waited until somebody brought eggs. The faculty disagreed.

They assembled in the main hall with a blue ribbon, a gold star, and enough respectful silence to make Tank suspicious. What these niggas want? he whispered. Apparently me. Everybody want you. I'm asking what format. Storm stepped forward. She looked exhausted. Faint silver lines trembled around her wrists where she had held the casino upright through the Heart's unstable link.

She had changed into a plain white shirt and dark trousers, but somehow still looked like the person a storm asked before making landfall. For exceptional courage in the rescue of the lower-annex prisoners, she said, and for returning the Heart of the Sky to this academy— I lifted a hand. She stopped. My people, I said. I pointed at Shawna. At Tank and Rosa.

At Mercedes, Porsche, Petra, Victor, Pike, Bell, Vega, Marcellus. At Lena sitting in a chair nearby with a blanket around her shoulders. Whatever that sentence is about to give me, make enough. Storm looked down at the medal. Then she smiled. We will. Tank leaned toward Rosa. Nigga got us jewelry through collective bargaining. You don't know what that means.

I know I'm collectively about to shine. The room laughed. The freed prisoners laughed too, some cautiously, some like they were surprised their bodies still remembered where laughter lived. I let Storm put the ribbon around my neck. Then I put the medal around Shawna's. She looked at me. You earned this. Baby, I got you. That was corny as fuck.

It was also true enough to make her eyes go bright. Breakfast finally arrived. We ate in the main hall because nobody wanted to leave the people still being examined by the medical team. Tables filled with food, borrowed clothes, shoes in every size, and phones people could use to call somebody who had not known where they were. Tank personally distributed the shoes.

He knelt in front of the gray-suited man, who introduced himself as Mr. Harlan Moss, fifty-two, a landscape architect whose gift let him persuade roots to grow through stone. He had been taken after refusing to work on one of Voss's private properties. Tank offered him two pairs. Moss looked at them. Both? Nigga, you got two kinds of occasions.

Moss laughed and chose the polished brown pair for now. He set the boots beside his chair with the careful pleasure of a man arranging evidence that tomorrow might exist. His phone call came through while Tank was lacing the boots for him. A woman filled the screen. Gray curls, a blue bathrobe, eyes so wide she looked almost angry with him. Harlan?

He set the phone against a water glass. Missed. Caught it before it fell. Anne. She covered her mouth. He covered his. For a few seconds the niggas couldn't say anything at all. Tank stopped touching the boots. I put my coffee down.

Around us breakfast kept happening, but slowly, as if everybody near that table had understood there was a voice trying to find its way home. Anne dropped her hand first. Where the fuck have you been? Harlan started laughing. Then crying. Then trying to answer both questions with the same breath. I've had a bad few months. I can see that. Your hair looks terrible.

He touched it. She began crying too. Nigga, there are people who need a whole orchestra to tell somebody they love them. That woman had done it with a complaint about his hair, and Harlan heard every note. He turned the phone to show her the room. Blankets. Breakfast. Shoes.

My badge, which she studied with the ferocity of a woman deciding whether to trust the next thing a man in a uniform told her. Did you find him? she asked me. My wife did. Then put her on. I waved Shawna over.

She took the phone, sat beside Harlan, and listened to Anne thank her so many times she finally had to say, Girl, breathe. We got him. Harlan looked at his boots. She bought me shoes before our wedding, he told Tank. I'd spent everything on the ring. Tank nodded gravely. Respectable allocation. They were brown. Like these.

I wanted black because I thought black looked distinguished. What happened? Harlan looked at Anne on the screen. What do you think happened? Tank looked at Rosa across the room. Brown shoes. Brown shoes, Harlan agreed. He told us about their wedding while Anne corrected him from the phone. It had rained. The roof had leaked over the little rented chapel's piano.

His brother had put a cooking pot under the leak and called it percussion. Anne's mother had objected to the music. Anne had walked down the aisle anyway, carrying her own umbrella, and closed it over her mother's face just long enough to finish saying yes. That isn't how it happened, Anne said. Harlan smiled at the screen.

You told your sister you wished it had. My sister talks too much. She sent me the letter. Anne stopped. Then she laughed so hard the picture shook.

That was their whole marriage coming through a little glowing rectangle: the rain, the ridiculous shoes, a letter somebody had kept, an argument that had survived thirty years because neither of them wanted to win it enough to stop having it together. I looked at Shawna. She looked back. Thirty years sounded like something a nigga ought to earn one morning at a time.

Anne's face steadied on the screen. I'm coming, she said. Harlan looked around the floating school. It's a little complicated to get here. Nigga, I spent five months trying to get you back. Don't tell me about parking. Tank bowed his head so she wouldn't see him laughing. Pike arranged a ground escort to the academy's landing station. Storm offered a shuttle.

By the time Anne ended the call, Harlan had said her name so often it had become the easiest word in the room. He put the phone down gently. Then he looked at me. I don't know what to give you. Take her dancing. He looked at the brown shoes. I pointed. Nigga, you already equipped. He laughed again. This time it came easily.

Tank finished the last knot, stood, and inspected his work. Wedding shoes, he said. Thirty years late, Harlan answered. Thirty years still going. Harlan pressed his lips together and nodded. Then he picked up his fork and ate like a man with somewhere to be. Shawna sat with Lena. Rosa poured coffee.

The three women spoke low enough that I heard only pieces: songs, five months, a sister in Denver, a room with a window. None of it needed me in the middle. I ate my eggs. A nigga can save a room and still let other people have conversations inside it. Afterward Reiss took us back beneath the school.

The transfer chamber looked smaller with the lights fully on. Without Voss's guards and the snow blowing through it, the place showed its seams: cables enclosed in heavy channels, rails bolted to the floor, a control platform crowded with dials Reiss touched as if apologizing to them. The Heart stood in a secured housing behind reinforced glass.

Bell had connected it to the academy's restored support system. It remained the school's core; we were not putting it in my trunk and hoping the clouds would understand. Storm stood at the controls. Reiss showed her the three rings governing the door's alignment. She listened, asked two questions, and repeated the sequence back without touching anything.

Only you can hold the boundary stable without drawing too much from the school, he said. Once I set the coordinates, you open it. I can't guarantee a second attempt if we close it fully. Then we make one passage out and one passage back, Storm said. He nodded. A single operation. After that, the frame comes apart. I looked at the empty circle.

And we don't come back with everybody suddenly unborn or some shit? Reiss shook his head. This isn't a road to your own childhood. Voss connected us to a branch and changed it. The history we came from still happened. We can't erase the Holocaust by driving through this machine. The room stayed quiet. We can help people trapped on its other side, he said.

People who exist there now. That is the choice in front of us. Shawna looked at me. That's enough. Yeah, I said. It is. Tank ran his hand along the closed carriage's empty rail. How many? Reiss spread the depot papers across a workbench. Keller's site held eighty-three adult prisoners at the last count. Thirty-one were people Voss had sent from the present.

The rest belonged to that altered year: Jewish prisoners, resistance members, other adults seized by the Nazi occupation. Some were forced to maintain the depot. Some were used in the power experiments. Some were awaiting transport to places whose names did not need to be written down for the fear to make sense. We don't know if the count has changed, Reiss said.

Then we count when we get there, Shawna answered. He showed us the station layout. Not a plan for conquering history. One depot, one connecting yard, one machine chamber Keller had built around the other end of Voss's door. A holding building stood beyond the tracks. A repair shed faced the station.

A road led into the town of Saint-Orage, whose name Reiss said had not appeared in the records of the world he had grown up in. I studied the repair shed. People in there work on engines? Some do. Good. People who know a machine can usually tell you which nigga keeps breaking it. Pike wanted to send a full patrol squad.

Storm shook her head. Voss was still free somewhere in the surrounding sky, and the academy had thousands of people to protect. We could take a small team through. The rest had to hold the way home. I chose Tank, Shawna, Victor, and Petra. Rosa stood up immediately. No. Tank looked at her.

Baby— You not disappearing into another century while I wait upstairs wondering if some dead motherfucker got lucky. He opened his mouth. She put a finger against his chest. I know what I'm saying. Mercedes stood beside Petra. Same. Porsche looked around the room. Nigga, y'all not leaving me to explain all this to the luggage. I looked at Storm.

She looked at the ring, then the vehicles waiting beyond the chamber doors. The limit is power draw and transport space, she said. Not whether someone seems impressive on a roster. Good, Shawna said. Because the roster didn't open the cells. We settled it the way my people settled things: by deciding what each of us could do and trusting each other to do it.

Rosa would help move prisoners and keep people together. Mercedes had worked enough crowded rooms to read a frightened group before it turned into a crush. Porsche spoke French well enough to make Marcellus raise both eyebrows when she finally admitted it. My first serious boyfriend was from Marseille, she said. You never mentioned that, Tank told her.

Nigga, you never asked me a question that wasn't about where I parked. Vega would come as the doctor. Reiss would remain with Storm to hold the return point. Marcellus stayed at Sky High with Lena and the freed prisoners; he said somebody needed to keep track of people while all the beautiful idiots went charging through a clock. You calling me beautiful? Tank asked.

Marcellus looked at him over his glasses. I included you in a group. Don't get greedy. Bell prepared three vehicles. My dark blue police Lamborghini. Tank's black Mercedes. A broad academy rescue van with room for supplies and people who could not walk. They would run on ordinary ground once through the door.

Their sky-lane receivers would have nothing to hold onto in the other year. No roads made of light, Bell said. Tank looked almost moved. Nigga, finally some pavement with manners. We were not taking a convoy large enough to empty the whole depot in one trip. The papers showed trucks and rail carriages already there.

Getting the prisoners to the door would depend partly on what they knew and what we could secure when we arrived. I didn't love uncertainty. I loved pretending it scared me even less. While Bell worked, my phone rang again. Knox had seen the prison footage. This time she didn't begin with a joke. You have what you need?

I looked around the chamber at my people. Shawna speaking with Vega. Tank listening to Rosa. Victor beside Petra, testing one small spark between his fingers. Porsche arguing with Bell about whether the rescue van had a proper heater. Mostly. I asked about equipment, Captain. So did I. She was quiet for a second. Then she said, Bring them back. That's the plan. And Aiden?

Yeah? If anybody asks which department sent you, say mine. I smiled. Chief, you sure you want that paperwork? Nigga, I want the picture. At noon, Voss tried to take his machine back. The first sign was a low metallic boom above us. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Storm looked up sharply. The control lights flickered. Pike's voice burst over the radio.

Three armed skiffs approaching the lower service bridge. Tank closed the Mercedes door. This nigga don't know when the party over. I went toward the bridge with him and Victor. Shawna followed, because apparently I still needed reminding that marriage was a formation. The skiffs hovered outside the maintenance windows. Black hulls. Men crouched behind their rails. A loudspeaker crackled.

Surrender the core and release Commander Sable. I looked at the speaker. Nigga, he asking for refunds. Victor's hands lit. Vega's voice came over his radio immediately. Brief use. He stared at the radio. How does she always know? Because she a doctor and you a patient. That relationship follows a nigga. The first skiff fired into the bridge. Glass shattered over us.

We dropped behind the stone wall. Tank pulled a maintenance worker down beside him and held him there while the man tried to explain that he had only come to change a light. Nigga, the whole light changing. Stay down. I returned fire through the broken window. The skiff veered.

Victor sent a short bolt into its steering ring, and the craft spun away toward an empty service platform. The men aboard scrambled to hold on. The second skiff came lower, aiming for the transfer chamber's outer doors. Shawna saw it before I did. They're going underneath. We ran back down the passage. The chamber doors shook under the first impact.

Bell stood behind the Heart's reinforced housing with two tools in each hand and the expression of a man prepared to defend his profession physically. Move the van, I told him. Where? Away from the part I'm about to use. I got into the Lamborghini. Shawna got in beside me. The outer doors buckled inward.

Beyond them the second skiff hung level with the floor, its nose pressed against the damaged frame. Two gunmen prepared to jump across. I turned on the police lights. They paused. Nigga, even in the sky, a man with a gun could still have an unresolved feeling about a traffic stop. I drove straight at them. Not out into the air.

Into the heavy rolling barrier parked beside the entrance. The Lamborghini shoved it across the floor and into the skiff's nose. The craft rocked backward. Its gunmen fell against each other. Bell hit the emergency shutters, and a steel screen came down between us and the sky. The skiff crashed against the outside. The third fled before Storm's wind reached it.

When I got out, Bell stared at the Lamborghini's newest dent. I held up one finger. That was barely a trip. He covered his face with two hands and pointed at the van with the other two. Please just go save people in another year. We finished preparing under guard. That evening, Shawna and I went back to our room for twenty minutes. We washed.

Changed. Stood at the balcony looking out over a sky that had somehow become familiar in four days. She wore dark trousers, boots, and a blue sweater beneath her jacket. The medal lay on the dresser. Her ring remained on her hand. Dante's watch ticked against her wrist. My cross rested outside my shirt. She touched its engraved back. Damn, boy, she said softly. Magnificent.

She smiled. You really believe that. Baby, you engraved supporting evidence. She pulled me toward her. We kissed slowly, without an audience, without somebody announcing that the sky had developed another emergency. I held the back of her head and felt how much I wanted all the future waiting after this one difficult tomorrow. You scared? she asked. Yeah. She nodded. No disappointment. No surprise.

Just her hand tightening in mine. Me too. We stood there another minute. Then she picked up her jacket. Come on. That woman been waiting. When we returned to the chamber, everybody was ready. Lena had come down with Marcellus to see us off. She took Rosa's hands and sang one clear note. Rosa answered it. No secret code.

Just two singers promising to recognize each other when the door opened again. Storm stood at the controls. The Heart glowed behind its glass. One branch, she said. One outward passage. One return. I hold this side. Reiss holds the alignment. You bring everyone you can back through the same point. I nodded. She turned the first ring. Blue light crawled around the frame.

The second ring moved. Cold entered the room before the view did. Then the metal circle filled with a snow-covered rail yard beneath an evening sky. A low station. A repair shed. The dark shape of a train. Beyond them, a building with barred windows and a light burning in one upper room. The place where somebody had seen us leave. I started the Lamborghini.

Tank started the Mercedes behind me. The rescue van's engine joined ours. Shawna reached across and touched my arm. Ready? I looked at her. At my wife, who could have stayed in a room above the clouds and had instead chosen this impossible road beside me. Nigga, I said softly, I been ready for you my whole life.

She laughed once and wiped her eye with the heel of her hand. Then I put the car in gear. The snow beyond the door began turning blue beneath my police lights.

# 12. There Was a Road Under Her Song

Single narrator: the funeral ticket man. Verbatim chapter body.

Isla Gloria looked like somebody had built a crown and then put beaches around it so it wouldn't seem arrogant. The palace stood on the highest point, white stone against green hills. Below it, the opera house faced the harbor with six tall columns and a roof the color of old copper. Roads wound between gardens, hotels and little houses painted yellow, blue and pink.

At the far end of the island, beyond a black ridge, cranes stood over the shipyard like long-necked birds nobody had fed. Shawna leaned against the rail of our ferry. That one, she said, pointing at the opera house. Nigga, I know. I want the blue room upstairs. You ain't seen upstairs. I can tell. Mercedes nodded beside her. She can tell.

Porsche looked at me. Nigga, don't argue with architecture. It picked her. We had come under an official invitation to tour the island before the final stages. The duke wanted cameras showing us enjoying his hospitality. We wanted to see the road beneath the opera house. Everybody was getting something out of the morning. Odette stayed on the mainland with Bellario and the witnesses.

Bruno came with us. So did Esteban, Cassius, Inez and Lark. Rosa had a rehearsal with the island musicians. Mercedes and Porsche brought clothing and money for people who might need to leave quickly, carried in bags that looked exactly like what rich women brought to an island because they were exactly that too. Nigga, my people could pack a rescue inside a vacation.

A harbor band greeted us at the quay. They began the duke's anthem. Then their drummer recognized me, hit a different rhythm and shouted, Captain! The brass section followed him without permission. The anthem became something faster, louder and much more interested in my arrival. People came out of the cafes. Workers leaned from windows.

A man carrying a crate set it down and began clapping over his head. The duke's representative, a thin man called Lord Orlan, tried to speak through the music. Welcome to— Aiden! the crowd shouted. His Grace hopes— Aiden! Orlan gave up and handed me the flowers. Shawna took them. For the house, she said. A line of cars waited beyond the quay.

The duke had arranged sedans. I had arranged for my Lamborghini to come off the ferry behind us. Tank's new black coupe followed it, replacement already purchased because Rosa said she couldn't watch him mourn a wheel for the rest of the month. Bruno climbed into the back of a large open car and made its suspension bow respectfully.

Orlan led us through town toward the opera house. People watched from every doorway. Not all of them cheered. Some looked at us with a cautious attention that made the bright buildings seem thinner. They had heard about the rescued workers. They knew people whose names were on Luc's list.

They didn't know yet whether I was a visitor who would leave them with a worse duke or a man who meant what the harbor wall said. I waved anyway. Then I stopped the car. A woman stood outside a bakery holding a little photograph against her apron. She was about sixty, face lined from sun and worry. I got out and walked to her.

Who? I asked. My son. Julian. He's a mechanic. She held up the picture. I recognized him from Cinder Dock. One of the eleven. The man who had knelt and touched the ground. He's safe, I said. She stared at me. He's on the mainland. Odette's people got him a room. We can call him right now. Her hand went to her mouth.

Porsche was beside me already with her phone. Five minutes later the woman was hearing her son's voice for the first time in two months. The whole street had stopped. She laughed. Cried. Called him an idiot for not eating enough. Nigga, mothers can cross an ocean between sentences and still arrive with a complaint ready.

When she handed the phone back, she took my face in both hands and kissed my forehead. You came back for him. I looked at the crowd around us. We got more to do. The cheering began slowly this time. It hit harder. At the opera house, a conductor named Maestra Valentina Sorell met Shawna at the doors.

She was fifty-seven, straight-backed, silver hair pinned high and the expression of a woman who had heard every excuse a singer could invent and rejected most of them before breakfast. I heard your performance at the banquet, she said. Shawna smiled. Thank you. I didn't say whether I liked it. No. But you opened the door. Valentina held her gaze. Then she smiled too.

Nigga, my wife had just won an argument in music without singing. The hall was magnificent. Red seats, gold balconies, a ceiling painted with clouds. The stage stood beneath a great blue curtain embroidered with silver stars. Shawna walked down the aisle slowly, touching the backs of seats as she passed. I watched her face.

Some things a man buys because he wants other people to see the price. Some things he wants because he has seen the person he loves imagine a life inside them. I wanted this whole fucking room. For her. Rosa joined the musicians near the pit. Tank followed, carrying her bag like it contained his religion.

Mercedes and Porsche went upstairs with a wardrobe assistant who had recognized them from Blue Hour. Lark asked to see the building's old machinery, and Cassius went with her. Esteban remained beside Inez, staring at a portrait in the side corridor. Amalia. She had sung here once, he told me. Not professionally. At a festival after a race.

The portrait showed her laughing with a flower in her hair, the same open happiness that had survived in the photograph on his table. The duke hadn't covered this one. Maybe he thought a dead woman's smile couldn't accuse him. Nigga was wrong about a lot of things. Valentina asked Shawna to sing one passage onstage. Shawna looked at me.

I sat in the front row. I'm here. She began. The empty hall filled around her voice. No microphone. No chandelier party. Just air, stone, wood and a woman standing where she wanted to stand. Valentina's expression changed halfway through the first phrase. By the end she had folded her hands against her chest. Shawna stopped. The echo stayed. Valentina looked at me.

You brought us a queen. Nigga, I've been saying. She blinked at the word, then laughed because my face had apparently made the meaning clear enough. I should like her to stay. That's her call. Shawna came down from the stage. I'll sing the closing concert, she said. With Rosa. And the full company. Valentina nodded. The duke has reduced the company.

Then unreduced it, I said. She looked at me. Nigga, bring everybody back. For the first time, the old conductor's eyes shone. We left them discussing music while I went looking for Lark. I found her beneath the stage, standing in front of an iron door with the third jeweled key in her hand. Shawna had given it to her before rehearsal.

Cassius stood beside her, holding a lamp. On the door was the same little white bird. The old service road, Lark said. Runs beneath the hill to the shipyard. How'd you find it? The stage carpenter's brother is missing. He stopped pretending he didn't know things when I said Mateo's name. Cassius held the lamp closer.

"Nigga, you sure this is a key for this door?" Lark looked at him. "Same bird. Same old maker's mark. Same shape." "That nigga's asking whether the door agrees," I said. "The door can disagree unlocked," Shawna said. Tank arrived behind us. "Niggas, did y'all find the downstairs?" "Whole downstairs," I said. "Under the singing?" "Nigga, under the singing." He looked back toward the stage.

"Rosa better not find out I missed her rehearsal to stand under it." The key turned. The door opened onto a sloping passage wide enough for a car. Electric lights ran along one wall. Far below, we could hear engines. Cassius looked at me. My father. Maybe. This time he accepted the word.

We went down together, with Tank and Bruno joining us at the first turn. Shawna came after them carrying her jacket and the expression that meant she had heard half an explanation and was coming for the other half in person. Nigga, don't leave me upstairs singing to a trapdoor. I was about to call. Then your timing's improving.

The passage ended at a broad underground garage. Old racing cars stood under covers. Crates filled one wall. At the far end, a group of workers loaded equipment into a truck under the watch of three guards. Lark stopped so suddenly Cassius nearly walked into her. Mateo. The man near the truck looked up. Thirty-four. Dark hair. Lean face. One hand bandaged.

He stared at her like the whole underground room had become a dream he was afraid to wake from. Lark? She ran. A guard stepped into her path. Bruno moved first. Nigga, I had seen strong men hurry. I had never seen a mountain decide it had an appointment.

He crossed the space in three strides, caught the guard around the middle and sat him on the hood of a covered car with enough force to end the conversation. Tank took the second guard. I took the third. It was not a long fight. Sometimes a man who has spent months frightening hungry workers discovers he has not actually been practicing for us.

Mateo dropped the crate in his hands. Lark hit him with both arms around his neck. He held her so tightly I looked away for a second. Not because it was private in the way a bedroom is private. Because there was something in that first breath together I didn't want to turn into a thing I owned by watching too closely.

Shawna took my hand. We let them have it. Then Mateo lifted his head. You came. Lark was crying openly now. Nigga, I told you to buy a plant and come home. You had one job after the plant. He laughed into her hair. I bought it. Then you better be alive to water it. I am.

She kissed him like she needed the answer in a language his mouth couldn't get wrong. Behind them, the other workers began moving toward us. There were nine in the garage. We took all nine. Mateo told us Nero and Celadon had been moved that morning to the Sovereign Sun, now anchored beyond the shipyard. The duke's captain, Morcant, had taken them personally.

He was preparing to leave the island before the final race. Cassius swore and turned toward the passage leading deeper under the hill. I caught his shoulder carefully. We got people to bring out. My father— We know where he is. We can go after him with everybody ready. His eyes burned. I held them.

Nigga, don't make him wait seven years for you to die running past help. He looked at the workers. At Lark and Mateo. At Inez, who had reached us now and stood beside the passage without trying to hide how frightened she was for him. He nodded once. All right. Then an engine started beyond the garage.

A black armored car came through the far entrance, followed by two motorcycles. Its windows were dark. Its front grille looked like it had been designed specifically to discourage touching. A voice came through a speaker. Step away from the workers. I looked at Tank. Tank looked at Bruno. Bruno looked at the covered cars.

Nigga, the three of us had the same beautiful problem at once. No Lamborghini. Plenty of cars. I pulled the nearest cover off. Under it sat a white racing machine with a long nose and a painted bird on the hood. The White Heron. Cassius made a sound beside me. Esteban, arriving at the garage entrance, stopped dead. My God. I opened the driver's door.

Nigga, your god got keys? Mateo reached into his pocket and threw them to me. I kept it running, he said. Of course he had. I got in. Shawna took the seat beside me. Behind us, Tank and Bruno moved the workers toward the service passage while Cassius and Inez covered the retreat. The armored car came forward. I started the White Heron.

Seven years of waiting woke beneath the hood. Esteban put one hand on the roof as I rolled past. Bring her back, he said. I looked up at him. Nigga, I'm bringing everybody back. Then I drove straight at the duke's black car.

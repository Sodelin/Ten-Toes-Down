Esteban said the wrong man's name, and Cassius stood up so fast his chair fell over.

“Nero Chrome,” Esteban said.

“That was my father.”

“I know whose father he was.”

“Nigga,” I said, “everybody leave the potatoes out of this.”

Cassius wasn't looking at me. He had one hand flat on the table and the other held against his injured shoulder. Esteban sat very straight, his wife's picture beneath his palm. Inez stood between them before either man moved again.

“Papa, you don't know everything.”

“I know who was beside the car.”

“You know what Bellario told you.”

Cassius laughed once. No amusement in it.

“Of course. Bellario.”

He turned toward the courtyard gate.

I caught him there.

“Where you going?”

“Somewhere my father isn't being tried by a man who let the duke buy his mouth.”

Esteban's chair scraped behind us.

Tank put one hand on its back.

“Sit down, my nigga.”

“I will not—”

“Then stand still. Pick one.”

I kept my eyes on Cassius.

“You can hear him and still hate what he says.”

“I've heard it for seven years.”

“Then hear it once with me here.”

His jaw moved. He looked at Shawna, who had come to stand beside the table, holding Amalia's photograph carefully by the edges.

“Your father drove this car?” she asked.

Cassius looked back despite himself.

“The White Heron,” he said. “He built it with Esteban.”

“Then sit down and tell us something nobody else gets right.”

That reached him.

He picked his chair up. He didn't apologize, and Esteban didn't ask him to. Sometimes, nigga, the best you can get from two men is both their asses back at the table.

Esteban began again.

Seven years earlier, the Grand Prix had been smaller. There were rich men in it, but money wasn't the only invitation. Mechanics entered cars they had built behind their houses. Boat captains raced old engines down the coast. The drivers ate at the same tables as the people who changed their tires, and at the end of the month the winner sailed to Isla Gloria with everybody who had helped him get there.

“Amalia loved the last night,” Esteban said. “She would dance until the musicians begged her to stop.”

Rosa smiled faintly. “I know that kind of woman.”

“She collected people. If a waiter looked lonely, suddenly he was sitting with us. If a driver lost, she found something he had done well and made him talk about that. When Aurelio bought the race, she told him the prize was already here. He only needed to leave it alone.”

“What'd he say?” I asked.

“That a woman who enjoyed giving things away should marry a poorer man.”

“Nigga.”

Esteban nodded.

“He said it smiling.”

That year Esteban and Nero had entered the White Heron together. One drove the mountain stages, the other the coastal ones. Amalia kept their books, knew every worker by name and had discovered that the prize fleet included vessels the duke had never actually paid for. Their owners had been promised shares in the Grand Prix. Instead, crews stopped receiving wages, captains disappeared from the entry list and the harbor gates began closing at night.

“She went to the island,” Esteban said. “A captain named Celadon took her. She came back with a packet of papers and told me we had to finish the race. Win it in front of everybody. Then we could make him give back what he had taken.”

“Why didn't she show you the papers?” Shawna asked.

“She did. I looked at the first page, kissed her forehead and said I trusted her.”

His voice roughened.

“I thought that was the same as listening.”

I felt Shawna's thumb move against my hand.

Esteban told us about the final mountain stage. The White Heron led by eleven seconds. Nero had driven the morning. Esteban was due to take over at the ridge. Amalia traveled ahead in a little blue support car carrying the papers and the team's food, because even when a man was about to expose a duke she thought he ought to have lunch.

At the ridge, Nero never arrived.

Instead, Bellario brought news that the support car had gone over the road. Amalia was dead. Nero had vanished. A green harbor car had been seen leaving the scene.

“The duke showed me a signed withdrawal,” Esteban said. “Nero's name. Money paid. A place on a ship leaving that night. Bellario said he watched Nero get into the green car.”

Cassius leaned forward.

“My father couldn't write with his right hand that week. He'd broken two fingers.”

Esteban blinked.

“The signature was steady as a schoolmaster's,” Cassius said. “I told them. I was twenty-four, and I told every man in that room. They said grief was making me irrational.”

“Why didn't you tell me?”

“You wouldn't open your gate.”

The words landed harder than shouting would have.

Esteban stared at him. Inez looked away, and I understood this was a conversation they had been walking around for seven years, keeping it alive by never allowing it to finish.

Cassius took something out of his jacket. A little metal bird, white paint worn off one wing.

“My father gave me this the morning of the race. Said if he won, I'd drive the first lap next year.”

He set it beside Amalia's photograph.

“He never came home.”

Outside the courtyard a car horn sounded.

Nobody moved.

It sounded again, and Tank stood up with the expression of a man grateful for a fresh target. He opened the gate. Bellario stood beyond it beside a cream-colored limousine, dressed for dinner, feather restored.

“His Grace requests Captain Aiden's presence at the opening banquet.”

I looked at the nigga for a long time.

“Does he?”

“Attendance is traditional.”

“So is knocking.”

Bellario glanced past Tank and saw the table. Saw Cassius, Esteban, Inez. His face didn't change, but his eyes counted us twice.

“An intimate gathering,” he said.

“Getting less intimate.”

Shawna squeezed my hand and stood.

“We'll be there.”

Bellario bowed to her with the precise depth of a man who practiced alone.

“His Grace particularly hopes you will sing.”

“Then he should particularly hope he's paid my musicians.”

The bow ended early.

After he left, Esteban went inside and returned with a small lacquer box. He opened it to reveal a folded letter, a yellow ribbon and a photograph of Amalia laughing beside a white boat.

“This came three days after she died,” he said. “A woman delivered it. She wouldn't give her name. I read the first line so many times I almost wore through it.”

“What's it say?” I asked.

He unfolded the top edge.

“Esteban, if you are reading this without me, do not let a man who never loved either of us explain my last day.”

Cassius closed his eyes.

Esteban held the letter toward Inez.

“I wasn't brave enough to obey her.”

She took it without a word.

We left the table standing. Plates, bread, photograph, the little white bird. Nobody tried to make it look like a dinner that had ended normally.

At the hotel, Shawna changed into a blue dress that moved around her legs like water had learned her name. She wore Dante's watch on her wrist and earrings I had given her after Sky High. She stood before the mirror fastening one, and I came up behind her.

“You want to skip this?” I asked.

“No.”

“We can eat somewhere nobody named Cruel owns the forks.”

She met my eyes in the mirror.

“I want to sing. I want Rosa to get paid. I want that nigga to look at us across his own table while we find out what he's hiding.”

“That's my wife.”

She turned around.

“And you want to race him.”

“I want to beat him.”

“Then do it with your eyes open.”

I kissed her forehead.

She pushed my chest lightly.

“Don't Esteban me.”

Nigga, I laughed so hard she smiled before she could help it. Then I took her face in both hands.

“I hear you. You want to sing, Rosa gets paid, we watch the duke. I don't go running off because somebody waves a faster car.”

“And?”

“And if it's a really fast car, I bring you.”

She hit me with the earring box.

The banquet occupied a terrace above the sea. Three hundred people sat beneath chandeliers hanging from living trees. At the entrance, a young doorman looked at my invitation, then at my badge, then back at the invitation.

"It says king."

"It also says captain," I told him.

"Which should I announce?"

Tank leaned over his shoulder.

"Nigga, use your diaphragm and say both."

"Is there a preferred order?"

"The nigga arrived first at everything today. Any order works."

The doorman laughed, took a breath and announced me so loudly the nearest chandelier trembled.

I slipped a folded bill into his palm.

"That's for respecting punctuation."

He looked at the amount, looked at me and announced my wife with even more feeling. Nigga had found a second diaphragm. A string orchestra played on a platform over the swimming pool. Along the terrace stood twelve cars under silk covers, each with its own guard and a woman holding a silver card naming the race it belonged to.

I walked in with Shawna on my arm, my badge against my chest and Tank behind me in a white suit that made him look like he had personally purchased summer.

Conversations stopped in waves.

The first wave was people recognizing me. The second was people asking the first wave who I was. The third was everybody looking at Shawna and deciding the answer must be more impressive than they had heard.

The duke rose from a long table at the center.

Aurelio Cruel was forty-eight, tall, broad through the shoulders, handsome in the way a sword can be handsome before somebody pulls it out. His hair was black with two white streaks. He wore a green diamond on one hand and a watch whose face seemed larger than the gold wheel I'd won.

“Captain,” he said. “Your entrance this afternoon was costly.”

“For who?”

The people close enough to hear laughed before they looked at him. He let them finish.

“For the harbor's dignity.”

“Nigga, the harbor selling shirts.”

This time the laughter reached the next table.

He smiled. “Come. Sit beside me.”

There were two chairs waiting. One for me, one for Shawna. Farther down the terrace, Mercedes and Porsche had been directed toward a little table behind a pillar.

I stopped.

“Where's my people?”

“We've arranged seating by distinction.”

“Then you arranged it wrong.”

The duke rested his fingertips on the back of my chair.

“A captain. A celebrated singer. Naturally, some guests have qualifications others do not.”

Mercedes had heard him. She walked over before I could answer, yellow silk catching the light.

“Mine got you to call my agency three times last year,” she said. “Porsche's got your minister to pay double for dinner and still ask if she had a sister.”

Porsche appeared beside her.

“I told him no. He looked financially devastated.”

A man at the duke's table suddenly became fascinated by his soup.

The duke's smile thinned.

“There is no need for vulgarity.”

“Nigga, you invited me,” I said.

I took the chair beside him, lifted it and carried it to Mercedes's table. Tank picked up the table itself. Rosa brought the flowers. Within twenty seconds we had joined two tables together directly in front of the orchestra, and half the terrace was standing to help because nobody wanted to be the last person on the wrong side of the evening.

Cassius arrived with Inez. He saw what we were doing and dragged his own chair over one-handed.

“Careful,” I told him.

“It's my good arm.”

“Keep one.”

The duke stood alone beside his empty place settings.

Then he laughed.

It was a good laugh. Full, generous, almost convincing.

“A king makes his own table,” he said.

I sat down and pulled Shawna into the chair beside me.

“Now you catching on.”

The waiter approached with a bottle and looked at the duke, then at me.

"Who should taste?"

"That nigga owns the bottle," Tank said. "This nigga owns the table."

"I own neither," I said. "My wife wanted wine. Give the woman wine."

Shawna held out her glass. "These niggas will appoint a president of the bottle if you let them."

Mercedes nodded. "Nigga will run unopposed."

"And win," I said.

The waiter laughed so suddenly he had to steady the bottle with both hands. The duke didn't laugh at all. Nigga was losing authority to a drink he had paid for.

Dinner came. Lobster, lamb, fish served whole on silver platters. Tank looked at the fish and said, “This better not be the nigga you saved.” Rosa nearly dropped her wine.

The duke joined us after the second course. He had accepted that if he wanted the most important table at his party, he would have to sit at mine.

He told stories about princes losing fortunes. He described a driver who had bet his castle and left in a taxi. He named the prize fleet: forty road cars, twelve racing cars, six yachts and a flagship called the Sovereign Sun. At the end of the month, the winner would sail that flagship into Isla Gloria's harbor and receive the island before every camera in the world.

“And if you lose?” I asked.

“I do not compete.”

“Convenient.”

“I own the competition.”

“For now.”

He watched me over his glass.

Shawna's hand settled on my knee.

The orchestra quieted. Rosa rose first, went to the little stage and sang a song so rich and warm the sea itself seemed to lean closer. Tank sat still through the whole thing. Nigga, the man could lift a table with dinner on it, but that woman's voice pinned him to a chair.

When she finished, he stood up before anybody else.

“That's Rosa!” he shouted.

Everybody knew. He told them anyway.

Then Shawna joined her.

My wife began without an introduction. One clear note, then another, rising over the water. I had heard her sing in rooms where I was the only person listening. I'd heard her angry, laughing, half asleep, making up words because she couldn't remember the real ones. This was different. She stood in the light with her shoulders bare and let the whole terrace hear how large she was inside.

The duke stopped drinking.

A woman at the next table began to cry.

I sat forward, both hands flat on my knees, because if I leaned back I felt like my chest might open.

Rosa answered her. Their voices wound together. The orchestra followed, and for once in my life, nigga, I didn't need to be doing the most impressive thing in the room. My wife was doing it, and she kept looking at me like the song had an address.

When they finished, the terrace stood.

Shawna came back through hands reaching for hers. A conductor kissed her fingers. One old man said something in Italian so emotional his wife nodded like he had finally gotten a point right after forty years.

The duke rose to greet her.

“You must perform on Isla Gloria,” he said. “The opera house was built for a voice like yours.”

Shawna smiled.

“Then make sure the people who clean it get paid.”

His hand stopped halfway toward hers.

At the edge of the terrace, a waiter dropped a glass.

Bellario was beside him instantly. Too quickly. He caught the man's arm and began steering him toward a service door. The waiter looked back once, directly at our table.

Lark stood up.

“That man knows Mateo.”

I rose with her.

The duke stayed between us and the door.

“Captain, dessert—”

“Nigga, move.”

His eyes hardened.

Then he did.

By the time we reached the corridor, Bellario was gone. The waiter stood alone beside a linen cart, shaking, blood on one finger where the glass had cut him.

Mercedes took a clean napkin and wrapped his hand.

“What's your name?” she asked.

“Luc Marel.”

“How old are you?”

“Twenty-nine.”

“Okay, Luc. Breathe.”

He looked past us toward the terrace.

“They said if I spoke to the drivers—”

Tank shut the corridor door.

“Now you're speaking to us.”

Luc swallowed. Then he pulled a folded service menu from his pocket. On the back, in hurried pencil, was a list of names. Thirty-two of them.

Mateo was halfway down.

Beside the list were two words.

CINDER DOCK.

Below that, a time.

MIDNIGHT FRIDAY.

Lark covered her mouth with one hand.

Luc looked at me.

“Are you really a policeman?”

I took the gold badge off my chain and put it in his good hand.

“Nigga,” I said, “tonight I am whatever you need me to be.”

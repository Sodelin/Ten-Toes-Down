Chief Gloria Knox arrived on Isla Gloria wearing a blue suit, sunglasses and the expression of a woman whose captain had made her vacation an international event.

She came through the opera house doors while Tank and Bruno were arguing about which one of them occupied more of the entrance.

“Move,” she said.

They moved.

Bruno looked down at Knox. "Ma'am, are you his commander?"

"When he remembers."

"Nigga remembers," Tank said. "Nigga just remembers at speed."

Knox looked toward me. "Captain, did you send my car over an ocean?"

"Which ocean?"

"That answer concerns me."

"Nigga's being precise," Tank said.

"Nigga's being evasive," Shawna corrected.

Knox put her sunglasses in her pocket. "At least everybody here knows him."

"We know that nigga," Rosa said warmly. "We packed accordingly."

“Nigga,” Tank whispered to Bruno, “that's where he got it.”

Knox took off her sunglasses and looked down the aisle at me.

“Captain.”

“Chief.”

“Nice theater.”

“My wife likes it.”

“So I've heard. The entire department has heard. One officer has started taking singing lessons in case you promote the orchestra.”

Shawna laughed from the stage.

Knox looked up at her.

“Mrs. Aiden.”

“Chief.”

“Keep him breathing.”

“Working full time.”

Nigga, women meet and immediately form a government around your stupid decisions.

Knox had come with six officers who were technically on leave and practically dressed like they expected trouble to recognize them. Odette met her near the front doors. The two women shook hands, exchanged a few words and began making the building safer without asking either of us how strongly we felt about furniture placement.

I appreciated expertise.

Especially when it arrived with my department's name on the jackets.

The night before the final race, the opera house held more life than it had seen in years. Singers rehearsed onstage. Workers ate in the side rooms. Drivers talked with mechanics in the courtyard. Somebody had found a television, and Clementine's victory played on it every twenty minutes, provoking the same delighted shouting each time the eggs appeared.

The marshal who owned Clementine had brought his wife to the island. Her name was Elise. She was fifty, cheerful, and had informed her husband that they were never selling the car because it now had a better story than either of them.

I gave them a new luxury sedan anyway.

She looked at the keys.

“Nigga,” she said, then covered her mouth. “I'm sorry. My husband keeps playing the video.”

I laughed and hugged her.

“Your car earned the accent.”

By nine, the square outside the opera house glowed with lanterns. Julian's mother sent bread. Rocco sent wine. Mercedes and Porsche arranged tables so the workers and performers could eat together. No separate room behind a pillar. No invitation stamped important enough to excuse a man being rude.

Tank sat with Rosa on the steps.

I passed them carrying a plate.

He looked up at me.

“Nigga, can you stop saving countries during dinner?”

“Only an island.”

“That's how you start.”

Rosa leaned against his shoulder.

“He saved my rehearsal.”

“He drove a car through it.”

“And brought the singers back.”

Tank considered that.

“Nigga's thorough.”

Across the square, Cassius and Inez stood beside the White Heron. She had one foot on the low curb, red dress moving in the evening breeze. He leaned against the car, listening while she spoke. Not planning his answer. Listening.

Nero watched them from a table with Esteban.

“I would have been terrible at this age,” Nero said.

“You were terrible at this age,” Esteban replied.

“Yes. But beautifully.”

They clinked glasses.

I went upstairs to find Shawna.

She stood in the blue room she'd chosen from the ferry, looking out over the harbor. It was exactly what she had imagined: pale walls, blue curtains, a little balcony facing the sea. Her music lay open on a desk. Dante's watch sat beside it while she adjusted the strap.

“You were right,” I said.

She turned.

“About the room?”

“About everything. But start there.”

She smiled and came to me.

Down below, the square hummed. The island had a night before it had a king, and we stood above it holding each other like the future was close enough to touch but not yet safe enough to trust completely.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

“Yeah.”

“You win. We sing. Everybody gets to sleep.”

“Nigga, that's a beautiful schedule.”

“Follow it.”

I kissed her.

Then the first explosion shook the harbor.

The windows rattled. A blue flare rose above the shipyard ridge. Down in the square, voices changed pitch. Music stopped.

Shawna closed her eyes for one second.

“Nigga.”

“I know.”

We ran downstairs together.

Black vehicles came down the royal road toward the opera square. Six of them. Armed men stood in the rear of two open trucks. Behind them, motorcycles spread through the narrow streets, engines rising over the sound of people moving toward the doors.

The duke had sent the rest of his evening.

Knox met me at the bottom of the stairs.

“People inside. Hold the square.”

Odette was already moving her officers toward the side streets. Tank and Bruno stood at the main doors, directing the crowd inward. Mercedes took the women nearest the tables toward the rear corridor. Porsche helped Elise and her husband through the courtyard gate. Rosa stayed with the singers, keeping them together while Valentina led the oldest musicians down the side steps.

Shawna looked at me.

“I'll get them through.”

“I know.”

She kissed me once, quick and fierce.

Then she went to work.

I walked down the opera steps.

Nigga, the square looked different when everybody you loved was behind you.

The first black car stopped near the fountain. A man climbed out wearing a gray coat and carrying a long gun. He was older, thick through the neck, his face calm with the certainty of somebody who had survived by making other men afraid first.

“Captain Aiden,” he called.

“Nigga, you know my name. Save us time.”

“Walk away from the building.”

I looked back at the opera house.

Then at him.

“That's my wife's rehearsal.”

He lifted the gun.

The world became noise.

Stone chipped beside me. I went behind the fountain as Knox's officers answered from the steps. The gray-coated man ducked behind his car. Two motorcycles cut across the square, riders trying to reach the side entrance.

Tank met the first one with a table.

He lifted it, turned and put it directly into the motorcycle's future.

The rider hit the wood, flew sideways and landed in the flower bed.

“Nigga,” Tank shouted, “closed section!”

Bruno caught the second rider when he tried to get past the doorway. The bike skidded away. Bruno put the man down, took his weapon and kicked it out of reach with the irritated efficiency of a champion clearing a gym floor.

I moved between the fountain and the parked cars.

A gunman came around the hood of a silver sedan. I struck his arm, drove him backward and fired when his partner raised a weapon from the opposite side. The second man fell. The first stumbled away with his hands up, his courage suddenly interested in retirement.

“Nigga, stay there.”

He stayed.

Across the square, Cassius and Inez had brought the White Heron out of the courtyard. Not into the gunfire. Between the side street and the people still crossing to shelter. Its white body blocked the opening long enough for Nero and Esteban to guide the last group inside.

A truck accelerated toward the gap.

Inez saw it.

She got behind the wheel, turned the White Heron sharply and moved the car out of the way just as the truck struck the stone bollards hidden beside the curb.

Its front rose.

Stopped.

The driver hit the windshield and slumped, alive but no longer participating.

Cassius looked at Inez through the open window.

“Nigga, marry—”

He stopped himself.

She stared.

Then she grinned.

“Finish the fight first.”

I heard it from ten feet away and almost laughed in the middle of a gun battle.

Love, nigga. Terrible timing. Excellent taste.

The gray-coated man had reached the steps.

Knox confronted him halfway up. He fired, forcing her behind a pillar, then turned toward the main doors where Shawna was helping Valentina move a wounded musician.

I crossed the square at a run.

Blue thunder flashed through my body.

One burst.

The distance seemed to tighten. My hands moved faster than the thought finishing in my head. I hit the gray-coated man before he reached the door, drove him off the steps and landed with him on the stone below.

His gun went one way.

My breath went another.

We rolled. He struck me in the jaw. I answered. He reached for a knife. I caught his wrist and held it against the pavement while the thunder burned out of me, leaving every muscle suddenly aware of its own weight.

“Nigga,” I told him through my teeth, “you picked the wrong building.”

He tried to headbutt me.

I put him down.

When I stood, the square tilted.

Knox caught my arm.

“Captain.”

“I'm here.”

“I can see that. Stay useful by sitting.”

She pushed me onto the lower step, took the man's weapon and went back into the fight.

Nigga, my chief had promoted bossing me around into an international discipline.

I sat for thirty seconds. Long enough to get my breath. Long enough to see the fight turn.

Odette's officers came through the side street behind the trucks. The attackers' line broke. Some ran. Some dropped their weapons. One tried to drive away and found Bruno standing beside his open door, holding it in place with one hand.

“Nigga,” Bruno said, “engine off.”

The engine went off.

The square fell quiet in pieces.

Sirens. Groans. People calling names. The little crackle of a damaged car cooling beneath the fountain's spray.

We had wounded. Cuts, bruises, one officer hit in the shoulder, a violinist with glass in his arm. Two attackers were dead. The others who hadn't fled lay under guard or sat with their hands visible, discovering how ordinary a bad decision looked after the shooting stopped.

Shawna came down the steps and knelt in front of me.

“How much thunder?”

“One burst.”

She checked my eyes.

Then she pressed her forehead against mine.

“Okay.”

Behind her, Rosa began singing.

Softly at first, from somewhere inside the doors. A note to find the people who were shaking. Another to tell them the room was still there. The singers joined her one by one, no orchestra, just voices moving through the broken quiet.

Shawna looked back.

Then she stood, helped me up and sang with them.

The sound filled the square.

People came to the windows. To the doors. The workers who had hidden under the stage came upstairs and stood shoulder to shoulder in the aisle. Celadon rang the Sovereign Sun's bell from the harbor, and its deep answer crossed the water into the music.

I stood between my wife and my brother, bruised, exhausted, wearing a badge over a gold cross and six keys waiting inside the building behind me.

The duke had sent guns to end her song.

Nigga, he had made the audience bigger.

By morning, the city had put my face on a bus.

Not an advertisement. The whole front of the bus was my face. The driver looked out through my sunglasses, and when it turned a corner my left cheek opened to let people off.

Tank watched it pass beneath our balcony.

“Nigga, they made your head public transportation.”

“About time the people rode in something great.”

Rosa was eating breakfast in his lap despite there being twelve empty chairs. She leaned forward to see the bus, then looked back at me.

“You charging for that?”

Porsche answered from the next chair.

“Now we are.”

It was the first official race day. The Grand Prix of Kings lasted a month, with seven major races, exhibitions, sea events and enough parties to make a man forget why he had brought his passport. The ceremonial qualifier had put me first on the grid. Today's Crown Circuit would take us through the city three times, then climb to the glass palace above the harbor.

Winner received the first of seven jeweled keys. All seven together opened the prize ceremony. The points decided who held them at the end.

I liked a competition that gave a nigga jewelry for doing his job.

Lark had spent the night in the garage with my car. When I went down, she stood beside it holding a mug that read WORLD'S WORST PASSENGER.

“Your wife bought me this,” she said.

“She knows talent.”

Shawna came down behind me carrying a little bag of breakfast. She put it on Lark's workbench.

“Eat.”

“I will.”

“Now.”

Lark took a piece of bread out without arguing. I respected how quickly Shawna had become the chief of all this.

Cassius waited near the far wall in a fresh racing jacket, arm still in the sling. His replacement car sat under a black cover.

“You racing?” I asked.

“No. Doctor said another impact could tear the shoulder worse.”

“Nigga, listen to the doctor.”

“I am. I hate it.”

He walked with me around the Lamborghini.

“Watch the third descent. There's a blind rise before the old customs house. Yesterday's practice showed a dip after it.”

“Anything else?”

He looked toward Lark. She nodded.

“The green harbor cars have disappeared. Every one of them. The service company says they changed the fleet overnight.”

“Fast workers.”

“Frightened workers.”

Luc had slept in one of our extra rooms, with Tank's jacket over a chair outside the door as a warning anybody with survival instincts could understand. He had told us the staff were being moved to Cinder Dock on Friday, four nights away. From there, boats took them to the island's old shipyard. Nobody who went there came back to work the mainland hotels.

He didn't know what happened to them. That was bad enough without inventing worse.

Chief Knox called while I was putting on my gloves.

“Captain,” she said, “why is there footage of your patrol vehicle on a boat?”

“International waters.”

“It was on two boats.”

“International boats.”

There was a pause.

“Did you save that driver?”

“Yes, ma'am.”

“Good. The department has received eleven marriage proposals, three requests for transfer and a yacht club membership.”

“Keep the membership.”

“Already did.”

I smiled.

Then I told her about Luc, the missing staff and the duke's fleet. She went quiet in a different way.

“Keep your people close,” she said. “Your badge gets attention. Attention can open a door or tell the wrong man which door you're standing behind.”

“I know.”

“And Aiden?”

“Yes, ma'am?”

“Win the race.”

Nigga, that was leadership.

The grid sat beneath an arch covered in blue flowers. The street beyond it climbed between grand hotels, then fell toward the port. Thousands of people pressed behind the barriers. More watched from balconies, windows, rooftops. A choir had been hired to sing the race anthem, but half of them started chanting my name when I got out of the car.

The conductor tried to recover the key.

Lost it completely.

Shawna kissed me beside the driver's door. She wore a blue team jacket with AIDEN on the back in gold. Under my name she had added HUSBAND, because apparently the women of Monaco required a second line of clarification.

Inez walked over in her red racing suit.

“You running the police car all month?” she asked.

“Why?”

“Because I want to know whether being arrested is going to count as losing.”

“Depends how fast you confess.”

She grinned. Then her expression turned serious.

“My father read the rest of Amalia's letter last night. He wants to show you after the race.”

“What'd he find?”

“A reason to hate himself.”

“Tell him wait till we know where to put it.”

She looked toward the duke's balcony. Aurelio stood beside Bellario under an awning, both dressed in white, like innocence was a team color.

“I told my father I'm winning today,” Inez said.

“Nigga, you can tell him you drove beautifully.”

She punched my shoulder lightly.

“Try to keep your car out of the architecture.”

The anthem finally ended. Drivers climbed in. The crowd grew tight and loud around us.

A marshal leaned through the window to ask whether my police lights were part of the car's original equipment.

"Nigga, they're part of the car's personality."

"They may distract other drivers."

"My face does that. You want me to race backward?"

Shawna bent at the waist laughing. The marshal tried to stay serious and failed around the mouth.

"Please use them only when necessary."

"Nigga, I am a necessary situation."

Tank pointed at the marshal from behind the barrier.

"That nigga about to learn."

"All of them," Porsche said.

I put both hands on the wheel.

For a moment I thought of Vegas. Big T's lot. My first night. The heat of the gun, the hurt in my side, Shawna looking at me like she couldn't decide whether I was rescue or a different kind of disaster. Now she stood behind a race barrier across an ocean wearing my name, and a city was waiting for me to do something impossible before lunch.

Nigga, life had finally started acting like it knew who I was.

The lights went out.

Inez launched beside me. A yellow car took the outside, its driver a narrow man called Prince Bastian who had introduced himself three times at dinner and seemed offended that no one had introduced him a fourth. He swept ahead into the first turn.

I let him enjoy it until the second.

Then I passed him so close he looked over like I had entered his living room without knocking.

The city became motion. White stone, blue water, red banners. We passed the opera steps with Inez half a car behind. She came alongside on the descent, and for two turns we ran together, close enough that I could see her eyes through the visor.

She was smiling.

I smiled back.

The nigga in the yellow car tried to fit between us.

There was no between us.

He backed off at the last second and nearly lost his rear wheels to the curb. Inez shook her head inside her helmet. I laughed once, sharp and happy, and we dropped into the tunnel beneath the old city.

Inside, the engines multiplied. Mine roared off the walls. Hers answered. Lights ran across the hood in long white bars. At the exit she took the lead by a nose.

I could have called the blue thunder.

I didn't.

That mattered to me. I liked knowing the difference between what I could do and what the power could add. The thunder was a burst, a loan against the strength in my body. I wasn't spending it because a beautiful woman had better timing through one tunnel.

I caught her at the harbor bend.

The first lap ended with us side by side.

Above the crowd I heard Tank's voice. I couldn't make out the words, but the volume suggested he had physically challenged the entire spectator area to doubt me.

On the second lap, things got ugly.

A white car came out of the service lane ahead of us. It belonged to the race, had a marshal's stripe on the side and should never have been moving across the active road. It stopped sideways at the entrance to the third descent.

Inez swore over the open race channel.

“Obstacle!”

She turned sharply, barely missing it. Her red car hit a loose section of barrier and spun toward a staircase descending into the market below.

I pulled left.

The yellow car behind me hit the stopped marshal's car. Metal folded. Smoke burst across the road. Somebody's horn started and didn't stop.

Inez's car slid backward down the first three steps.

People in the market scattered.

“Nigga, no.”

I took my car onto the staircase beside her.

The first impact drove the breath out of me. The second made my teeth meet. I kept the Lamborghini straight while her car bounced against the stone rail, scraping sparks from the wall. We descended side by side, two beautiful machines doing something no manufacturer had put in the brochure.

At the bottom stood a flower stall, an old stone well and a crowd trapped between them.

I hit the horn.

People moved.

Inez's car wasn't going to stop in time.

I drove across her path and caught her front corner against the rear of mine.

The impact spun us both. My shoulder hit the door. Flowers exploded across the windshield. We came to rest around the stone well, her car on one side, mine on the other, with a wreath hanging from my mirror like I had won a funeral.

For one second all I heard was ticking metal.

“Inez!”

“I'm here.”

“You hurt?”

“My pride.”

“Nigga, it needed room anyway.”

She laughed once, then groaned.

I was out before she opened her door. Her ankle had twisted beneath the pedals. She could move it, but not without pain. I helped her onto the low stone wall around the well.

A woman from the flower stall came over holding two huge bouquets.

“For you,” she said breathlessly.

“Ma'am, I just destroyed your business.”

“You saved my sister.”

Behind her, another woman was crying and pointing at the place where Inez's car would have gone.

The flower seller pushed the bouquets into my hands.

“Take them.”

Nigga, what was I supposed to do? Refuse flowers while standing in a car crash shaped like a love letter?

I handed one bouquet to Inez.

“First date on the mountain, second in a well. You moving fast.”

“Tell your wife I want a refund.”

The crowd laughed. It helped. Fear loosened enough for people to start moving again.

My radio crackled. Shawna's voice came through, low and urgent.

“Talk to me.”

“I'm good. Inez twisted her ankle. We're both out.”

“Your chest?”

“Fine.”

“Your side?”

“Fine, baby.”

She took one breath I could hear all the way from the grid.

“Then get her help.”

The marshals reached us two minutes later. One tried to tell me to stand aside. I showed him my badge, pointed to Inez and told him to be useful in that direction.

Above us, at the top of the staircase, Bellario appeared.

He was speaking into a radio.

Smiling.

Not at me. At somebody I couldn't see.

I looked down at the white marshal's stripe reflected in the broken glass on my hood. Then up at his radio.

Something in me went still.

Inez followed my eyes.

“You see it too,” she said.

“I'm starting.”

The race continued around us. The leaders had gone. The announcer's voice drifted over the buildings, saying Captain Aiden had made another courageous sacrifice and would surely receive the admiration of the entire principality.

Admiration was nice.

Nigga, I wanted my key.

Lark arrived with Tank and a service crew. She went straight to the Lamborghini, checked the wheels, looked beneath the front and stood back with both hands on her hips.

“You are a violent owner.”

“She still run?”

“She is offended. She still runs.”

I got in.

Tank leaned through the window.

“Aiden.”

“What?”

“You serious?”

I looked at him.

He stepped back and slapped the roof.

“Go get them niggas.”

There was no easy road out of the market. There was, however, an arcade through the old customs building, a broad set of doors and a courtyard that opened onto the last climb. Lark looked at the route. Looked at me. Then she moved her tools out of the way.

I drove through the customs house with both bouquets on the passenger seat.

A guard dropped his sandwich.

I shouted an apology through the window because I respected lunch.

We came out on the climb half a lap behind the leaders. My car's rear panel flapped. The steering pulled a little. There were petals in every vent. Nigga, we looked like the world's most expensive prom had escaped from a hurricane.

I drove anyway.

One car at the old palace. Another beside the railway. Two through the harbor tunnel. The crowd saw the broken blue Lamborghini and rose in sections, pointing, shouting, spreading my return faster than the announcer could understand it.

By the final lap I was fourth.

By the glass palace I was third.

Ahead, Prince Bastian's yellow car chased a silver coupe toward the line. Behind them rolled the white pace car that should have withdrawn at the final corner.

Instead, it slowed across the road.

Bellario stood at the finish with the jeweled key on a cushion.

Nigga wanted me stopped one more time.

I put on my siren.

The crowd screamed.

The pace car didn't move.

I leaned out the window and pointed straight at its driver.

“You are under arrest, motherfucker!”

He looked back.

That was his mistake.

The silver coupe went left. Bastian went right. I went straight, not into the pace car but across the low marble divider beside it, the Lamborghini lifting just enough to make every camera on the finish line tilt upward.

For a breath I was above all three of them.

Then I landed beyond the pace car, the steering wheel kicking against my hands, and crossed the line first with my siren singing louder than the anthem.

The palace windows shook with the crowd.

I stopped, climbed out and walked back toward the pace car. Its driver had both hands up already. Bellario came toward me with his radio against his chest.

“Captain, the result requires review.”

“Nigga, review this.”

I pointed to the driver.

“Who told him to stay on the track?”

“A communication error.”

“Who told the other car to block the descent?”

“An unfortunate sequence—”

“Keep talking. You might confess by accident.”

The cameras moved closer. Bellario saw them. Saw the crowd. Saw the duke on the glass balcony, watching him with a face that offered no rescue.

He lifted the cushion.

“The first key,” he announced, voice suddenly enormous, “to Captain Aiden.”

The driver of the pace car pointed at Bellario.

"That man told me—"

"Nigga, I know which man," I said. "I'm asking why you let that nigga choose where your car stopped."

"He's the marshal."

"And I'm the nigga you tried to stop. See how the second title got important?"

Tank arrived beside me. "Nigga, keep pointing. We love directions."

Bellario lifted the cushion higher. "The key, Captain."

"Look at this nigga changing the subject with jewelry," Porsche said.

"Good jewelry?" Mercedes asked.

"We'll ask Marcellus. Nigga ain't bribing us with plate twice."

I took the key.

Then I took his radio.

“Evidence,” I said.

He looked like he wanted to bite me.

Shawna came through the crowd at a run. I caught her with my good arm, lifted her off the ground and kissed her while the city shouted around us.

“You brought me flowers,” she said when she saw the car.

“Whole market.”

“You're bleeding.”

“Small amount.”

“Nigga.”

I loved when she said it like that. Like my entire government name had disappointed her in one syllable.

She pressed a cloth to the scrape above my eyebrow. Behind her, Inez arrived in the medical cart with her ankle wrapped and both bouquets in her lap. She had kept mine safe.

“You won in that?” she asked, looking at my car.

“Car wanted it bad.”

She shook her head slowly.

“I've raced the best drivers in the world.”

“Now you have.”

She laughed, then raised her bouquet toward the crowd.

“Aiden!” she shouted.

The crowd answered so loudly the duke stepped back from his balcony.

That night, Lark laid Bellario's confiscated radio on our dining table. It had gone silent the moment I took it. Nothing useful remained on it, and we weren't going to invent a miracle because a radio looked important.

But Luc recognized the little silver mark etched on the back.

A white bird.

“The Sovereign Sun uses that mark,” he said. “The old crew put it on their equipment.”

Cassius reached into his pocket and put his father's little metal bird beside it.

Same wings.

Same lowered head.

Inez unfolded Amalia's letter.

“Now,” she said, “we read the whole thing.”

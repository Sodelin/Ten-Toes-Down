# 16. The Storm Asked Who Was Driving

Single narrator: the funeral ticket man. Verbatim chapter body.

Nigga, that's a hurricane. Tank stood under the hotel canopy watching rain bounce off the pavement. It's a storm, Lark said. It just threw a chair. A terrace chair skated past us and hit a planter. Mateo caught it before it reached the road. See? Tank said. Nigga trying to furnish the harbor. Mateo laughed, carried the chair inside and came back to Lark.

Three days free. Already he knew Tank would hold weather personally responsible for its behavior. The Night Baptism was supposed to start at sunset. Nobody had seen the sun for hours, so an official announced that it had probably set and carried on with his flags. Rain lashed the harbor. Boats pulled at their ropes.

The course ran along the coast, through tunnels and over a causeway to a lighthouse, then back toward the city. It should have been postponed. Aurelio offered each driver the chance to withdraw, taking care to stand where the camera could record every refusal. Nigga brought a photographer to a forecast. I chose the dark green Aston Martin from the loan fleet.

The police Lamborghini needed a rest, according to Lark, who had explained this with the keys in her pocket. She put one hand on the Aston's roof. Bring this one back. Nigga, I bring things back. Assembled. Mateo bent over the open door to check a fitting. That does make a difference. You been free three days and already joining her side?

Nigga, I picked her side before they locked me up. Lark handed him a tool without looking. He handed another back. They disagreed about something beneath the dashboard, kissed briefly when they both straightened, and went right back to disagreeing. I watched until Lark caught me. What? Nothing. Car looks good. Then look at the car.

Shawna arrived in a dark coat over her team jacket, carrying my helmet. Water shone in her hair. She made me look at her before she handed it over. You don't have to prove the storm wrong. I ain't mad at the storm. Good. Nigga ain't met me. She closed her eyes, then laughed despite herself. There he is. I took the helmet.

She kept hold of it one moment longer. Come back and tell me about it. I kissed her forehead. Every turn. Nero stood beside Cassius's silver car at the grid, speaking quietly to his son. Inez waited nearby in red, watching the rain strike the road. Rafe had withdrawn. Adrian had entered.

So had six other drivers whose pride, ambition or bank accounts had made a complicated decision on their behalf. The duke stood beneath a huge green umbrella. The stranger in the dark uniform stood behind him without one. Rain ran off his shoulders. Nigga didn't blink. I walked past both of them toward the car. Aurelio spoke.

Perhaps tonight we discover whether you are fortunate or talented. Nigga, you discovered talent at the fountain. Now you're investigating denial. The mechanics nearby burst out laughing. He let the sound pass over him. The sea gives nothing back for admiration. Then stop watching me so hard. I got in. The light changed. We left into the rain.

The Aston moved differently from the Lamborghini, heavier through the first turn, planted when the road opened. I kept it steady while water blurred the windshield and headlights tore long tunnels through the darkness ahead. Adrian took an early lead. Inez followed him. Cassius stayed just behind me, patient, listening to the road instead of the audience. The city vanished behind us.

On the coast road, the wind struck sideways hard enough to move the whole car. I corrected and felt the tires answer. Rain drummed against the roof. The engine held its deep note beneath everything, a sound I could trust when the world outside had become water and occasional stone. Nigga, I told the car, we doing fine. It did not answer in words.

Cars know when to be professional. We reached the first tunnel in fourth. Inside, the sudden absence of rain felt almost violent. The road shone under the lamps. I passed Adrian's teammate at the exit, came out into the storm in third and saw Inez's red taillights ahead. She moved left before the next bend. I stayed right.

We ran side by side through a wall of spray. Then her voice came over the channel. You enjoying the ocean now? Nigga, my wife wanted all of it. She laughed, and we took the curve together. At the lighthouse causeway, the marshals waved yellow flags.

Water washed over the low wall in bursts, not deep enough to stop the cars but enough to make every movement matter. Adrian slowed. Inez held back. I came alongside the white car at the first long straight and saw his face through the window, jaw tight, eyes fixed ahead. He was frightened. Good drivers get frightened.

They just don't always let the fear choose their hands. I passed him cleanly. The lighthouse rose ahead, its beam sweeping the rain. We circled beneath it and turned back toward the mainland. The sea hit the wall with a force that made the road shake. Then the lights went out again. Not the harbor this time. The lighthouse. The beam stopped.

For one second the whole causeway disappeared. A truck came out of the darkness at the far end, headlights high, moving toward the race against the traffic. Nigga. I saw the shape. The distance. The narrow space between its wheels and the wall. I didn't have time to be angry. I took the Aston toward the low sea barrier.

The right wheels climbed the stone edge. The car tilted. Rain struck the side window. For a breath we ran on two wheels, the truck filling the space where my hood should have been. Its mirror struck mine. Glass vanished. Then I was past. The Aston came down hard, rear tire bursting as it hit the road.

I fought the wheel, kept the nose straight and heard the truck brake behind me as Inez and Adrian scattered around it. Truck on the causeway! I shouted into the channel. Cassius answered. I see it. A crash sounded behind me. Then his voice again. Clear. We're clear. I breathed. The tire was gone. The car dragged to the right.

Sparks ran beneath the rear corner, bright against the rain. Ahead lay five miles to the city and the finish. Lark's voice came through. Aiden, stop. The wheel still on. Nigga, that isn't the whole requirement. I can feel it. Then feel where you can pull over. I slowed enough to bring the car under control. Other drivers passed.

Inez first, then Adrian, then Cassius, who stayed beside me for a moment. You need me? he called. Nigga, go win something. He hesitated. Go! He went. I found a marshal's shelter near the mainland end of the causeway. Two officials stood beneath it, white-faced. One had a radio that wasn't working. The other stared at my ruined wheel. Do you have a service car?

I asked. He nodded toward an old blue coupe parked beneath a tree. It was not a racing car. It looked like a schoolteacher had lent it to his cousin and expected it back with a full tank. The rules allowed a replacement vehicle in the event of race interference, provided the driver completed the course from the point of the stop.

Nero had made me read that part before the start. Nigga believed in knowing where a duke might try to lie. I pointed at the coupe. That one. The official shook his head. It's mine. You got insurance? Yes, but— I got money. We both protected. He looked at my face. Then at the race disappearing into the rain. Then he handed me the keys.

My wife named it Clementine. Nigga, Clementine about to become famous. The marshal looked at his keys in my hand. "Nigga, you really taking Clementine?" "Nigga, she ready." "She's never been in a race." "Neither had that nigga's boat yesterday. Look what happened." He stared toward the storm.

"My wife going to kill me." "Tell her the nigga with the badge borrowed it." "She'll say which nigga." "Then show her the race." He laughed, frightened and delighted at once. "Nigga, bring my eggs." "I heard you the first time, breakfast captain." I got in. The seat smelled faintly of peppermint. A little knitted bird hung from the mirror.

There was a bag of groceries in the back and a classical music station playing softly beneath the storm. I moved the eggs onto the floor. Greatness respects breakfast. Then I drove. Clementine did not leap forward. Clementine considered. Then she went.

I took the first bend clean, let the little car gather speed on the descent and felt its narrow body slip through the wind more easily than the heavier Aston had. It had no business being in the race. Neither had I, according to most of the people whose names were now beneath mine on the standings. We understood each other.

At the old tunnel I caught the last racing car. The driver looked over. Looked again. Nigga, I said through the open window, you getting passed by groceries. I went around him. The radio crackled with disbelief from the commentary booth. They had found me on a camera and were trying to decide whether the picture had been mislabeled.

Captain Aiden appears to be driving— A car, Tank shouted somewhere behind the open team channel. Nigga, finish the sentence. Lark was laughing now. She didn't want to. I could hear her fighting it. Keep it together, she told me. Me or Clementine? Both of you fools. We climbed toward the city. One car. Then another.

The little blue coupe ran beneath the lamps with its knitted bird swinging wildly and the eggs safe on the floor. I drove every turn as if I had known the machine my whole life. No blue thunder. No borrowed power. Just hands, road and a stubborn little engine discovering it had more in it than its owner had ever asked.

By the harbor I was fourth. The crowd saw me. Nigga, I will never forget that sound. It started with laughter. Not mockery. Astonishment too large to come out politely. Then it became shouting. People pointed at the blue coupe, at my badge held against the windshield, at the grocery bag visible through the rear window. That's him! Nigga, that's Aiden!

He in a regular car! The nigga made it irregular! Inez saw me in her mirror and started laughing over the radio. No. Yes. Aiden. Nigga, move. I got milk. She fought me through the next bend anyway. Nearly held me. I passed her at the exit with the knitted bird swinging like it was cheering for its own family. Cassius was second. Adrian first.

The final straight opened before us, wet and bright beneath the city lights. Adrian's boat-race pride had become a car-race lead, and he meant to keep it. Cassius pressed him on the inside. I came through the gap between their spray. Clementine's engine screamed. The little bird hit the windshield. We crossed first by half a bumper.

I stopped beneath the finish arch with steam rising from the hood and the classical station still playing. For three seconds nobody moved. Then the official whose car I had borrowed arrived on the back of a service motorcycle and fell to his knees in front of Clementine. My wife is never going to believe this. I got out and handed him the eggs.

Nigga, breakfast intact. Shawna ran toward me laughing so hard she could barely breathe. She hit my chest with both hands, then kissed me, rain on her face, joy everywhere. You won in that? I looked back at the little blue coupe. Baby, that's Clementine. The crowd took up the name. Clementine. Clementine. Clementine.

The duke presented the fifth key while a thousand people chanted for a schoolteacher's grocery car. Nigga, I had never seen a rich man lose to produce before. It looked excellent on him.

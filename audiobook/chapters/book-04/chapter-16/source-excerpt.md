The Count of Valcera asked to dance with my wife while I was standing close enough to hear the mistake form.

Forty-one years old. Silver hair. Black velvet jacket. A smile that had probably opened a lot of doors for the nigga before he reached the one with my name on it.

"One dance," he said.

Shawna looked at him.

Then at me.

The little smile she gave me was dangerous. Not because she wanted the count. Because she wanted to see what kind of foolishness a nigga would build around the possibility.

"He asked me," she said.

"I heard the nigga."

"Then let me answer."

I took a sip of champagne that suddenly tasted like patience.

She turned back to him.

"Thank you, but I came with my husband."

The count bowed.

"A fortunate man."

"Nigga," I said, "that's the first accurate thing you've said."

He took the refusal properly and moved away. I watched him go until Shawna put two fingers under my chin and brought the rest of my attention home.

"You were about to buy the orchestra."

"Already paid them."

"To stop playing?"

"Baby, don't insult my imagination. I was going to have them play something no nigga could dance to."

"So your plan was to ruin your own evening?"

I considered the champagne. It offered no defense.

"A tactical expense."

She laughed into my shoulder. "You heard me say no."

"Beautifully. Strong finish. Excellent breath support."

"Then enjoy being the man I said yes to."

That landed. I put my glass on a passing tray and offered her my hand with all the ceremony I had been wasting on another nigga's back.

"Mrs. Aiden, would you—"

"My name is Shawna. Try the whole woman."

"Shawna. My wife. Owner of this evening and several excellent shoes. May a nigga have the dance?"

She placed her hand in mine.

"He may."

Behind us Tank cleared his throat. Rosa had apparently been listening.

"Don't copy it," I told him.

"Nigga, I was breathing."

"You was rehearsing."

Rosa offered Tank her hand before the two of us could make a municipal project out of asking women who had already come with us to move twelve feet across a room.

We reached the floor together. Shawna stopped me at the edge and straightened my collar.

"And be nice to the count. He accepted my answer."

"I accepted it too."

"You been accepting it with your whole neck."

I let my shoulders drop. She felt it happen through my hand, smiled once, and stepped beside me. The room had plenty of eyes. I finally had mine on the right person.

The ballroom final took place in the restored lower hall of the palace. We kept the roofless old room above locked and watched. Its stones, its secret stair, and the seven-part crescent were too important to leave unattended while the island danced.

Below, the hall shone. Blue roses along the pillars. Silver candles on the tables. Mirrors cleaned until a nigga had to confront the full quality of his tailoring from every direction. A hundred couples had come to watch twenty-four compete, and half the men looked at my jacket with the private pain of somebody realizing he should have spent more money.

My sling was gone. Vega had checked the shoulder that morning and warned me against treating improvement like immortality. I promised a quiet evening. She looked at the orchestra, the guards, the cameras, and the four former enemy captains drinking at my table.

"Define quiet," she said.

"Baby, we got strings."

She refused to accept music as a medical category.

Isolde came down the stairs with Marcellus on her arm.

The room changed when they entered.

Not the way it changed for me, loud and hungry and already reaching for cameras. This was older. People who knew her name stood without being asked. Dancers turned from their own reflections. The orchestra leader lowered his hands until she reached the floor.

Marcellus wore the white dinner jacket again, properly cleaned, the little cut on his leg hidden and nearly healed. He held himself straight beside her. The old nigga had stopped trying to defeat time. He was standing in it with a woman who remembered him before the gray and liked what she saw afterward.

I leaned toward Tank.

"Look at our jeweler."

"Nigga got his whole past wearing earrings."

"Present," Rosa corrected.

Tank nodded. "Nigga got his whole present wearing earrings."

Isolde heard him.

"Better," she said.

The first competition belonged to the veteran pairs. Couples who had danced together for years. Couples who had lost partners and found new ones. One pair had met only a month earlier and moved with such fierce concentration that nobody dared suggest a month was too short to matter.

Marcellus and Isolde stood last in the line.

When the music began, I watched his hand settle at her back.

No trembling now.

They moved.

The first turn was almost cautious. The second wasn't. By the third I could see the photograph alive again, not copied, not pretending thirty-five years had vanished. Something better than that. Two people making room for everything that had happened and still finding the same place to put their feet.

Isolde smiled at him.

Marcellus smiled back with the whole face I'd rarely seen him allow himself.

They won before the judges raised their cards. Every dancer in the room knew it. The applause began at the end of the last note and kept going until Isolde pressed her forehead against his and laughed because there was nowhere else for the feeling to go.

The old nigga cried.

Didn't hide it. Didn't apologize. Just stood there holding the woman while the room gave them the years it could.

I clapped until my shoulder complained.

"Vega watching," Tank whispered.

"Nigga, let her charge me for joy."

Our turn came after midnight.

The Grand Dance Crown had three rounds. A slow dance. A fast one. A final piece chosen by the orchestra with no announcement, so a couple had to hear each other instead of waiting for the steps they'd practiced.

The count and his wife were good. Her name was Anika, thirty-eight, and she danced with a look that said she'd spent years making his confidence appear deserved. They moved beautifully together when he remembered not to fight her. When he forgot, the beauty tightened around the edges.

Shawna watched them from beside me.

"He pulls too hard," she said.

"Nigga likes proving he's leading."

She looked up at me.

I held her gaze.

"I learned," I said.

"I know."

We took the floor.

The slow dance began with a single violin. I felt her breath before I moved. Let the first step belong to both of us. The room turned around her shoulder, candlelight slipping across the watch on her wrist, her fingers warm against my neck.

I could have looked at the crowd.

I wanted to. Nigga, I am still me. A room full of people admiring my wife and the man she chose is a beautiful thing to notice.

But she was looking at me, and the room lost the competition.

We turned through the light.

Somewhere beyond her face a judge lowered his pen. Somewhere a camera moved. Somewhere the count realized the audience wasn't watching his perfect jacket anymore.

I only knew because Shawna's smile changed.

"What?" I whispered.

"You stopped showing them."

"Baby, I'm showing you."

She brought her hand a little closer against my shoulder.

We finished the first round to applause that sounded farther away than it was.

The fast dance brought the room back.

Drums under the strings. Brass rising. The floor suddenly alive beneath everybody's feet. I took Shawna through the first quick turn and felt her laugh before I heard it. That laugh did something to my knees no enemy had managed. We moved faster, not frantic, each change answering the last so cleanly I could almost feel the music waiting for us to arrive.

Tank started shouting.

Then Rosa.

Then Mercedes yelled, "NIGGA, THAT'S A HUSBAND," and half the room lost whatever dignity it had brought to the event.

The count tried to match our pace. Anika could have. He couldn't. At the far turn his foot caught the hem of her dress. She recovered him before he fell, smiling so professionally it made the mistake hurt worse.

I saw it.

Shawna saw it.

We didn't laugh.

A woman saving her man from his own feet deserved better than being made the joke.

We finished together, breathing hard, her forehead against my chest for one second before she stood straight and let the room see her smile.

Two rounds to us.

The final music began without warning.

The old seven-turn melody.

Not the exact tempo from the ruined ballroom. Faster now, richer, the orchestra giving it a new body. Isolde lifted her head across the room. Marcellus's hand closed around hers.

The count looked sharply toward the musicians.

I understood then.

Saint had arranged the piece. He wanted to see the dance. Wanted the cameras to show him how our feet moved through the sequence that had opened his precious room.

The nigga thought he'd found a way to steal knowledge without coming ashore.

Shawna felt my hesitation.

"What?"

"He's watching."

She looked toward the camera above the far mirror.

Then back at me.

"Then give him something worth watching."

We danced.

Not the lock. Not the seven stops. The music belonged to more than a buried door. We took the melody somewhere else, turning where the old sequence would have paused, letting a long step become a close one, making the whole thing ours while Saint watched from the wrong end of the sea.

My wife followed me so quickly I felt drunk with being understood.

Nigga, there is power in a car. There is power in a gun. There is power in a room standing when you enter.

And then there is a woman knowing where you mean to go before you have found the words to ask her.

The last note held.

I brought her close.

We stopped together.

The room exploded.

The count didn't applaud.

His wife did.

She came over before the judges finished adding their numbers and kissed Shawna on both cheeks.

"Beautiful," she said. "Both of you."

The count stood behind her with one hand inside his jacket.

I saw the movement.

So did Porsche.

She stepped behind him and put the stolen champagne bucket over his wrist before the knife cleared the cloth.

The sound was small and expensive.

He bent forward, gasping.

Mercedes took the knife from his fingers.

"Nigga," she said, "that's not a dance move."

Graves reached us at once. He held the count while Knox checked the little radio concealed beneath his collar.

Saint's voice hissed through it.

"Did you get the sequence?"

I took the radio.

"Nigga, you gotta feel it in your hips."

I dropped it into Mercedes's empty champagne glass.

The count's wife looked at him as if she had finally found the right distance from which to see her marriage.

"A knife?" she said.

He opened his mouth.

She removed the wedding ring from her finger and placed it carefully in his jacket pocket.

"You can keep leading yourself."

Then she walked over to Isolde and asked whether she might sit with her.

The older woman made room.

They crowned Shawna first.

I insisted.

The gold laurel settled against her dark hair, and she looked at me with a happiness so bright I nearly forgot to accept my own.

Isolde raised her glass from the table.

"To people who know how to share a room," she said.

Marcellus touched his glass to hers.

Tank pulled Rosa closer.

Graves looked at Knox.

And my wife took my hand in the middle of the floor while every nigga in the room stood to see us dance once more.

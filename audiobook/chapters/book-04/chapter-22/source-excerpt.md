We buried Solomon Saint on a hill where he could overlook the island without making any more decisions about it.

No statue. No giant speech. His surviving men were permitted to attend, and the ones who had surrendered kept their lives. The ones who had ordered attacks on civilians went with Knox's officers to answer for them. I didn't stand at the grave pretending I'd discovered forgiveness between the gunfight and breakfast.

The nigga had tried killing my people.

I had killed him first.

The island got another morning.

That was the part I cared about.

By noon all twelve ships flew blue.

They lined the bay with their crews on deck, horns sounding one after another as the flags went up. Keel stood beside Dawn on the first pier. Graves had a new jacket. Knox had the expression of a woman watching a successful operation while refusing to admit how much she enjoyed the man standing next to her.

Tank counted the ships twice.

"Twelve," he said.

"Nigga, you found multiplication?" Mercedes asked.

"I found ownership. Different joy."

The champagne bucket stood on the table between them full of blue roses. Mercedes had polished it until the whole harbor could admire itself in the side.

Tank set the dented bucket from the Apostle beside it.

"Brought your cousin home."

Mercedes stared.

"What the fuck is that?"

"A veteran. Show respect."

Porsche turned it and found the dent. "That's a very specific opinion somebody had about a head."

"Saved us a ladder," Tank said.

Mercedes looked at the ugly bucket again. Then she took three roses from her own and arranged them in it.

"Fine. He can stay."

"He?" Rosa asked.

"Look at him. Stubborn, useful, needs cleaning. That's a man."

Tank put a hand to his chest. "Nigga, I just got here."

A waiter approached to clear the table. Four people told him not to touch the buckets at the same time. He withdrew with the careful nod of a man who had learned which part of rich people's nonsense might affect his afternoon.

Porsche watched him go.

"We need to tip that man."

"Already did," Mercedes said. "I'm difficult. I'm not cheap."

I left them arguing over whether two buckets counted as a collection. Behind the table, crews were coming ashore in clean shirts. Some recognized one another and embraced. Some stood back, uncertain where they belonged until Alma called them toward the food. There was room. We had spent twelve days making room the hard way; the least a nigga could do now was let people sit in it.

I stood on the pier with Shawna beside me and the championship crown in my hands.

It was ridiculous.

Gold inside gold inside gold, all those points reaching upward like the thing was trying to win another competition against itself. Saint had designed it for a head that needed help looking important. On me, it just looked like the jeweler had finally been given enough room to express his opinion.

Shawna put it on my head.

The harbor roared.

Niggas on boats. Niggas on roofs. Niggas in the square who had been calling me captain twelve days earlier and now needed three titles just to finish the first breath. The heavyweight champions stood beside the scientists. The cooks stood beside the cops. Sex workers leaned over the balcony of the best hotel on the island and waved silk scarves at the man who had just helped two of their friends buy one of their own.

I held my wife's hand up with mine.

The roar got louder.

"That's your woman," somebody shouted.

"Nigga," I called back, "that's why I'm standing this straight."

They laughed and kept cheering.

Then the island gave Shawna her own stage.

Not another temporary platform over Alma's vegetables. A permanent pavilion at the water, open to the sea, with a roof shaped to carry a singer's voice into the square. The workers had been finishing it while the championship kept us busy. Tomas showed her the name above the entrance.

SHAWNA'S HARBOR.

She stopped walking.

I watched her read it once.

Then again.

"They asked me," I said quietly. "I said ask her."

She looked at Tomas.

He held out the little ceremonial key.

"May we?"

My wife nodded before she trusted her voice.

Then she hugged him, and Alma, and the two young adult carpenters standing beside the entrance looking more frightened of her gratitude than they'd been of the general's soldiers.

I stood back and let her have it.

A nigga can give his wife diamonds. I had done that with enthusiasm. This was people giving her a place because she had already put something in their lives they wanted to keep hearing.

She sang there that evening.

The blue piano came off the yacht, carefully, with Tank's engineer supervising every inch as if the instrument had a nervous system he had personally promised to protect. Rosa joined her. Silk brought the band. Darius attempted a backing vocal until Kong informed him that friendship did not require lying about pitch.

The harbor ate, drank, danced, and spent enough money to make my westside managers proud from a distance.

I had kept the empire. The cars. The clubs. The island. The twelve ships. The new fortune waiting in the vault while Marcellus and the people who understood old art made sure we didn't treat a painting like an expensive tablecloth.

I was still a criminal.

Still a captain.

Still the nigga my wife could order into a chair when my shoulder hurt.

Nothing I had won improved on the last one.

Near midnight, Knox found me beside the water.

Graves stood a little distance behind her, giving us room without looking like he wanted to be anywhere else.

"I'm going back to Vegas," she said.

"City misses your sunshine."

"Someone has to prevent your officers from requesting Lamborghinis as standard equipment."

"Forward-looking niggas."

She almost smiled.

"Graves is coming with me."

I looked past her at the man.

He stood straighter.

"Police business?" I asked.

Knox looked me directly in the eyes.

"Some."

Nigga, I had never respected a one-word answer more.

I offered Graves my hand. He shook it hard.

"Look after her," I said.

The chief raised an eyebrow.

"And let her look after you," I added. "Nigga, I know the arrangement."

Graves smiled.

Knox took his hand when they walked away.

No hesitation. No pretending the crowd couldn't see it. She pulled him close at the end of the pier and kissed him under the harbor lights while the band played something slow.

Tank saw from across the terrace.

He slapped my arm.

"Nigga, the police got romantic jurisdiction."

"Let the woman work."

Marcellus and Isolde sat beneath the new pavilion's side lanterns with two untouched glasses of wine between them. His Blue Mercy ring caught the light when he moved his hand. The real stone, the one we'd already bled for before all this, still belonged to him.

Isolde touched the setting with one finger.

"Celeste's?" she asked.

He nodded.

"It's beautiful."

"Yes."

There was no argument in it. No woman asking a man to prove new love by throwing old love into the sea.

Marcellus took a small box from his pocket.

Inside was a bracelet of seven tiny blue stones, their settings joined by a narrow line of silver shaped like dancing feet.

"This one," he said, "I made for you."

Isolde looked at it for a long time.

Then she held out her wrist.

He fastened it with steady hands.

"No more letters that don't arrive," she said.

"No more leaving without saying where."

She kissed him, and the old nigga smiled so hard I looked away to give the moment something cameras didn't need to own.

They would travel together. Isolde wanted to see Midnight City, whose dance halls she'd known when she was younger. Marcellus wanted to go wherever she intended to wake up next. He pretended his decision was professional interest in the Crown of Midnight until she touched his cheek and told him he had always been a poor liar when he was happy.

Mercedes and Porsche showed me the first sign design for the Velvet Mile.

Green and violet. Their colors. Their names on the papers. My money in the walls, their decisions inside them.

"Opening in Vegas after the work is done," Porsche said.

"Aiden brings the cars," Mercedes added.

"Nigga, I bring the whole street."

They kissed my cheeks at the same time, then went back to arguing about whether the rooftop pool needed a piano of its own.

My wife heard that from the stage.

"Yes," she called.

The matter was settled by the highest authority available.

Later, Tank found me at the yacht rail.

Rosa had gone below to rest her voice. He stood beside me looking at the ships for a while, the big nigga quiet in a way I had learned not to interrupt too quickly.

"Vegas," he said at last.

"For the wedding."

"Yeah."

"We'll do it big."

He shook his head, smiling.

"Nigga, I know you. Big ain't the worry. I just want everybody there."

I looked at him.

"Then everybody comes home."

He nodded.

We stood together a little longer, looking at the place we'd arrived with trouble waiting and were leaving with our people louder, richer, and more in love than the general had known how to bear.

The invitation to Midnight City lay on the table inside.

Heavy black paper. Silver letters. A crown pressed into the seal. Emperor Zero had sent it by courier after the call, as if a man became more frightening when his manners required better stationery.

The tournament of criminal kings.

A throne waiting for somebody arrogant enough to think the rest of the world would agree he belonged in it.

Nigga, I had already packed.

We left the island two mornings later.

Long enough for Vega to be satisfied I could lift my arm without making her reconsider her career. Long enough for the ships to take on crews who wanted to be aboard them. Long enough for Shawna to sing once more at her harbor and promise the audience she would come back.

Keel remained with the fleet and the island captains. Cassius and Inez stayed a little longer with their boats and their engagement, promising to answer when the next race got interesting. Darius and Silk went back toward Vegas to prepare the film celebration and argue over its music. Knox and Graves left together. No wedding yet. Plenty of future in the way they walked onto the plane.

Our car convoy went aboard the transport for Midnight City.

Dark-blue Lamborghini. Old black Mercedes. The best of the Monaco fleet. Luggage, instruments, clothes nobody needed but everybody intended to wear. Tank and Rosa. Mercedes and Porsche. Marcellus and Isolde. My wife and me.

We watched Isla Gloria fall away behind the ship.

The twelve blue flags stood above the harbor.

The pavilion roof caught the sun.

My wife leaned against me at the rail, Dante's watch warm on her wrist, my hand around hers.

"Every city wants you," she said.

"They got excellent judgment."

"What do you want?"

I looked at her.

The ocean moved around us. Ahead, another crown, another beautiful liar, another room full of men who would have to learn my name the exciting way.

Beside me, the woman I had been trying to impress since the first night looked up with sunlight on her mouth.

"Nigga," I said softly, "I'm standing next to it."

She smiled and pulled me down to kiss her.

Behind us my brother shouted that somebody had parked a Ferrari in front of the breakfast room.

Mercedes shouted back that the Ferrari looked hungry.

Rosa started laughing. Porsche joined her. Marcellus and Isolde came up the stairs holding hands like the ship had been built to take them somewhere together.

And ahead, beyond the bright water, Midnight City waited for the world champion to arrive.

Let the niggas get dressed.

I was bringing my wife.

# 4. Every Cop in the World Wanted My Autograph

Single narrator: the funeral ticket man. Verbatim chapter body.

The general gave me a car with no doors and told me to pursue justice. This nigga had seen the Lamborghini. This nigga knew what I drove. Nigga still rolled out a rich man's shopping cart and looked at me like a favor had occurred. I looked at the car, then at the nigga, then back at the car.

Somewhere a manufacturer had built this motherfucker without imagining the nigga who would be insulted by it. I looked at the car. I looked at him. "Nigga, justice could at least have windows." It was a low white racing machine with a roll cage, enormous tires, and the temperament of a lawn mower that had inherited money.

The other competitors stood beside identical cars in matching uniforms. Twenty-four captains from twenty-four fictional police forces, all invited to show the world what a real officer could do. Saint had put me at the far end in a uniform two sizes too small. I came in my own. Chief Knox walked the line behind me with the ceremonial sword under one arm.

She had taken off her summer jacket because the sun was hot and she intended to insult several men comfortably. "Your entry conditions permit a personal vehicle," she told Saint. "Only if it meets the spirit of the contest." My Lamborghini rolled through the gate behind her with Mercedes driving and Porsche leaning over the passenger door eating a peach.

The entire line of police captains turned. "That's the nigga's police car?" "That's the nigga's slow police car," Tank said. "Nigga has a faster one?" "Nigga has an island full of faster ones." "How do I join this nigga's department?" Knox looked down the line. "Start by standing up straight." Twenty-three niggas found their spines together.

One man took off his sunglasses and put them back on as if the first look had been a problem with the lenses. I pointed at the dark blue hood. "That nigga got spirit." Saint examined the gold police shield on the door. His reflection looked miserable in my paint. "A single passenger is permitted," he said. Mercedes handed me the keys. "Good.

I'm coming." Shawna stood beneath the awning with Rosa. She wore yellow today, and every breeze seemed to know exactly what to do with the skirt. I went over to kiss her before the start. "What do you want if I win?" I asked. "A blue piano." "Done." "On a boat." "Bigger done." "That stays level when the sea moves." I looked at Tank.

"Nigga, find an engineer." He held up his phone without looking away from Rosa. "Already searching." My wife brushed a little dust from my shoulder. "And you back in one piece." "You asked for the expensive things last." The pursuit course wound from the stadium up through the hillside, across a dry riverbed, through the old port, and back along the sea wall.

Three gates along the way held red flags. A driver had to bring back one from each gate. Opponents could take a flag by touching the long padded arm fixed to the side of their car against another car's red target. That was the official game. The unofficial game was twenty-three niggas looking at me like I had personally insulted every road they'd ever driven.

Captain Graves came over while Mercedes fastened her belt. "You understand there are no second chances on the upper road," he said. "Then don't waste your first one staring at my car." His eyes flicked toward the dashboard. Toward the seat. Toward the wheel. "It's a very good car." "You can say beautiful, nigga. It won't make you less police." He almost smiled.

At the starting line, Mercedes adjusted the rearview mirror toward her face. "Need that," I said. "I need to know how I look when we win." "You look rich." "Then stop talking and make me accurate." The gun sounded. Twenty-four engines hit the morning together.

We came off the line in a cloud of white dust, the little pursuit cars jumping over the rough ground like angry goats. I let three pass me before the first bend. Mercedes watched the mirror. "Graves on your right. Bald nigga on your left. Handsome one trying to get between them." "They all handsome if they behind us." I took the inside turn.

The padded arm on the nearest car swung toward my door. I moved just enough to let him miss and watched his arm strike the car beside him instead. Both men shouted. A red flag shot up from the struck car's rear basket. Mercedes caught it before it touched the ground. She held it across her lap.

"Souvenir." The first gate stood at the bottom of the dry riverbed. Drivers funneled toward it, tires fighting loose stone. Graves went high along the bank. Two others stayed low. I saw the gatekeeper lift three flags for them and calculate how long it would take to get another one ready for me. I didn't give him the time.

The Lamborghini hit the bank, rose across it, and came down beside the gate like a dark blue argument. Mercedes leaned out and took the flag from his raised hand with two fingers. "Thank you, sweetheart." The nigga bowed. We were already gone. Mercedes looked back at the gatekeeper.

"Nigga bowed." "Polite nigga." "Other niggas just got a flag." "Other niggas didn't bring you." She settled deeper into the seat. "Nigga, that's the kind of navigation I enjoy." I grinned. Behind us the gatekeeper was still waving, some nigga taking pictures of a nigga taking a good morning personally. On the upper road the sea opened beneath us.

It was the kind of blue that made a man forgive weather for everything else it had ever done. White buildings clung to the hillside. People watched from balconies holding breakfast plates, cheering whenever my siren cut through the engines. A woman threw flowers. Mercedes caught one and tucked it behind her ear.

"These people understand arrival." I was smiling when the black truck came around the blind curve. It didn't belong in the race. It had no number, no padded arm, and six men standing in the open back holding rifles. The first shot knocked a piece off the stone wall beside my head. Mercedes dropped the flower.

"Nigga, that's real." "I noticed the enthusiasm." I swung the Lamborghini behind a pursuit car as its driver ducked. The truck blocked most of the road. Beyond it the second gate fluttered red and cheerful, not understanding its career had changed. Graves braked beside me. His car struck the wall, sparks running down the side.

He looked at the gunmen, then back toward the stadium below. His general's flags flew over both. "Keep the other drivers back," I told him. "I can engage—" "Nigga, keep them alive. That's an engagement." I drove at the truck. Mercedes caught the handle above her door. She didn't scream. I liked that about her. Plenty of people could act brave in a safe room.

Mercedes looked death in the face and got offended about the outfit. "If we die," she said, "I want you to know this dashboard is too low for my legs." "Write the factory when we win." The truck driver turned to block the narrow gap by the wall. I let him believe I wanted it.

At the last instant I took the raised stone verge on the other side, put two tires high, and brought the Lamborghini onto its side wheels. The horizon tipped. Mercedes pressed herself against the seat as the sea moved above her shoulder. We passed between truck and cliff with my door a hand's width from the rock.

One gunman stared directly into my windshield as we went by. His mouth opened. He had enough time to appreciate the work and not enough time to improve his aim. The car dropped onto four wheels beyond the truck. I turned it across the road and fired back through my open window. The men scattered behind the truck's cab. One dropped his rifle.

Another jumped into the ditch and discovered the ditch contained an extremely angry goat. Mercedes watched him struggle. "Local police," she said. Behind the truck, Graves and two captains had left their cars. Together they overwhelmed the driver while my shots kept the men in the back from rising.

The whole thing ended with five gunmen on the ground and the sixth trying to negotiate with livestock. Graves came around the cab with his jaw clenched. "Those are Saint's men." "Nigga, did the matching outfits help?" He looked at me for a long moment. "I gave him my word that I would compete fairly." "Then do.

You didn't give him your fucking eyes." The other captains gathered around us. The bald one had a cut above his eyebrow. The handsome one was bleeding through his sleeve. Nobody wanted to get back in a car until they knew whether the next corner had another truck waiting. I understood. A nigga can agree to lose a race without agreeing to lose a lung.

These niggas had come with trophies in mind, not funerals. I looked at the wounded nigga, the bald nigga, the handsome nigga no longer worrying about his sleeve. All of them were waiting for the nigga Saint had told them to beat. I took the radio from the truck and put it on the hood. Saint's voice crackled out.

"Has the captain stopped?" I pressed the button. "Just to admire the goats." Silence. Then I heard a chair scrape somewhere. Mercedes leaned close to the radio. "The girls say hello." I left it on the hood. Graves looked down the mountain. The whole race had stopped because I had stopped. Twenty-three proud men waited to see if I would go on.

"We finish," I said. The bald captain nodded first. "Behind you?" "If you can keep up." Nigga grinned like I'd given him his birthday. The second gatekeeper handed Mercedes a flag while bowing so low his cap fell off. At the port we came into streets lined with hanging laundry and cheering workers.

Every other car stayed close behind us, a moving wall of captains who had come to beat me and discovered a better use for their engines. Then a delivery van rolled into the crossing. Its driver jumped out shouting that the brakes were gone. The van rolled downhill toward an outdoor café full of people who had turned their chairs to watch the race.

I didn't have any Blue Thunder in my hands yet. I had been saving it because a nigga couldn't spend electricity like money just because he happened to have some. Now I gave the engine a short bright pulse. The Lamborghini leaped forward. I came alongside the van, matched its awful rolling speed, and pressed my front quarter against it. Metal folded.

My beautiful paint cried without a voice. We turned together, slowly enough to hurt, hard enough to shove my shoulders against the belt. The van struck a stack of empty fish crates instead of the café. Crates exploded across the street. One landed perfectly on the café owner's head. He lifted it off, looked at the untouched tables, and held up both thumbs.

I rested my forehead against the wheel for one breath. Mercedes touched my arm. "You there?" "Yeah." "Good. Because if you pass out I'm driving, and this bitch already offended my knees." I laughed and straightened. My left hand shook once. I kept it low and used the right. At the third gate, the flags had disappeared. Saint hadn't been subtle.

He had simply ordered them removed while we were coming down the hill. A little man in a cream suit stood beside the empty pole looking like his stomach wanted to leave without him. "Regrettable," he said. "Without the final pennant, no competitor can record a complete—" Mercedes got out.

She walked past him, opened the nearby shed, and found twenty-four red flags on a chair. The man closed his eyes. "Nigga," she said softly, "I have found married men in smaller hiding places." She handed one to every captain. "Nigga, say thank you," she told the first. He said it. So did the next nigga. And the next.

By the fifth nigga she was smiling like a woman handing out report cards to a class that had finally learned manners. "Good nigga. Another good nigga. Look at y'all. International courtesy." The little man in the cream suit wished he had gone into the shed with the flags.

Then she got back in my car and crossed her legs as if she had stepped out to powder her nose. We entered the stadium together. The crowd rose. They had seen the truck on the live feed before somebody cut the signal. They had seen the rescue at the café after Porsche somehow found a different camera and put it back on.

Now they saw twenty-four cars pouring through the arch, every police captain on his feet behind the wheel except me because a nigga was tired and had a roof. Graves tried to pass me on the final straight. I respected him for it. I also beat him by half a car. At the line he laughed openly for the first time.

"Had to try." "Good captain." "Better driver," he admitted. "Nigga, don't stop. I'm enjoying this." Saint waited beside the winners' platform holding the first golden laurel. His smile looked like he'd had it installed against medical advice. Before he could speak, the bald captain took the microphone. "We agreed on something out there," he said. Twenty-two men stood behind him. Graves joined them.

"Captain Aiden led us through a live attack, saved civilians during the race, and still crossed first. We recognize him as the champion of pursuit." The crowd roared. Saint had to put the laurel around my neck himself. I bent just enough to make it possible and looked him in the eyes while he did it.

"Nigga," I said quietly, "your games got expensive fast." He released the ribbon like it had burned him. Then the captains started lining up for pictures. Not one. All of them. Some wanted me between them and their cars. One wanted me to hold his radio and say his call sign.

The bald captain admitted he'd told his department he would bring back my autograph if he couldn't bring back the trophy. Knox stood off to the side, watching twenty-three men wait their turn. "You collecting departments now?" she asked. One nigga behind her asked whether I could sign his cap. Another nigga had brought his badge.

A third nigga wanted a photograph for his mother but admitted, when Mercedes looked at him, that his mother did not know how to open photographs on a phone. "Nigga, it's for you," she said. "It's for me." "Let the nigga enjoy himself," I told her.

"I take excellent pictures." "Chief, they followed me home." Shawna came through the crowd and put her hands on my face. She looked at my pupils, my hands, the little line of strain under my mouth. "One burst," I said before she asked.

"And one side of the car." "Baby, both sides still exist." She kissed me, slow enough for every camera to understand that photographs had limits. When she let me go, she looked over my shoulder. At the harbor below, workers were lowering an enormous blue piano onto the deck of a white yacht.

Tank stood beside them, arms folded, while an engineer explained the platform beneath it with wild hand movements. My wife blinked. "Already?" "Nigga found an engineer." Her laugh hit me harder than Darius had. I took the laurel off and put it around Mercedes's neck. She looked down at it, suddenly quiet. "For your knees," I said. She touched the gold leaves.

"You didn't have to do that." "You caught the flags." Porsche leaned against her shoulder for the picture. "And the niggas caught feelings. Look at them." The captains were still standing in line. Saint was not smiling anymore.

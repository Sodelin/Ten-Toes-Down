I promised we'd come and ended the call before Tank could tell me how fast his new relationship went from zero to sixty. At the bakery, two men were robbing the register. Nigga, two grown motherfuckers in black masks stood between me and salted butter at nine-fifteen in the morning. One held a gun. The other held a pastry bag. I stopped in the doorway.

The woman behind the counter looked at me. Then at them. Then she sighed with such relief that the nigga holding the gun turned around to see what she had discovered. "Everybody stay—" He stopped. I took off my sunglasses. His friend looked up from the pastry bag. "Nigga." "Yeah," I said. "Morning." The gunman's eyes widened above the mask.

"Aiden?" "Captain, while you doing that." The other man lowered the bag. "Nigga, I told you we should've gone across town." "You told me the croissants were good!" "They are good! That doesn't make this nigga less here!" I looked at the baker. "You all right?" She nodded. "They haven't hurt anybody." "Good. Let's keep that little achievement intact." I held out my hand.

The gunman stared at it. "What?" "Nigga, give me the gun." His friend nodded urgently. "Give the nigga the gun." "Shut up." "You shut up. That's the nigga from the bridge." I waited. The gunman looked at the photograph of me beside the register. The baker had put it there after I paid for a year's worth of pastries for the officers working night shifts.

In the photograph I held a croissant like a man presenting an important discovery to science. The nigga looked from the photograph back to me. "You really live here." "Nigga, what you think I do? Hover over the city between incidents?" His friend began laughing nervously. The gunman handed me the weapon. Then a third man burst out of the back room.

Of course there was a third nigga. Bad planning loves company. He saw me, saw his friends surrendering, and ran straight through the side door. I looked at the two men in front of me. "Stay." They stayed. The baker reached beneath the counter and brought out the butter. "I'll hold this." "Appreciate you." I ran outside.

The third man jumped into a stolen delivery car and pulled away hard enough to scatter a row of empty crates. I got into the Last Word, hit the lights, and followed. Nigga, my wife had given me one item. One. The delivery car turned onto the boulevard. Ahead, a slow procession of trucks moved toward the civic plaza.

Workers were bringing in equipment for a public celebration I had forgotten to put on my calendar because apparently the city kept finding new reasons to honor me without checking whether I had breakfast plans. On the largest truck stood a covered statue. I recognized the shape beneath the sheet. "Nigga," I muttered. The thief swerved between the trucks.

I followed, close enough to see him looking at me in the mirror with growing spiritual concern. He took the next turn too fast. His car clipped the statue truck's rear corner. The straps snapped. The sheet flew off. A twenty-foot bronze Aiden rolled backward onto its transport platform, one enormous arm pointing at the sky. Nigga, I looked excellent. The truck driver braked.

The statue slid toward the open end. The thief's car stopped beneath its outstretched boot. He looked up. Then at me pulling alongside. Then up again. "You gotta be fucking kidding me," he said. I lowered my window. "Nigga, even my statue caught you." The statue's boot settled against his hood with a deep metallic groan.

The front of the delivery car folded enough to trap it in place. The thief sat frozen behind the windshield while the largest version of my shoe in existence held him politely against the road. Nobody was hurt. Several people were already filming. The nigga opened his door very slowly and raised both hands. "I surrender." "To which one of me?" He stared.

I pointed up at the statue. "Nigga, jurisdiction complicated today." The crowd began laughing. Knox arrived in her own car five minutes later. She looked at the bronze boot on the stolen vehicle. Then at me. Then at the sculptor standing nearby with both hands pressed to his head.

"Captain," she said, "I am glad to see you contributing even while stationary." "Efficient department." The thief looked between us. "Y'all always like this?" Knox put a hand on his shoulder. "Unfortunately for your morning, yes." She took him away. The sculptor approached me, trembling. "The heel may be damaged." I looked up at my bronze face. "Nigga, the cheekbones survived.

We're ahead." He laughed, then almost cried, then asked whether I would stand beside the statue for a photograph before the recovery crew moved it. I did. The crowd cheered. Somebody shouted, "Niggalicious!" Three other niggas shouted it back. I pointed toward them. "Educated citizens." My phone rang. Shawna. I answered.

"Baby." "Butter." "I got it." "I'm watching a twenty-foot version of you arrest a man on my phone." "Then you understand the delay." "Aiden." "Salted. I remembered." She tried not to laugh. I heard her fail. Nigga, that sound could still make a whole city move to the background. I went back to the bakery. Knox's officers had taken the first two men away.

The baker stood behind the counter with the butter wrapped in paper and a bag of pastries beside it. "For your wife," she said. "She sent me for one thing." "Tell her I insisted." "Nigga, excellent witness." The woman laughed. I paid, left more than the bill required, and drove home without purchasing the bakery, the block, or any additional part of municipal government.

Growth, if a nigga wanted to call it that. At the house, Shawna stood in the kitchen wearing one of my shirts and an expression she had clearly been practicing since the video appeared. I held up the butter. She looked at it. Then at the pastries. Then at me. "You made it." "Nigga, against all odds." She came around the counter and kissed me.

Not because the city had cheered. Not because a statue of me now required a new heel. Because I had come through the door and she wanted me close. I put the groceries down and held her until the pan on the stove reminded us breakfast still existed. We ate on the terrace. The garden moved in the breeze.

The white horse grazed beyond the fence. Somewhere inside, my phone kept filling with photographs of bronze greatness and one very frightened thief. I left it there. Shawna buttered her toast and watched me over her coffee. "What?" I asked. "You look happy." I considered making a joke. Then I let the simple thing stay simple for one breath. "I am." She smiled.

After breakfast, we opened the messages from our friends. Rosa sent a recording of the new song. Tank could be heard humming badly in the background until she laughed and made him start again. Cassius and Inez sent a photograph from their racing garage, both fathers standing behind them arguing over a car nobody had asked them to fix.

Their first team driver had won a race that morning. Cassius wrote that victory tasted better when the woman beside him stole his champagne afterward. He called. He had opened the workshop early. Sunlight was good at that hour, he said. Blue Mercy shone on his hand while he worked.

Celeste's letter remained in the drawer where he could reach it. He had begun teaching two grown apprentices who asked good questions and held their tools badly, a combination he described with obvious delight. Isolde taught dancing in the room above the workshop and came downstairs to steal his coffee, correct his posture, and kiss him before the apprentices could pretend they had not noticed.

"Come by next week," he told us. "I have something for the house." "Nigga, we got enough jewels." He laughed. "A clock. You two are always late." Shawna took the phone to defend herself. I listened to them argue and leaned back in my chair. Gideon and Orson were in prison. Their cases had ended months earlier.

Zero lay in a grave in the desert, named plainly, with no throne above him and no unfinished business beneath the stone. The people who had followed him had made their choices, served their sentences, changed their work, or gone home. None of them was waiting in a dark room to become the last page of my life. The last page was here.

My wife on the terrace. My brother calling about dinner. My city loud beyond the walls. Butter on the table. Aight, my nigga. Now look at us. That nigga Tank got a ring on his hand and a woman who makes him dance. That nigga Victor still thinks he can beat me at something, and I love the nigga enough to let him keep trying.

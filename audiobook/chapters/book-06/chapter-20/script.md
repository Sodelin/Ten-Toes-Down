# 20. The Bachelor Party with the Wrong Speech

Single narrator: the funeral ticket man. Verbatim chapter body.

Tank asked for a quiet bachelor party. Nigga, he asked me. That was his first mistake. His second mistake was telling Porsche the budget did not matter. His third was allowing Victor Voltage to bring a karaoke machine he had personally improved.

By nine that evening, the rooftop of one of my clubs held a pool table, a stage, four musicians, enough champagne to make a divorce attorney feel hopeful, and a sign that read ONE LAST NIGHT OF FREEDOM. Rosa saw the sign during setup. She looked at it. Then at Tank. "Freedom?" The nigga turned to Porsche with pure betrayal in his eyes.

Porsche took down the sign. Ten minutes later it read ONE LAST NIGHT OF UNCHECKED LAUNDRY. Rosa approved. So did I. We had not excluded the women. Nigga, most of the men present would have spent the evening calling them anyway.

We gave Rosa and Shawna the lounge downstairs with their friends, and kept the roof for Tank's people until the two parties inevitably decided the other one had better music. Cassius arrived wearing a white jacket and carrying a bottle so old the cork probably remembered a different government. Victor wore blue and looked dangerously pleased with the equipment onstage.

Anton came with Roland, who had accepted the invitation with the bewildered gratitude of a man still adjusting to being invited somewhere without a weapon requirement. Gideon was in prison. Orson was in prison. Roland was standing beside a pool table, holding a drink with a little umbrella in it. Nigga, choices had produced very different evenings.

Tank came out of the elevator wearing black silk and the cautious expression of a man who suspected happiness might have booked entertainment. He saw the stage. Then the lights. Then the drinks. "Brother." "Quiet," I told him. He looked at the drummer. The drummer waved both sticks. "Nigga, define quiet." "Nobody's shooting." Tank considered that. "Fair." The first hour went beautifully. We played pool.

We drank. Cassius told a story about a Monaco hotel that had tried to charge him for damaging a fountain he insisted had damaged him first. Victor described the faculty meeting where my portrait had been approved and did impressions of three professors so accurate Seraphine would have expelled him from dinner if she heard. Roland listened for a while before laughing.

When he finally did, Tank pointed at him. "There. Nigga's becoming a person." "I was a person before." "You wore a white suit to shoot at my brother. That's a job description." Roland raised his glass. "A former job description." We drank to that. Downstairs, the women's party grew louder.

Adult dancers came through the elevator carrying feathered costumes, bottles, and the bright, tired confidence of people who had finished working and wanted the rest of the night to belong to them. Some of the guests disappeared into private rooms with their partners. Others stayed at the bar telling stories filthy enough to make a priest develop an urgent appointment.

There was cocaine at the far end of the party. There were men acting as if cocaine had personally invented their business plans. There were women rolling their eyes at those men and choosing better company. I had a drink, a pool cue, my brother beside me, and no desire to spend the next morning explaining to Shawna why the ceiling had become interesting.

A woman in silver boots leaned against our table and looked at Tank. "You're the groom." He nodded. "Yeah." "You look nervous." "Nigga, people keep noticing." She smiled. "Good. Means you know the size of what you're getting." Tank looked toward the elevator. Rosa's laugh floated up from below. The nigga smiled without thinking about it. "Yeah," he said.

"I do." At the pool table, Anton challenged me to a game. I accepted because, nigga, a king ought to provide recreational disappointment to his friends. He was good. Better than I expected. The big man moved around the table with delicate care, lining up shots as if he apologized to the balls before making them obey him. Tank watched from a chair.

"This nigga got little hands hidden inside the big ones." Anton smiled. "Precision is not limited by size." "That's what I tell Rosa." The whole table groaned. "Nigga!" I shouted. "What? I meant dancing!" Nobody believed him, including the nigga who had said it.

I won the game with a shot that sent the last ball cleanly into the corner while the cue ball stopped exactly where I wanted it. Anton looked at the table, then at me, and bowed with exaggerated despair. "Is there anything?" "Nigga, I still buy the wrong butter occasionally." "A comforting flaw." "Don't spread it around." Tank laughed and reached into his jacket.

A folded paper fell out onto the chair beside him. I saw it but thought nothing of it. That was how the trouble started. Porsche came upstairs looking for a list of song requests. Victor had connected his improved karaoke machine to the stage speakers and announced that he could make any man sound magnificent, a claim I found both interesting and redundant.

"Where's the new song?" Porsche asked. Tank was at the bar. Cassius pointed toward the folded paper on the chair. "There, perhaps." Perhaps. Nigga, that word has injured more evenings than cheap tequila. Porsche took the paper to Victor. Victor read the first line, smiled, and decided it was clearly an emotional number.

He handed it to the keyboard player, who began finding chords under it. Then the lights changed. The stage microphone came alive. "Ladies and gentlemen," Victor announced, "a new song from Captain Aiden, prepared especially for the groom." I turned from the pool table. "Nigga, what?" The crowd cheered. Tank looked at me from the bar, confused. I looked at Victor.

Victor held up the paper. Nigga, the man had the confidence of a superhero and the information of a drunk wedding guest. A dangerous combination. Porsche pushed a microphone into my hand. "Go. They love you." "Those are separate issues." The keyboard player began.

I stepped onto the stage because a nigga can only explain confusion so loudly before a crowd decides it is part of his introduction. The first line appeared on the screen behind me. I KNOW HOW TO STAY. I stared. The second line followed. I STAYED IN ROOMS WHERE LEAVING WOULD HAVE BEEN SMARTER. I looked at Tank. His face changed.

Nigga, those were his vows. His private words for Rosa, folded in his pocket while he tried to become the man who could say them out loud without shaking. Victor had put them on a twelve-foot screen with a slow jam behind them. Tank started toward the stage. I lifted my hand. Not at him. At the keyboard player. "Different song." The musician blinked.

"Captain?" "Different song, nigga. Give me something with an ass on it." The drummer laughed and changed the beat. I took the paper from Victor, folded it, and put it inside my jacket. Then I turned back toward the crowd. "Y'all know my brother?" They cheered. "Nigga's getting married." Louder.

"Which means the rest of us are going to have to stop borrowing his shirts without asking Rosa where the good laundry lives." Tank stopped halfway to the stage. The crowd laughed. I began talking over the beat. Not his vows. My own ridiculous shit. "That's my nigga by the bar, looking like a bank vault learned to love.

That nigga got a ring, got a woman, got a reason to stop pretending the sofa is a bed. Rosa said dance, the nigga said which direction. Rosa said blue, the nigga bought the ocean a sample card." The audience howled. Tank covered his face. "Nigga, this man stole a boat with his girlfriend beside him and still found time to argue about cake.

That's commitment. That's range. That's a nigga who knows love comes with refreshments." Rosa appeared in the elevator doorway. Shawna stood beside her. Both women looked at Tank. Then at me onstage. The drummer gave me a flourish. I pointed at my brother. "He got hands like trouble and a heart like somebody left the porch light on.

He'll drag a nigga out of a fire, then ask whether anybody saved his good jacket. He'll fight the world, but Rosa says sit and the nigga discovers furniture immediately." Tank laughed despite himself. Rosa put both hands over her mouth. I let the beat run beneath the next words. "That's my brother. That's the nigga. The one who came. The one who stayed.

The one I'm standing beside when the whole city watches him get the best thing he ever asked for." I raised my glass. "To Tank." The room rose. Every nigga at every table stood, cheering, glasses in the air. Tank looked at me. The anger had not vanished. I could still see it under the embarrassment, under the love. That was fair.

Something private had nearly been taken from him in a room full of people he wanted to trust. I came off the stage and put the folded paper in his hand. "Let's talk." We went onto the quieter side terrace. The city glowed below us. Music moved through the glass doors behind us.

Tank held the paper between two fingers like it might bite him if he looked at it too long. "You read it?" he asked. "The first two lines. Same as everybody." "I didn't give it to you." "I know." "Nigga, I been working on that." "I know." He looked at me. "Then why—" "Porsche thought it was the song list. Cassius guessed. Victor got enthusiastic.

A whole chain of niggas with confidence and no fucking evidence." Tank breathed out. The explanation did not magically make the feeling leave. I let him stand with it. After a while he unfolded the paper. His own handwriting filled the page, big and careful. Some words crossed out. Others written above them.

A man who had never needed help making his fists understood trying to choose exactly what his heart ought to sound like. "I don't want it to sound like you," he said. I looked at him. "Good." "Everybody expects some big shit when we talk. Some line. Some thing they can yell back.

I don't want to get up there and sound like a nigga trying to perform my own wife." Nigga, that hit me cleanly. I put my glass on the railing. "Then don't." He looked down at the page. "What if I forget?" "You know her." "What if I can't say it?" "She's gonna be standing right there. Talk to Rosa.

Let the rest of us be furniture." He smiled faintly. "You gonna manage furniture?" "Nigga, for five minutes. Because I love you. Don't exploit it." He laughed. The terrace door opened. Victor stepped out with Porsche and Cassius behind him.

All three looked sober in the specific way people become sober when they realize a joke nearly cost somebody something they cannot replace with a better bottle. "Tank," Victor said, "I am sorry. I should have asked." Porsche nodded. "Me too." Cassius put a hand over his heart. "I guessed. Poorly." Tank looked at them. Then at the paper.

"Niggas," he said, "y'all had my wedding vows on a karaoke screen." Nobody defended it. For a second the city hummed beneath us. Then Tank started laughing. Not because it had never mattered. Because it still mattered, and the three niggas in front of him knew that now, and nobody was asking him to pretend otherwise.

"I KNOW HOW TO STAY," he sang badly in Victor's dramatic voice. Porsche doubled over. Cassius covered his face. Victor looked mortified, then began laughing too. "The chord progression was tasteful," he said weakly. "Nigga, the chord progression was about to get punched." I laughed until my shoulder hurt. Rosa came out onto the terrace. She looked at the five of us.

"You finished?" Tank straightened. "Yeah." She held out her hand. "Good. I want to dance with the man from that song." He looked at me. I pointed toward her. "Nigga, furniture time. Go." He went. We watched them through the doors as she pulled him into the music. He put the folded paper safely inside his jacket before taking her other hand.

Victor leaned beside me at the railing. "You handled that well." "Nigga, you almost made my brother marry a bass line." He winced. "Yes." "Next time ask." "There will not be a next time." I looked at him. He considered our history. "Next time I will ask." "There you go. Growth." Inside, Shawna caught my eye and lifted her hand.

I left the niggas on the terrace and went to my wife. "Nice song," she said. "Freestyle." "I noticed. You mentioned yourself less than usual." "Nigga, it was difficult." She laughed and put her arms around me.

We danced while Tank and Rosa moved beside us, while the band played, while the city shone below a rooftop full of grown people telling outrageous stories and trying, in their own loud ways, to be good company for the people they had chosen. Later, Tank handed me the folded vows. I shook my head. "Keep them." He smiled.

"I got what I need." He tapped his chest once. Then he looked across the room at Rosa. Nigga, I believed him.

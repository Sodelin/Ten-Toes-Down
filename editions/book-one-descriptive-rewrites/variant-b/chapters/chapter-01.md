## 1. The Funeral Had a Two-Drink Minimum

“Nigga, it’s my motherfucking funeral.”

The ticket man kept his palm out. Forty dollars, according to the sign taped to his folding table. Two-drink minimum, according to the other sign. A little electric candle sweated orange light onto a bowl of breath mints. Behind him, my face stood six feet tall in a rented gold frame.

He finally looked up at me, then over at the photograph, comparing the merchandise.

“You look better in the picture.”

“I’m alive.”

“Then you can pay.”

This was Vegas after the homecoming. I had already been through enough to earn a quiet entrance into a building. Apparently the building disagreed. Warm air rolled out whenever the door opened, carrying butter, perfume, and the wet smell of flowers somebody had bought wholesale. Inside, a bass player was testing the same three notes. Outside, I was arguing with a nigga about whether death included parking.

They had used a photograph of me getting arrested. Somebody cropped out the cuffs, pasted angel wings behind my shoulders, and left the officer’s hand right where it was. A police escort into heaven. My expression said I wanted a lawyer. Beneath it, silver letters said REST EASY.

I called Tank. He answered with his mouth full.

“Why is there a cover charge at my funeral?”

“Shrimp was expensive,” he said.

“Why do I have shrimp?”

“You died successful.”

I could hear him chewing through the phone and, a fraction later, through the open door. My own best friend was thirty feet away, eating the evidence.

“Are you eating right now?”

“Grieving.”

I hung up and gave the ticket man two twenties. He pressed a damp stamp against the inside of my wrist. DECEASED, in purple block letters. The last D smeared when he let go.

“One well drink,” he told me.

I pointed at the minimum. “Your sign says two.”

“Second one’s your responsibility.”

Behind me, a man in a black shirt had stopped pretending to read the program. He looked from my face to the portrait, then took his phone out.

“Hold on. Ain’t that the deceased?”

The ticket man inspected my wrist. “Paid deceased.”

The man asked for a picture. I asked whether he meant a picture of me alive. He said people would think he knew me personally.

“You’re meeting me personally, nigga.”

“Yeah, but a funeral picture hit different.”

He put an arm around my shoulder before I could improve his understanding. Somewhere on that man’s phone is a picture of him smiling beside me while I hold a memorial program for myself. I remember thinking I should charge him. That’s how quickly Tank’s business model started working on me.

***

The room had probably hosted weddings before we got to it. You could see the pale squares on the carpet where the regular tables had stood, and a brass arch over the little stage had been wrapped in black cloth without anybody removing the white flowers beneath. Petals showed through wherever the fabric sagged. We had rented grief on top of somebody else’s happiness and put a bar between them.

The stage faced the entrance, a low platform reached by three shallow steps. My closed coffin stood on it just in front of the band, close enough to the steps that a speaker could rest a hand on the lid while addressing the room. It was polished dark enough to reflect the chandeliers. Tables crowded either side of a central aisle. To the left, waiters came and went through a swinging kitchen door with a round window. A mirrored wall ran along the right, doubling the candles and making the room look twice as expensive and four times harder to escape.

I found the chocolate fountain beside the kitchen and unfolded my program. The paper had a slick finish that stuck slightly to the stamp on my wrist.

“Why am I survived by eleven children?” I asked when Shawna reached me.

“Sponsors.” My wife leaned over the list. “Those are sponsors.”

“One of them’s Jeremiah.”

“Jeremiah’s Collision Repair.”

She wore black, the dress cut open along one leg, a diamond resting in the hollow of her throat. A small hat tilted over one eye. She had put care into looking like a widow who intended to remain expensive. When I slid my hand around her waist, the cloth was cool from the room’s air conditioning. Her back underneath it was warm.

“You enjoying widowhood?” I asked.

“The parking is excellent.”

I pulled her closer. She set two fingers against my mouth and looked past my shoulder before she spoke again.

“Eight minutes. Kitchen. Stay there until we get the case.”

Across the room, Mercedes waited beside the stage steps in a red suit. She had pressed her thumbnail into the middle of her other palm so long it left a white crescent. Every few seconds she stopped, flattened the hand against her thigh, then started again.

Silas Gold had stolen the original recordings of her first album. The masters. He had them on a hard drive in a silver case, and he was offering other people a chance to own the nights she had already lived. Mercedes could sing the words again. She could call musicians, find a room, pay for new microphones. She couldn’t rebuild the studio they had knocked down or make everybody walk into it at the same age with the same troubles. On the last chorus of one song, her voice had caught. She’d left the crack in. Said it sounded like her.

Silas intended to sell that too.

Most days he stayed behind bulletproof glass, spending other men’s knees and teeth. The news of my death had improved his interest in social occasions. Shawna promised him a private auction after the memorial: my watch, my cars, the collection. Bring the recordings and they could settle everything together. He would get to stand beside my coffin and shop through my life while my wife watched.

That was the bait. Mercedes would inspect the drive backstage. Porsche had an identical case ready to switch. I had the difficult assignment of remaining behind a door for eight minutes.

“I could take it when he gets here,” I told Shawna.

Her fingers left my mouth and closed around my wrist. “And his men could start shooting into everybody who paid to eat shrimp.”

I looked at the crowded tables. An older woman was cutting food for the man beside her. A waiter held a tray above a little knot of guests trying to squeeze chairs into an aisle that did not contain more aisle.

“Apparently excellent shrimp,” I said.

“Aiden.”

I let the joke die where it stood.

Mercedes came over and straightened my collar. Up close, I could see the pressure mark in her palm. Her hands were steady when she touched my shirt.

“Don’t do the thing,” she said.

“Which thing?”

She looked around my funeral. “The thing where this becomes about you.”

“My photograph is six feet tall.”

“Exactly. The nigga has enough publicity.”

I wanted to promise her the kind of rescue that made everybody cheer. She wanted her music back without having to watch it become a weapon in somebody else’s hand. Those were different wishes, and I could hear hers clearly enough when I stopped preparing my own entrance.

“You’ll get it,” I told her.

“I know.” She squeezed my shoulder. “I want to enjoy getting it.”

She went back to the stage. Shawna kissed me once, gently, and turned me toward the kitchen.

“Kitchen.”

“Y’all got one word for me tonight.”

She gave my back a final push. I went through the door into steam and hot metal.

***

The cook stood over a deep tray of potatoes, turning them with a spoon broad enough to bury a man. Sweat darkened his collar. Every time the swinging door opened, the music came in loud; when it closed, the kitchen fans swallowed the top half of it. There was a speaker above the service shelf so the staff could hear announcements. It mostly supplied reasons to hate announcements.

Tank took the microphone with a plate still in his hand.

“We gathered here because my brother would want these niggas supporting the bar.”

Through the round window, I saw glasses rise. Tank waited for them to settle, cleared his throat, and began describing what a generous man I had been. He said I’d given him the shirt off my back.

I leaned toward the service opening before I thought about it. “Police was looking for a man in a red shirt.”

The laugh reached the kitchen like a door kicked open. I nudged the swinging door with my shoulder, already smiling at the room. Shawna sat near the aisle with her back to me. She lifted one finger without turning around.

I backed into the potatoes.

“But my nigga came back,” Tank said, and this time his voice changed.

He told them how I came to the station. Told the officers Tank hadn’t done shit. Paid what needed paying. Sat for six hours on a hard bench and took him to breakfast when they let him out. I rested my palm against the kitchen door. On the other side, nobody moved enough to squeak a chair.

Then Tank sniffed into the microphone.

“And the nigga asked for his shirt.”

The drummer rolled them into the laugh. He had kept his sunglasses on through both prayers, but the motherfucker knew when to hit.

A boo came from near the entrance. I moved my face to the little window.

Silas Gold stood inside the doorway wearing white fur. Four men spread around him. The smallest carried the silver case by its handle, keeping it close to his leg. The biggest had to turn his shoulders to clear a chair. His neck was wider than my thigh. He had the blank, solemn look of a man waiting for somebody else to tell him what his body wanted.

Silas held up a bottle. “Miss the good part?”

Tank looked at the coffin. “Depends how this speech goes.”

Silas laughed, and his men joined him half a beat late. Their shoes left four dark tracks through spilled ice near the bar as they followed him down the aisle.

Shawna got up to meet him. He bent over her offered hand, trying to kiss it. She turned her fingers as he lowered his face. His mouth met his own thumb. Even through kitchen glass I enjoyed the precision.

“The recordings,” she said.

“After my tribute.”

“Mercedes inspects them first. We agreed.”

Silas opened his arm toward the crowd. “Can’t buy timing, darling.”

He took the microphone from Tank and put his bottle on my coffin. Tank picked it up and handed it back. Silas set it down again. Tank handed it back again. Neither spoke. The drummer tapped a small, patient tick against the rim of his snare.

“You got somewhere to be?” Silas asked Tank.

“Eventually, nigga.”

Silas faced the room and announced that he and I had differences. In the kitchen I mouthed, You stole my boat, motherfucker. Then he began speaking about appetite. About things I had left unfinished. He looked at Shawna when he mentioned people left unprovided for.

“I would never presume—”

“Then don’t,” my wife told him.

A woman slapped her table so hard a spoon jumped. “That nigga got instructions!”

Silas stood through the laughter with his teeth showing. He had wanted a dead man, a grateful widow, and a microphone. The room kept returning damaged versions of the order.

He crooked a finger at Mercedes.

She stayed by the steps.

The smallest man carried the case up the steps and held it open. Silas put his bottle on the coffin, took out the drive, and raised it where Mercedes could see. Then he waved the guard back down. The man returned to the foot of the steps with the empty case open in one hand. Silas kept the drive beside the microphone.

“A tribute,” he said. “Our little lady can sing. And apologize for some things she’s said about me.”

Mercedes approached the bottom step. “You promised an inspection.”

“Sing first.”

Behind him, the biggest man planted himself in the backstage doorway. Porsche stood beyond his shoulder with the duplicate case. He shifted his bulk half an inch, enough to shut off the passage without touching her.

Silas offered Mercedes the microphone, then pulled it out of reach.

“Ask nicely.”

I took off my jacket.

The cook looked up. His spoon stopped over the potatoes.

“You want me to hold that?”

“Please.”

He took it by the collar and hung it away from the stove. “You coming back?”

I looked through the window at the man standing beside my coffin.

“Apparently I do.”

***

I went out carrying a tray of shrimp. The tray was warm on the bottom, slick at one edge where butter had run beneath the paper. I took it because it was there, and because a man carrying refreshments can cover a surprising amount of ground before anybody decides he is the problem.

Shawna saw me in my shirtsleeves. Her eyes moved to Silas and then to the aisle. She slid her chair back far enough to clear my path. Across from her, Tank lowered his plate to the table with both hands.

A woman at the first table looked at me, then at the coffin.

“Lord.”

The man beside her crossed himself.

“Don’t do that,” she whispered. “He got shrimp.”

“Could still be spiritual.”

“Move your chair, nigga.”

He scraped it inward. I passed without losing one shrimp. Near the stage, the drummer held a stick above the snare and waited to find out what kind of entrance this was.

The smallest guard stood at the foot of the steps with the open case in his left hand. I offered him the tray. He took a shrimp with his right without looking all the way up.

“Sauce?” I asked.

He nodded. I tipped the tray toward the little cup. He dipped the shrimp and stepped back chewing. Above him, Silas heard my voice.

Silas turned so fast his fur collar slapped his mouth. Standing beside the coffin, he stared down at me. His jaw worked once with nothing coming out.

Forty dollars. Worth it.

“Nigga,” I said, “it’s my motherfucking funeral.”

He shoved the microphone into its stand and reached inside his coat.

I took the three shallow steps as his pistol cleared the fur and reached him beside the coffin. The tray hit underneath his wrist. The shot punched into the ceiling. His gun spun across the platform and skidded beneath the piano, and the room ducked all at once, flowers trembling above tables as people dragged one another down.

Plaster rained onto my neck. The trumpet player folded behind his chair. Tank caught two guests around the shoulders and took them behind a table, holding his drink above all three of their heads.

I closed my free hand in Silas’s coat.

“Everybody else brought flowers!”

He seized the bottle from the coffin and swung it into my shoulder. The glass broke against me with a thick crack. For a second I felt the blow without feeling the cut. Then heat spread down my arm and liquor ran cold into it. I lost my breath on a word that wasn’t fit for my memorial.

I hit him across the face with the tray.

Metal bent. Sauce struck the white fur. The drive jumped out of his other hand, turning once in the light. Mercedes caught it against her chest with both arms, stumbling backward into the stage rail.

“Got it!” she shouted.

The biggest guard came out of the backstage doorway. Porsche swung the duplicate case low across his advancing ankle. His shoe caught the handle; his body kept coming. He stumbled down the steps straight toward Shawna, who had lifted the chair she moved out of my way.

She met him with it.

The legs broke around his face. A tooth ticked across the floor like a dropped bead. He landed sitting among the splinters, one hand pressed to his mouth, blinking up at my wife.

“Stay,” she told him.

He discovered obedience right there.

In the mirrored wall, I saw another guard come around behind me. His gun hand rose in the reflection. Mercedes saw him from the stage. She jammed the recovered drive inside her jacket, caught the microphone stand in both hands, and swung it above me as I ducked.

The metal shaft hit his chest. He staggered into the drummer’s platform and fell through the kit. A cymbal folded under his shoulder. Its broken stand rang against the floor, and the bass drum rolled until a keyboard player stopped it with his shoe.

The drummer stood over the wreckage. He looked at the cymbal. Looked at the man trying to crawl out from beneath it.

“That’s vintage, nigga.”

Then he hit him with the stool.

Silas slipped out of the coat in my fist and bolted around the coffin toward the steps. I had fur, blood on my sleeve, and one ugly second of being a step behind. Tank got up from behind the table, bounded onto the low stage, and met him at the top. He caught Silas around the waist, swung open the coffin lid with his free hand, and tipped him into the satin.

The coffin rocked on its stand. Tank dropped the lid.

Something inside squealed.

Tank froze. “Shit.”

The lid flew up again.

A little man in a gold tracksuit sat upright beneath Silas. He wore my spare sunglasses crooked on his face, one lens fogged from lying in a closed box. Silas had landed across his legs. The man shoved both palms into Silas’s back and screamed at Tank.

“NIGGA, I TOLD YOU NINE-FIFTEEN!”

I looked at Tank. Tank found something urgent to study on his shoes.

“You hired a corpse?”

“Resurrection package,” he said.

I waited.

“It included the dance.”

“And fog,” the little man shouted, pointing over Silas’s shoulder.

Tank gestured at the white dust drifting through the stage lights. “We got atmosphere.”

“That’s ceiling.”

“Work with the venue, nigga.”

The man climbed out over Silas’s stomach, using him without apology for a step. He straightened his gold jacket and pulled both cuffs down to his wrists.

“I do the surprise. I do the dance. I leave. Ain’t nobody said wrestling.”

Silas’s fourth man had edged toward the entrance. Tank pointed at him.

“Sit your ass down. The nigga hasn’t danced.”

He sat at an empty table and put both hands flat on the cloth.

The smallest guard remained beside the steps, a shrimp tail in his right hand and the empty case in his left. He lowered the case to the floor as gently as if sound could convict him.

“I’m with catering,” he said.

From the kitchen, the cook answered, “No the fuck you are not.”

***

It took seven minutes to make the room resemble a party again. Tank and Porsche collected the guns. Silas’s men sat with their wrists tied behind them while waiters dragged broken chairs out of the aisle. Somebody went under the piano with a cloth to retrieve the first pistol. The bartender stood up from behind his counter, checked the bottles, and announced he was still open. That restored public confidence faster than anything I said.

Silas tried to rise out of the coffin. The hired corpse put a sneaker against his forehead and eased him back into the satin.

“Stop touching my face.”

“Stop being in my office, nigga.”

Mercedes went to the sound desk along the side wall. She took the drive out of her jacket, found the cable the technician offered, and plugged it in. Porsche stood beside her while the little screen filled with folders. Their shoulders touched. Under the edge of the table, Porsche reached for her hand.

I started toward them. Shawna hooked two fingers through my belt and held me where I was.

So I waited. My shoulder had begun to pulse. Every beat brought a small wet pull from the cloth stuck to the cut. Around me, people whispered over the scraped legs of tables, trying to decide which part of the evening they would tell first. Mercedes put on the headphones. She chose a file and closed her eyes.

We could hear nothing she heard.

Her mouth moved once, silently, keeping time with something that had existed before Silas put a price on it. Then she took the headphones off and wiped beneath one eye with the side of her finger.

“All here.”

Tank let his breath go. “Then play one, my nigga.”

Mercedes looked around the room. Crushed flowers lay near the stage steps. Two men were bleeding into napkins printed with my name. My huge portrait had slipped sideways against the wall, giving the whole mess a disapproving angel.

She walked to the stage.

The drummer was tightening a spare cymbal onto a stand. Mercedes asked whether he was good. He asked whether she planned to do something slow.

“No.”

He sat down. “Then yeah.”

She gave the sound technician a track number and pointed at my portrait.

“Turn that nigga around. I need the room.”

I turned it myself. The frame was heavier than it looked; lifting it tugged the cut open again. I put the smiling policeman and my borrowed wings against the wall and stepped back into the candlelight with the plain brown backing facing the guests.

A hiss came out of the speakers. Then saxophone, warm and rough, the recorded breath at the beginning of the note still there. The live bass found it. The drummer settled beneath both of them, and Mercedes sang low enough at first that the room had to stop talking to hear her.

She wasn't thanking anybody. She wasn't explaining what we'd done. She planted one heel against the monitor and let the next line rise out of her chest. By the chorus, people were standing in the spaces between fallen chairs, catching hands, turning sideways to make room.

The little man closed the coffin lid and climbed onto it.

Silas pounded underneath him. “Get off!”

“Nine-fifteen, bitch!”

Gold trousers flashed above the dark wood. The man danced hard enough to shake the flowers off one corner. Tank lifted both arms in approval.

“That nigga earned his deposit!”

Porsche laughed with her face tilted toward the stage. The cook came out carrying another tray, my jacket draped over his shoulder. A woman relieved him of the tray, put it on a table, and took both his hands.

“I’m working,” he told her.

“Not anymore.”

He looked toward the kitchen, where nobody volunteered to save him, and let himself be pulled into the music.

Shawna put her fingers into mine. She touched my sleeve with her other hand. I flinched before I could pretend it didn't hurt.

“You're bleeding.”

“Little bit.”

She looked at the dark patch spreading down my arm. “We'll get it looked at.”

“After the song.”

She moved to my good side and stayed there. On the last chorus, Mercedes's younger voice caught through the speakers. The little break she had refused to erase. Onstage, the woman she was now sang beside it, smiling as she met the same words. Porsche stopped laughing to listen.

Shawna leaned against my ear. “Eleven sponsors is a lot of mouths to feed.”

I laughed, hurt my shoulder, and held her closer anyway.

At the entrance, the ticket man opened the door to another group.

“Sixty dollars.”

I pointed at him across the room. “Nigga, it was forty!”

He nodded toward Mercedes, the man dancing on the coffin, and me standing beside my widow.

“Live show now.”

***

By the time the police took Silas away, the band wanted overtime and Tank was negotiating possession of the remaining shrimp. Officers led the guards down the central aisle past people who refused to stop eating for them. The little man stood beside the empty coffin counting his money. He had taken my sunglasses off at last. The skin beneath them carried two pale oval marks where he'd sweated through his resurrection.

I was sitting near the sound desk with a clean napkin held against my shoulder when the young technician brought over two glasses. He was about twenty-three, a little beard along his jaw, headphones resting around his neck. He kept looking at Shawna and then at the DECEASED stamp on my wrist.

“Can I ask something?”

“Apparently you can charge for it,” I told him.

He smiled, then asked how we met.

Shawna laughed before I got a word out.

“Let me tell it.”

“Baby, I was there.”

“So was I. One of us remembers who saved whom.”

The technician pulled a chair toward the table. Tank called for somebody to get him a plate. The young man said he was on the clock.

“So am I,” Tank said. “I organized a death.”

Mercedes brought a napkin. Porsche moved the bottle within reach. Tank dragged over another chair, its legs juddering across the place where somebody had spilled champagne. Shawna sat against my good side and took one of the clean glasses. The room was cooling now that the outer doors stood open. Candle flames leaned toward the night air. Under the tables, plaster dust had settled into the carpet between petals and broken ice.

I looked at the young man waiting for the story.

“First night in Vegas. You want the whole thing?”

He nodded.

Shawna settled in against me. “Start before you became reasonable.”

“We still waiting on that chapter, nigga.”

Tank laughed into his shrimp.

I took a drink. For a moment the liquor touched the same place at the back of my throat it had touched that first night. The room in front of me stayed full of people I loved, but I could remember the other wall shaking under the bass. The heat off the cars. Big T's hand heavy on my shoulder. Twelve men waiting by the doors, none of us knowing yet what the night was about to take.

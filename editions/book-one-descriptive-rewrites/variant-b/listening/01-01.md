## 1. The Funeral Had a Two-Drink Minimum

“Nigga, it’s my motherfucking funeral.”

The ticket man kept his palm out. Forty dollars, according to the sign taped to his folding table. Two-drink minimum, according to the other sign. A little electric candle sweated orange light onto a bowl of breath mints. Behind him, my face stood six feet tall in a rented gold frame.

He finally looked up at me, then over at the photograph, comparing the merchandise.

“You look better in the picture.”

“I’m alive.”

“Then you can pay.”

This was Vegas after the homecoming. I had already been through enough to earn a quiet entrance into a building. Apparently the building disagreed. Warm air rolled out whenever the door opened, carrying butter, perfume, and the wet smell of flowers somebody had bought wholesale. Inside, a bass player was testing the same three notes. Outside, I was arguing with a nigga about whether death included parking.

They had used a photograph of me getting arrested. Somebody cropped out the cuffs, pasted angel wings behind my shoulders, and left the officer’s hand right where it was. A police escort into heaven. My expression said I wanted a lawyer. Beneath it, silver letters said REST EASY.

I called Tank. He answered with his mouth full.

“Why is there a cover charge at my funeral?”

“Shrimp was expensive,” he said.

“Why do I have shrimp?”

“You died successful.”

I could hear him chewing through the phone and, a fraction later, through the open door. My own best friend was thirty feet away, eating the evidence.

“Are you eating right now?”

“Grieving.”

I hung up and gave the ticket man two twenties. He pressed a damp stamp against the inside of my wrist. DECEASED, in purple block letters. The last D smeared when he let go.

“One well drink,” he told me.

I pointed at the minimum. “Your sign says two.”

“Second one’s your responsibility.”

Behind me, a man in a black shirt had stopped pretending to read the program. He looked from my face to the portrait, then took his phone out.

“Hold on. Ain’t that the deceased?”

The ticket man inspected my wrist. “Paid deceased.”

The man asked for a picture. I asked whether he meant a picture of me alive. He said people would think he knew me personally.

“You’re meeting me personally, nigga.”

“Yeah, but a funeral picture hit different.”

He put an arm around my shoulder before I could improve his understanding. Somewhere on that man’s phone is a picture of him smiling beside me while I hold a memorial program for myself. I remember thinking I should charge him. That’s how quickly Tank’s business model started working on me.

***

The room had probably hosted weddings before we got to it. You could see the pale squares on the carpet where the regular tables had stood, and a brass arch over the little stage had been wrapped in black cloth without anybody removing the white flowers beneath. Petals showed through wherever the fabric sagged. We had rented grief on top of somebody else’s happiness and put a bar between them.

The stage faced the entrance, a low platform reached by three shallow steps. My closed coffin stood on it just in front of the band, close enough to the steps that a speaker could rest a hand on the lid while addressing the room. It was polished dark enough to reflect the chandeliers. Tables crowded either side of a central aisle. To the left, waiters came and went through a swinging kitchen door with a round window. A mirrored wall ran along the right, doubling the candles and making the room look twice as expensive and four times harder to escape.

I found the chocolate fountain beside the kitchen and unfolded my program. The paper had a slick finish that stuck slightly to the stamp on my wrist.

“Why am I survived by eleven children?” I asked when Shawna reached me.

“Sponsors.” My wife leaned over the list. “Those are sponsors.”

“One of them’s Jeremiah.”

“Jeremiah’s Collision Repair.”

She wore black, the dress cut open along one leg, a diamond resting in the hollow of her throat. A small hat tilted over one eye. She had put care into looking like a widow who intended to remain expensive. When I slid my hand around her waist, the cloth was cool from the room’s air conditioning. Her back underneath it was warm.

“You enjoying widowhood?” I asked.

“The parking is excellent.”

I pulled her closer. She set two fingers against my mouth and looked past my shoulder before she spoke again.

“Eight minutes. Kitchen. Stay there until we get the case.”

Across the room, Mercedes waited beside the stage steps in a red suit. She had pressed her thumbnail into the middle of her other palm so long it left a white crescent. Every few seconds she stopped, flattened the hand against her thigh, then started again.

Silas Gold had stolen the original recordings of her first album. The masters. He had them on a hard drive in a silver case, and he was offering other people a chance to own the nights she had already lived. Mercedes could sing the words again. She could call musicians, find a room, pay for new microphones. She couldn’t rebuild the studio they had knocked down or make everybody walk into it at the same age with the same troubles. On the last chorus of one song, her voice had caught. She’d left the crack in. Said it sounded like her.

Silas intended to sell that too.

Most days he stayed behind bulletproof glass, spending other men’s knees and teeth. The news of my death had improved his interest in social occasions. Shawna promised him a private auction after the memorial: my watch, my cars, the collection. Bring the recordings and they could settle everything together. He would get to stand beside my coffin and shop through my life while my wife watched.

That was the bait. Mercedes would inspect the drive backstage. Porsche had an identical case ready to switch. I had the difficult assignment of remaining behind a door for eight minutes.

“I could take it when he gets here,” I told Shawna.

Her fingers left my mouth and closed around my wrist. “And his men could start shooting into everybody who paid to eat shrimp.”

I looked at the crowded tables. An older woman was cutting food for the man beside her. A waiter held a tray above a little knot of guests trying to squeeze chairs into an aisle that did not contain more aisle.

“Apparently excellent shrimp,” I said.

“Aiden.”

I let the joke die where it stood.

Mercedes came over and straightened my collar. Up close, I could see the pressure mark in her palm. Her hands were steady when she touched my shirt.

“Don’t do the thing,” she said.

“Which thing?”

She looked around my funeral. “The thing where this becomes about you.”

“My photograph is six feet tall.”

“Exactly. The nigga has enough publicity.”

I wanted to promise her the kind of rescue that made everybody cheer. She wanted her music back without having to watch it become a weapon in somebody else’s hand. Those were different wishes, and I could hear hers clearly enough when I stopped preparing my own entrance.

“You’ll get it,” I told her.

“I know.” She squeezed my shoulder. “I want to enjoy getting it.”

She went back to the stage. Shawna kissed me once, gently, and turned me toward the kitchen.

“Kitchen.”

“Y’all got one word for me tonight.”

She gave my back a final push. I went through the door into steam and hot metal.

***

The cook stood over a deep tray of potatoes, turning them with a spoon broad enough to bury a man. Sweat darkened his collar. Every time the swinging door opened, the music came in loud; when it closed, the kitchen fans swallowed the top half of it. There was a speaker above the service shelf so the staff could hear announcements. It mostly supplied reasons to hate announcements.

Tank took the microphone with a plate still in his hand.

“We gathered here because my brother would want these niggas supporting the bar.”

Through the round window, I saw glasses rise. Tank waited for them to settle, cleared his throat, and began describing what a generous man I had been. He said I’d given him the shirt off my back.

I leaned toward the service opening before I thought about it. “Police was looking for a man in a red shirt.”

The laugh reached the kitchen like a door kicked open. I nudged the swinging door with my shoulder, already smiling at the room. Shawna sat near the aisle with her back to me. She lifted one finger without turning around.

I backed into the potatoes.

“But my nigga came back,” Tank said, and this time his voice changed.

He told them how I came to the station. Told the officers Tank hadn’t done shit. Paid what needed paying. Sat for six hours on a hard bench and took him to breakfast when they let him out. I rested my palm against the kitchen door. On the other side, nobody moved enough to squeak a chair.

Then Tank sniffed into the microphone.

“And the nigga asked for his shirt.”

The drummer rolled them into the laugh. He had kept his sunglasses on through both prayers, but the motherfucker knew when to hit.

A boo came from near the entrance. I moved my face to the little window.

Silas Gold stood inside the doorway wearing white fur. Four men spread around him. The smallest carried the silver case by its handle, keeping it close to his leg. The biggest had to turn his shoulders to clear a chair. His neck was wider than my thigh. He had the blank, solemn look of a man waiting for somebody else to tell him what his body wanted.

Silas held up a bottle. “Miss the good part?”

Tank looked at the coffin. “Depends how this speech goes.”

Silas laughed, and his men joined him half a beat late. Their shoes left four dark tracks through spilled ice near the bar as they followed him down the aisle.

Shawna got up to meet him. He bent over her offered hand, trying to kiss it. She turned her fingers as he lowered his face. His mouth met his own thumb. Even through kitchen glass I enjoyed the precision.

“The recordings,” she said.

“After my tribute.”

“Mercedes inspects them first. We agreed.”

Silas opened his arm toward the crowd. “Can’t buy timing, darling.”

He took the microphone from Tank and put his bottle on my coffin. Tank picked it up and handed it back. Silas set it down again. Tank handed it back again. Neither spoke. The drummer tapped a small, patient tick against the rim of his snare.

“You got somewhere to be?” Silas asked Tank.

“Eventually, nigga.”

Silas faced the room and announced that he and I had differences. In the kitchen I mouthed, You stole my boat, motherfucker. Then he began speaking about appetite. About things I had left unfinished. He looked at Shawna when he mentioned people left unprovided for.

“I would never presume—”

“Then don’t,” my wife told him.

A woman slapped her table so hard a spoon jumped. “That nigga got instructions!”

Silas stood through the laughter with his teeth showing. He had wanted a dead man, a grateful widow, and a microphone. The room kept returning damaged versions of the order.

He crooked a finger at Mercedes.

She stayed by the steps.

The smallest man carried the case up the steps and held it open. Silas put his bottle on the coffin, took out the drive, and raised it where Mercedes could see. Then he waved the guard back down. The man returned to the foot of the steps with the empty case open in one hand. Silas kept the drive beside the microphone.

“A tribute,” he said. “Our little lady can sing. And apologize for some things she’s said about me.”

Mercedes approached the bottom step. “You promised an inspection.”

“Sing first.”

Behind him, the biggest man planted himself in the backstage doorway. Porsche stood beyond his shoulder with the duplicate case. He shifted his bulk half an inch, enough to shut off the passage without touching her.

Silas offered Mercedes the microphone, then pulled it out of reach.

“Ask nicely.”

I took off my jacket.

The cook looked up. His spoon stopped over the potatoes.

“You want me to hold that?”

“Please.”

He took it by the collar and hung it away from the stove. “You coming back?”

I looked through the window at the man standing beside my coffin.

“Apparently I do.”

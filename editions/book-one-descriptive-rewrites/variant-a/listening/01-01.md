## 1. The Funeral Had a Two-Drink Minimum

“Nigga, it’s my motherfucking funeral.”

The ticket man sat behind a folding table with a cash box between his knees. Behind him, a pair of velvet curtains kept most of the light inside the ballroom, but the bass came through freely. I could feel it in the soles of my shoes. Somebody in there was enjoying my death with a very expensive sound system.

“Forty dollars,” he said.

I waited for him to recognize me. When that failed, I gave him the same information printed in gold across the memorial banner above his head.

“I’m Aiden.”

He looked up at me, then sideways at the six-foot photograph propped beside his table. Whoever had designed it had started with a picture of me getting arrested, cropped out the cuffs, and given me angel wings. They had left the cop’s hand on my shoulder. Apparently I needed a police escort into heaven.

The ticket man studied both versions. “You look better in the picture.”

“Nigga, I’m alive.”

“Then you can pay.” He tapped the cash box. The latch rattled. That was all the spiritual guidance he had for me.

This was Vegas, after we came home. By then I had survived enough shit to believe the city might give me a quiet evening out of professional respect. Instead, somebody had put a two-drink minimum on my own memorial. There are places where a man can come back from the dead and frighten people. In Vegas, they check whether resurrection counts as reentry.

I stepped out of the line and called Tank. Through the curtains I could hear a fork hitting a plate; through my phone I could hear the same fork, closer.

“Why there a cover charge at my funeral?”

“The shrimp was expensive,” my brother said. He sounded occupied.

“Why do I have shrimp?”

“You died successful.”

I watched a woman in black sequins squeeze past me with a man carrying her coat and both their drinks. She had already been inside. Her mouth shone with cocktail sauce.

“Nigga, are you eating?” I asked Tank.

He chewed once more before answering. “Grieving.”

I hung up and paid the forty. The ticket man took my wrist, pressed a rubber stamp against it, and released me with DECEASED drying above my watch.

“That gets you one well drink.”

I pointed at the sign. “It says two-drink minimum.”

“Second one’s your responsibility.”

A man waiting behind me leaned around my shoulder. He had been listening with the bright, hopeful expression of somebody discovering his evening might exceed the advertised value.

“Hold up. Ain’t that the deceased?”

The ticket man checked my stamp. “Paid deceased.”

The man brought out his phone and asked for a picture. He wanted people to think he had known me personally. I told him we were currently meeting personally, in real fucking time, with blood circulating through both of us. He considered that a weak promotional angle.

“Yeah,” he said, turning his phone upright, “but the funeral picture hit different.”

I left him posing beside the photograph and went through the curtains.

The room smelled of hot butter, flowers, and the sort of perfume that arrives before the person who bought it. Round tables filled the ballroom. A bar ran along the right wall beneath a mirror; at the far end, a low stage held a piano, two keyboards, drums, and enough brass instruments to announce an invasion. My coffin stood on a wheeled stand up on that platform, in front of the band. Two shallow steps at its left end led down to the main aisle. They had draped the coffin in flowers. They had also set a card on it reminding mourners to tip the staff.

For a moment I stopped beside the chocolate fountain and looked at what my friends had built. Not the decorations. The sight lines. Tank near the stage, able to reach either aisle. Porsche moving behind the musicians with her duplicate silver case. Mercedes standing still in a red suit, where nobody could mistake her for one more woman in mourning.

And Shawna, crossing the room toward me before I had finished finding her.

My wife wore black with a slit up one leg and a diamond at her throat. The brim of her hat shaded one eye. I had watched her put that hat on at home. She had tilted it, considered herself in the mirror, and tilted it farther. By the time she got to the funeral she looked like widowhood was something she could return if the service disappointed her.

I held up the program I had picked off a table. “Why this say I’m survived by eleven children?”

“Those are sponsors,” she told me.

“One of them’s Jeremiah.”

She put her finger beneath the rest of the line. “Jeremiah’s Collision Repair.”

I lowered the program and slipped a hand around her waist. “You enjoying widowhood?”

“The parking is excellent.”

She let me pull her close, then touched two fingers to my mouth. Her smile stayed on for the room. The pressure of those fingers told me the conversation had changed.

“Eight minutes. Kitchen. Stay there until we get the case.”

From where we stood I could see the kitchen door, left of the stage. It had a round window at face height. A second doorway led backstage, beyond the end of the piano. That was where Mercedes would inspect what Silas Gold had stolen, and where Porsche would make sure he left holding something much less valuable.

“Soon as he walks in, I could take it,” I said.

Shawna glanced across the tables. “And Silas’s men could start shooting into everybody who paid to eat shrimp.”

“Apparently excellent shrimp.”

“Aiden.”

That one word put my feet back under the plan.

Silas had the original recordings of Mercedes’s first album. A whole season of her life sat on a hard drive inside his silver case. The studio where she had made it was gone; some of the people who had played on those nights had scattered; even the girl she had been at the microphone could not be summoned back by telling her to sing it again. In the last chorus of one song, her voice had cracked. She had kept the take. Said it sounded like her.

When she told me about the theft, she did not cry. She described the files carefully, almost politely, and kept pressing her thumb into the middle of her palm. I noticed her doing it again that night beside the stage. A person can hold herself together so hard you start worrying about what it costs her hands.

Silas usually conducted business from behind bulletproof glass and sent other men out to make people miserable. My reported death had improved his confidence. Shawna had offered him a private auction after the memorial: my watch, my cars, parts of the collection. Bring the recordings, she had told him, and they could settle everything at once. The prospect of buying pieces of me had persuaded that motherfucker to leave his aquarium.

Our part was simple. Mercedes would verify the drive. Porsche would switch the case. I would remain in the kitchen, where my contribution to subtlety could be supervised by a man with potatoes.

Mercedes caught me watching her and came over to straighten my collar. She didn't need to straighten it. She needed to put her hands somewhere else for a moment.

“Don’t do the thing,” she said.

“Which thing?”

She looked from my face to the gigantic version by the entrance. “The thing where this becomes about you.”

“My photograph is six feet tall.”

“Exactly. The nigga has enough publicity.”

I let that one settle. Then I covered her hand where it rested on my shoulder.

“You’ll get your music.”

“I know,” she said. “I want to enjoy getting it.”

That was the part I had nearly run past. I could get so interested in fixing a hurt that I forgot the person carrying it might want to choose what the fixing felt like.

Mercedes went back to the stage. Shawna kissed me, turned me by the shoulders, and sent me toward the kitchen. I went. Some victories begin with a man accepting that his wife knows which fucking room he belongs in.

The cook gave me a place beside a steel prep table. Through the door’s round window I could see most of the stage, and an old speaker above the shelves brought in whatever the microphones caught. The cook kept working as Tank climbed the steps with a plate in one hand and took the microphone in the other.

“We gathered here because my brother would want these niggas supporting the bar.”

Glasses rose all over the ballroom. Tank waited for them to settle. He could take a room when he wanted it. People saw his size first and sometimes forgot there was a man in there watching what made them breathe.

“Aiden was generous,” he said. “Nigga gave me the shirt off his back.”

I leaned toward the little service microphone beside the speaker. “Police was looking for a man in a red shirt.”

Laughter came through the wall before the speaker could deliver it. I pushed the door open to appreciate my work, but Shawna raised one finger without even turning toward me. I retreated. The cook slid a tray of shrimp away from my elbow and carried on.

Tank waited out the laugh. Then his voice changed.

“But my nigga came back.”

I put my palm on the door and stayed there.

He told them about the station. How I had said he hadn't done shit, paid what needed paying, waited six hours, and bought him breakfast when they finally let him out. Hearing him tell it, I remembered the hard chair, the old coffee, and the way he had come through the door trying to act as if he hadn't spent the whole night wondering whether anybody would still be there.

Tank sniffed into the microphone. “Then the nigga asked for his shirt.”

The drummer rolled gently beneath the laugh. I liked him immediately. He had listened long enough to know where the weight was before he moved it.

The room had barely recovered when Silas arrived.

I saw the white fur first, passing between the tables. Four men followed him. The smallest carried the silver case against his thigh; the biggest walked with his elbows out because apparently being enormous hadn't provided sufficient obstruction. Silas carried a bottle and wore a grin he intended to put on somebody else’s loss.

He asked Tank whether he'd missed the good part. Tank looked at the closed coffin and told him it depended how the speech went.

Shawna met Silas at the stage steps. He bent over her offered hand, aiming his mouth at her knuckles. She shifted just enough for him to kiss his own thumb. From the kitchen, I admired the economy of it.

“The recordings,” she said.

Silas wanted to give his tribute first. Shawna reminded him that Mercedes was supposed to inspect them. He waved at the crowd as if the agreement had become unreasonable simply because he now had an audience.

He took the microphone from Tank and set his bottle on my coffin. Tank handed it back. Silas set it down again. Tank returned it again. The drummer watched the bottle travel and supplied a small tick each time it reached the other end.

“You got somewhere to be?” Silas asked him.

“Eventually,” Tank said.

Silas turned his back on him and began telling the room that he and I had possessed our differences. One difference was that I liked my boat and he had stolen it. He left that out. He spoke about my appetites, then looked down at Shawna and began suggesting that I had left people unprovided for.

“I would never presume—”

“Then don’t,” my wife said.

A woman near the front slapped her table. “That nigga got instructions!”

Silas waited for the laughter to die. His fingers tightened around the microphone until I could see the strain in his wrist through the kitchen window.

Then he called Mercedes up.

She stayed where she was until the small man opened the case. Silas set his bottle back on the coffin, took out the drive, and held it by one end. He knew what she was looking at. I could tell by the way he moved it a little whenever her eyes settled.

He offered her the microphone, then pulled it back.

Sing a tribute first, he said. Apologize for what she had said about him. Ask nicely.

Behind him, the largest guard stepped across the backstage doorway. Porsche stopped on the other side with the duplicate case. I saw her measure the space between his shoulder and the frame. There wasn't enough room to slip through. The inspection was gone; Silas had brought Mercedes to the edge of getting her life back so he could make her beg in front of everybody.

She put one foot on the bottom step. Stopped.

That is the moment I remember most clearly. Not the shooting that came later. Her hand opening at her side, empty, while he held the drive over her.

I took off my jacket.

The cook looked up. “You want me to hold that?”

“Please.”

He hung it away from the steam. “You coming back?”

I picked up the shrimp tray and checked the window one last time.

“Apparently I do.”

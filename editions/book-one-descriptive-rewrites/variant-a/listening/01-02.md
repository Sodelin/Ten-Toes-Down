I came through the kitchen door without hurrying. Shawna saw the tray in my hands, looked at the stage, and drew her chair back out of the aisle. Tank set down his plate. Nothing passed between us that needed to be shouted. We had learned each other by then, including the disappointing fact that my version of patience had an expiration date.

A woman at the aisle turned toward me and whispered, “Lord.”

Her companion began crossing himself. She caught his arm and pointed at the tray.

“Don’t do that. He got shrimp.”

“Could still be spiritual,” the man said.

“Move your chair, nigga.”

He moved it. I passed with the shrimp level. At the foot of the stage I stopped beside the man who had carried the case and offered him one. He accepted. I offered sauce. He dipped carefully. Until that instant, I think he had been having a manageable evening. I left him finishing his shrimp and climbed the two shallow steps, coming onto the platform beside Silas.

Silas heard my voice and spun around. The fur collar slapped him across the mouth. He stared at me, at the coffin, and back at me.

Forty dollars. Worth it.

“Nigga,” I told him, “it’s my motherfucking funeral.”

He jammed the microphone into its stand and reached inside his coat. I was close enough to see the pistol come clear and close enough to do something about it. I drove the edge of the tray beneath his wrist.

The shot went into the ceiling. The gun flew from his hand, hit the stage, and skidded under the piano. Plaster showered the musicians. The trumpet player bent over his instrument as if a note he had been saving might escape during the emergency.

People screamed and dropped between the tables. Tank got two guests behind one before either understood why his arms were around them. Across the aisle, Shawna stood with her chair held in front of her.

I caught Silas by the fur and dragged him away from the edge of the stage.

“Everybody else brought flowers!”

He snatched the bottle off the coffin and broke it against my shoulder.

Pain drove down my arm to the fingers. My grip loosened, but a fistful of fur stayed caught in my curled fingers. For a breath I couldn't tell whether the warmth beneath my shirt was liquor or blood. Then I could tell, and being able to tell did nothing to improve my mood.

I hit him with the tray.

His head went sideways. The drive flew from his other hand, and Mercedes stepped into its path, catching it against her chest with both palms.

“Got it!” she shouted, climbing the last step onto the platform with the drive clutched to her chest.

The large guard left the backstage doorway and hurried along the platform toward me. Porsche came through behind him with the duplicate case held by its handle. At the top of the steps he planted one foot to turn; she caught up and swung the case low into that ankle. His foot stopped and the rest of him continued down the steps, directly into the chair Shawna had raised.

Wood broke across his mouth. He landed sitting in the pieces, feeling his front teeth with a look of profound disappointment.

“Stay,” Shawna told him.

He considered her, then the remains of the chair. He stayed.

I still had Silas’s coat bunched in one hand. In the mirror behind the bar I saw another guard draw behind me. Mercedes saw him too. She tucked the drive inside her jacket, seized the microphone stand with both hands, and swung as I ducked.

The stand caught the man across the chest and drove him backward into the drum kit. Cymbals crashed. One folded against its mount. The drummer stood up, looked at the damage, and then looked down at the guard trying to disentangle himself.

“Nigga, that’s vintage.”

He picked up his stool and brought its padded seat down across the guard’s shoulder and back. The man collapsed over the bass drum with both arms around his head. That was his much closer experience of the rhythm section.

Tank left the guests behind their table and came back up the steps. Silas wriggled out of his fur and ran straight toward him in his shirtsleeves. Tank caught him around the waist. With his free hand, my brother flung the coffin open and tipped Silas inside.

The lid banged down. Something beneath it squealed.

Tank stopped moving.

I knew that look. It meant a detail had returned to him after the moment when the detail would have been fucking useful.

The lid flew up. A little man in a gold tracksuit sat upright underneath Silas. He wore my spare sunglasses. One lens had slid below his eye, and he looked at Tank with the rage of a professional whose working conditions had deteriorated without consultation.

“NIGGA, I TOLD YOU NINE-FIFTEEN!”

I let the fur coat fall out of my hand.

“You hired a corpse?”

Tank looked at his shoes. “Resurrection package.”

“Nigga.”

“It included the dance.”

“And fog,” the little man said, pointing at him from the coffin.

Tank pointed at the plaster dust drifting above the stage. “We got atmosphere.”

“That’s ceiling.”

“Work with the venue.”

The performer climbed over Silas’s stomach and brushed his tracksuit down. He had contracted to surprise, dance, and leave. Wrestling had not appeared in the agreement. He explained this at a volume that reached the back tables.

Silas’s fourth man began edging toward the exit. Tank pointed without turning.

“Sit your ass down. The nigga hasn’t danced.”

The man sat. The smallest guard, who had somehow retained both the empty case and his shrimp tail, lowered the case to the floor.

“I’m with catering.”

The cook leaned out of the kitchen.

“No the fuck you are not.”

It took seven minutes to collect the guns, tie up the men, and establish that the bar remained open. That last part did more for the room than any reassurance I could have offered. People lifted chairs, checked their friends, and began telling the people beside them what those same people had just seen.

Silas lay in the coffin with his coat folded beneath his head. Every time he tried to sit up, the performer eased him back with a gold sneaker against his forehead. Silas objected to having his face touched. The little man objected to having Silas in his office. Neither had much sympathy for the other’s position.

Mercedes carried the drive to the sound desk. A young technician made room and handed her headphones. I watched the screen fill with files, watched her choose one, and started toward her.

Shawna caught the back of my belt.

I stopped beside my wife. The cut in my shoulder had begun throbbing, but it was manageable if I held the arm still. Under the sound desk, Porsche found Mercedes’s hand. They stayed that way while she listened.

When Mercedes took the headphones off, she wiped beneath one eye with her knuckle.

“All here.”

Only then did Tank let out the breath he had been holding.

“Then play one, my nigga.”

Mercedes looked across the room. Flowers lay in the aisle. Two of Silas’s men were pressing memorial napkins against their faces. My photograph had tilted sideways against the entrance wall. There was very little dignity available, which may have been exactly what she needed.

She went to the stage and asked the drummer whether he was good.

He was fitting his spare cymbal. “You doing something slow?”

“No.”

He tightened the wing nut and sat down. “Then yeah.”

She gave the technician a track number. A hiss came through the speakers, followed by a saxophone recorded in a room that no longer existed. The band listened for a bar and joined it. Mercedes pointed across the ballroom at my portrait.

“Turn that nigga around. I need the room.”

I turned it myself.

She began low, almost talking to the first table. Then the bass came under her and she planted one heel against the monitor. Her next line rose until the back tables could feel it. People stood in the gaps left by fallen chairs. Hands reached for hands. The cook came out with my jacket over his shoulder and another tray of food; a woman relieved him of the tray, set it on a table, and pulled him into the dance before he could explain his employment.

Onstage, the little man closed the coffin and stepped onto it. Silas pounded beneath his shoes.

“Get off!”

The performer checked the time. “Nine-fifteen, bitch!”

Then he danced. Tank lifted both arms in vindication. That nigga had been wrong about nearly every practical arrangement that night, but he had booked a professional.

Shawna slipped her fingers into my good hand. She had noticed the blood at my sleeve. When she touched the shoulder, I winced, and she stopped pretending it was only a stain.

“We’ll get it looked at,” she said.

“After the song.”

She stood against my unhurt side and let me have that much.

The last chorus came around. Through the speakers, Mercedes’s younger voice caught for half a breath. There it was: the crack she had refused to erase. Onstage, the woman she had become sang beside it, smiling. Porsche stopped dancing to listen. I could see Mercedes watching her listen, and for once I had enough sense to leave the moment alone.

My wife leaned up to my ear. “Eleven sponsors is a lot of mouths to feed.”

I laughed, hurt my shoulder, and laughed again because she had known I would.

At the entrance, the ticket man pulled the curtain aside for another group.

“Sixty dollars.”

I pointed across the room. “Nigga, it was forty!”

He indicated Mercedes, the coffin dancer, and my bleeding, upright ass.

“Live show now.”

By the time the police took Silas away, the band had demanded overtime and Tank was negotiating for the leftover shrimp. We gathered near the sound desk because nobody wanted to be the first person to turn the evening back into an ordinary night.

The young technician brought two clean glasses. He was twenty-three, with a little beard and headphones still around his neck. His eyes kept going from Shawna to the DECEASED stamp on my wrist, trying to fit the marriage and the memorial into one sensible account.

He asked how we had met.

My wife laughed before I could answer. “Let me tell it.”

“Baby, I was there.”

“So was I. One of us remembers who saved whom.”

The young man pulled a chair close. Tank called for somebody to bring him a plate. When he protested that he was on the clock, my brother reminded him that he had personally organized a death and was also still working.

Mercedes handed the technician a napkin. Porsche moved the bottle within reach. Shawna settled against my good side, and the people who knew the story made themselves comfortable enough to hear it disputed.

I asked the young man if he wanted the whole thing. He nodded.

“Start before you became reasonable,” Shawna told me.

“We still waiting on that chapter, nigga,” I said.

Tank laughed into his shrimp. I took a drink and let the room grow quiet around the edges. The musicians were packing away their instruments. Someone was sweeping plaster off the stage. My wife’s knee rested against mine.

It had not begun with any of that. It had begun before these people were my people, before the ring, before I knew which hand Shawna reached with when she was frightened and refused to show it. Back then, I had thought arriving in a city meant finding somewhere to stand. I hadn't understood how many people might eventually be standing there with me.

The bass had been shaking a different wall that first night. Big T had his hand on my shoulder. Eleven other men waited beside the cars.

And I was still young enough to think I could see all the trouble in front of me.

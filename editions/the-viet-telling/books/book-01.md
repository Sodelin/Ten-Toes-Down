# Book I: The Price of Standing

## 1. Membership

The man who offered me a place in the syndicate had somebody else's tooth stuck to his cuff.

He saw me looking. He peeled it off and set it beside his cigarettes.

"We are discussing your future," he said.

Behind him, a man sat tied to a chair. The rope was good. The chair was cheap. Blood dripped from the man's chin onto his bare chest. He had been here long enough for one puddle to dry beneath another.

"Then look at me," I said.

Vinh raised his eyes.

I was twenty-four. My passport said Aiden Tran. My mother had named me Trần Minh An, and nobody in our house had ever needed my passport to know who I was. I had spent my first fourteen years in California and the next ten in Ho Chi Minh City. I understood every word spoken in that warehouse, including the ones the men at the door thought I hadn't heard.

They had been told to break my legs if I refused.

On the table lay a contract, a photograph of my father's repair shop, and a pistol. The contract interested me. The photograph told me what kind of man had written it.

"The Wharf Circle doesn't recruit," Vinh said. "People ask."

"I asked."

"People wait."

"I've finished waiting."

He pushed the contract toward me.

I let it stop against my wrist. He had moved the pistol with the same hand, drawing both objects into the light. There were men who needed an audience for every decision. Vinh had arranged three: the guards, the injured man, and me. I wondered which of us he expected to survive the lesson.

The Circle owned a transport company and held stakes in warehouses. Its men made money carrying cargo, arranging loans, running gambling rooms, and charging people to be left alone. When the legitimate businesses lost money, the other businesses collected it. Vinh managed collections. Above him sat a man called Bảo, whose photograph appeared at charity dinners.

I had brought them an offer. I would recover a stolen account archive. In exchange, I wanted a partnership in one of their yards, a vote at the table, and the right to settle the obligations attached to my name.

I had brought money too. They wanted the other thing more.

"Why us?" Vinh asked.

"Because you keep sending men to ask my neighbors the same question."

"You could have stayed away. Studied. Made yourself rich."

"I am rich."

He glanced at my shoes. Plain black, wet from the rain.

"You don't look it."

"You still thought the photograph would work."

One of the men behind me shifted his weight. I heard a jacket catch against the pistol at his hip.

Vinh smiled.

"A clever boy."

"A partner," I said. "If we finish talking."

He opened the folder beneath the contract and showed me what his people had learned. Two companies had licensed work I had done on scheduling problems. There were foreign payments and a photograph taken outside a university building in California. He had circled the money in red. He had not circled the dates.

"Your parents know about this?"

"The money? Yes."

"They still work."

"They like working."

"Your father likes changing tires."

"My father likes choosing what he does."

Vinh tapped the balance he had circled. "That's a beautiful freedom."

I looked at the man behind him. One of his toes was pressed hard against the floor. The rest of his foot trembled. He was trying to make a body that could no longer stand carry a little weight through the chair.

"You have something to say," Vinh observed.

"I'm listening."

He disliked that. People had listened to him carefully for years, but usually because he held something they wanted returned.

"You pay more than we ask," he said, "because you imagine that buys you the right to speak as if you already belong. You give away a little profit. You rescue a street. Then you tell yourself you've changed something."

"You haven't asked what I want to change."

"My nephew knows your street. The tailor. The woman who sells breakfast. The printing business upstairs from the shop. Those are small things. We don't have to make an enemy of you over small things."

I picked up the photograph.

Dad was visible through the open shutter, seated on a low stool with a wheel between his knees. Mom stood in the doorway holding a cup. The photographer had probably believed he was documenting something vulnerable.

I put the photograph down again.

"Your nephew came at four yesterday," I said. "He asked the tailor to pay a second time. She showed him the receipt your man had given her. He said the receipt only covered damage to the front window."

"Then there was a misunderstanding."

"He put his hand through the cloth she was sewing and tore it."

"Did he?"

"She has to replace the cloth. Her customer needs that dress on Saturday. The material is no longer available from the shop she bought it from. So now the job costs two days she hasn't got. You sent a man who thought he broke something worth the price of a window. He didn't know what he was touching."

Vinh watched my face while I spoke. He was searching for a place where anger would make the next movement obvious.

"You paid her," he said.

"I found the material."

"Of course you did."

"And your nephew returned this evening. That is why I'm here."

"He said you frightened him."

"He understood me."

The injured man moved in his chair. Vinh looked back, and the movement stopped. On the nearest wall, a fan turned without moving much air. It had the heavy, reluctant rhythm of a bearing that had needed attention for months. My father would have heard it from the door. He would have fixed it while somebody explained why it couldn't be fixed.

I had not told him where I was going.

At nineteen I had tried living alone in an apartment large enough to impress people I did not like. I had stayed six months. Then the building's owner asked whether I wanted the floor above it, and I understood that I had spent half a year hearing nobody move in the next room. I came home with my books and a machine too large for the stairs. Dad helped me carry it. Mom asked whether I intended to pay for the electricity.

People thought I lived over a repair shop because I hadn't made enough money to leave.

I had made enough money to know what leaving cost.

My wife had a more practical explanation.

"Your mother has the only kitchen you trust," Shawna said, "and your father is the only man who can tell you your haircut looks fucking stupid without starting an international incident."

We had married the previous year. Shawna Nguyễn made clothes for women who expected the room to change when they entered it. Her own fitting room was across the city, but she had turned half our apartment into a place for designs that arrived in her head after midnight. There were sketches on the dining table and expensive cloth under a sheet in the room I had once claimed for books. I had offered her a bigger building. She said she wanted customers before monuments.

She was in Singapore that night, arguing with a supplier whose sample silk had been better than the shipment he proposed to send. Before leaving, she asked what I intended to do while she was gone.

"I'm considering an investment."

"Does this investment have a normal fucking accountant?"

"I haven't met the accountant."

"That is the most expensive sentence you have said all month."

She kissed me, took the suitcase out of my hand, and told me to answer my phone.

Now the phone lay beside a contract Vinh had written in ink. Shawna had sent a photograph of six identical bolts of cloth and the message: One of these lying bastards is polyester. I will find him.

I turned the screen down before Vinh could read it. There were parts of my life he was never going to sit close enough to understand.

Vinh took out another sheet of paper. "Your share of the yard. Fifteen percent."

"Twenty-five."

"You won't manage it."

"I will."

"You won't know what you're managing."

"Then let me lose my investment."

"Bảo chooses the manager."

"Then he can choose me."

He laughed once, without much pleasure. "Do you know how many men have sat where you're sitting?"

"Does the chair remember?"

The guard nearest the door let out the start of a laugh. Vinh turned his head. The laugh disappeared into a cough.

I had not meant to amuse him. I didn't care whether fifty men had asked for the chair or five hundred. They weren't here. I was. People explained the past when they wanted it to do work they couldn't do themselves.

Vinh flattened the new page with his palm.

"Twenty. No authority over staffing. No right to sell without approval. Your vote concerns ordinary operations only."

"What happens when an ordinary operation needs someone beaten?"

"We have people for that."

"You brought one."

I looked at the man in the chair.

"He isn't staff," Vinh said.

"He was."

The room became still enough for me to hear water running in the gutter outside. The man in the chair had closed his eyes.

Vinh asked, "Who told you that?"

"He looks at you when a guard moves. He expected you to stop them. A stranger would look at the door."

Vinh reached for a cigarette. He didn't light it.

"You notice things."

"You made them easy to see."

"Can you notice what Bảo will do if you insult him in his own yard?"

"He can come here and find out."

"You don't get to summon him."

"Then stop asking me questions for him."

For a moment I thought he would reach for the gun. His fingers moved toward it and stopped. He had been told something about me beyond the money. He didn't know how much of it to believe.

Two weeks earlier, I had been at a private dinner with people who made a living moving things across long distances. Most of their business was honest. That did not make their judgment better. A man called Đức had spent the meal explaining why I should invest through him rather than own anything directly. When I refused, he said I was young enough to confuse intelligence with protection.

He sent two men to continue the conversation in the parking area. The first put a hand on my shoulder. The second tried to take my bag.

I left them seated beside their car with their shoes in their laps. I had not planned that part. One man's shoe came off when I put him down, and the other removed his without being asked. People sometimes made their own decisions about how to show they were finished.

I called Đức from the driver's seat and told him the cost of their hospital visit would be included in any future discussion. He apologized. By morning three strangers had asked to meet me.

One worked for the Circle.

Now Vinh said, "Twenty-five, subject to Bảo's signature."

"And the staffing."

"We discuss staffing after the archive."

"Put that in writing."

He drew a line through the paragraph and wrote beneath it. His handwriting was careful. That surprised me. I expected him to want every part of his life to happen quickly.

He turned the paper around.

I read it. Then I read the paragraph above it again.

"Attached obligations," I said.

"Standard wording."

"Whose standard?"

"You're buying into an operating concern. There are obligations."

"I didn't say there weren't. I asked whose."

Vinh laid the unlit cigarette beside the tooth.

"Recover the archive. It contains the list. After you recover it, we'll have something to discuss."

There was the answer he had wanted me to reach. He could offer me an empty chair all night, because he knew what was tied underneath it.

I should have left. I knew that even then. There was enough money in my accounts to move my parents somewhere safe, if they had agreed to go. Enough to open a hundred shops. Enough to hire lawyers and lose years proving that men who stood in doorways were standing in the wrong ones.

But I wanted the yard.

I wanted to walk into a room like this and decide how the conversation ended. I wanted the men who frightened our neighbors to remember my voice before they remembered their instructions. I could have called that protection and made myself sound better. It was power. Protection was one of the things I intended to do with it.

Vinh saw something of that in my face. His shoulders relaxed.

"Good," he said.

He gestured toward the chair.

"Finish him."

The bound man lifted his head. He had a narrow face and a torn right ear. His left hand was purple beneath the rope.

"What did he do?"

"Took money."

"How much?"

Vinh named a sum. I transferred it from my phone. His own phone sounded against the table.

"That settles the money," I said.

"You misunderstand the exercise."

"No. You want to find out whether I'll kill somebody when you ask. I'm finding out whether you can honor a payment."

The jacket behind me moved again.

I reached backward without looking. My hand closed around the man's wrist before his pistol cleared the cloth. I turned once. His elbow struck the steel door with a sound the room could not absorb. I took his gun and laid it beside Vinh's.

The other guard stayed still.

I kept the broken arm in my hand until Vinh looked at me. The guard had stopped trying to pull away. He was making a thin sound through his teeth.

"He needs a doctor," I said.

"Let him go."

"Tell him why."

Vinh stared at me. Behind his eyes, a decision changed shape.

"Go outside," he told the guard. "Someone will take you."

I released him. He moved with his injured arm held against his body and left the door open behind him.

Vinh looked at his phone.

"Cut him loose," he said.

I used the paper knife beside the contract. The bound man tried to stand. His knees failed, and I caught him under the shoulder.

"Name."

"Hậu."

"Can you walk?"

He looked at the door.

"Yes."

He was lying. I carried him out anyway.

At the threshold Vinh called after me.

"You have twenty-four hours. Bring the archive to our private yard. Then you sign."

"Put the photograph away."

"Or?"

I turned. I did not raise my voice.

"Or this is the last peaceful conversation you have with my family."

He picked up the photograph. He put it facedown.

Outside, rain struck the concrete hard enough to bounce. A container tractor moved behind the boundary wall, carrying someone else's ordinary cargo toward the port. Beyond the industrial roads the city was still awake. People were going to work. People were going home.

Hậu gripped my sleeve.

"You bought a dead man."

"Then stay alive until I get my money's worth."

I put him in the passenger seat and fastened the belt around him. His hands lay in his lap. I thought he had lost consciousness until he said, "They'll charge you for the chair."

"Let them."

"They charge for everything."

I looked at the warehouse in the mirror. Vinh stood in the doorway. He was holding the contract in one hand, protecting it from the rain with the other. I had left him my money and taken someone he had expected to kill.

He was trying to decide which of us had been cheated.

I took Hậu to a hospital. When the nurse asked whether I was family, I said I was responsible.

That was the first answer I gave that night which frightened me.

## 2. The Man in the Bed

The admitting nurse looked at the blood on my shirt and asked which of us was hurt.

"Him."

"That wasn't the question."

I looked down. There was a cut across my left palm where the paper knife had slipped against the rope. I had not noticed it in the warehouse.

"Mostly him."

She gave me a cloth and pointed to a chair. Two other people moved Hậu through the doors. He tried to lift his head when they took him away, and I caught the direction of his eyes. He was checking whether I would follow.

"I'm staying," I told him.

He disappeared behind a curtain.

The waiting area held a young man with a bandaged foot, a woman asleep upright with a helmet in her lap, and an old couple sharing a bag of small cakes. A television showed people moving their mouths over a strip of news. Nobody was watching it. The old man offered a cake to the woman with the helmet. She woke enough to shake her head, then slept again.

I took the chair nearest the doors. It was plastic, with a leg that had worn shorter than the others. I folded a receipt under it.

After a while the nurse came back with a form.

"He says he fell."

"He didn't."

"I can see that."

"Then write what you can see."

She looked at me for a long moment. "I will. I also need to know whether someone may come here looking for him."

"Yes."

She took the form back. Her expression did not change, but the order in which she did the next things did. She spoke to a colleague, made a call, and moved Hậu to another place. I liked her immediately.

"You should have told me first," she said when she returned.

"I didn't know whether telling you would get him turned away."

"You don't get to make that decision for me."

"You're right."

She waited, perhaps because men who said that often wanted credit for saying it.

I held out the completed form. "Where do I pay?"

"There. Then wash your hands properly."

I paid. I washed. I watched a little piece of somebody else's shirt disappear into the drain and thought of the twenty-four hours Vinh had offered me.

It was just after one. The appointment in the private yard was at midnight. He had given me a day in the way men like him gave anything: approximately, with the difference belonging to them.

I had work to do before morning, but I stayed until Hậu was awake enough to know where he was.

He lay with his right hand on the blanket, watching the door. The other hand had been wrapped and raised. His swollen face made it difficult to tell whether he was young. Thirty, perhaps. Older than me, though fear and exhaustion had temporarily made that fact irrelevant.

"They asked questions," he said.

"Hospitals do that."

"I told them I fell."

"You need a better staircase."

He looked at me, then made a sound that hurt him. I couldn't tell whether he had tried to laugh or cough.

"Don't do that," I said.

"Talk?"

"Whatever your ribs object to."

I brought a chair to the bedside. He watched my hands as I sat. People had touched him all night, and none of those touches had been his decision. I kept my hands on my own knees.

"Do you want someone called?"

"No."

"There's a name on your emergency form."

"My sister. Leave her alone."

"I meant to tell her you're here."

"That's not leaving her alone."

I nodded. He stared at me as if he expected a condition to follow.

"You don't work for me," I said.

"You paid for me."

"I paid Vinh to stop."

"Then you've got no idea what you paid for."

I looked at the bruises disappearing beneath the blanket. "Probably."

He turned his face away. A trolley passed outside, one wheel clicking at every turn. We listened to it until it stopped.

"I did take the money," he said.

"I assumed you might have."

"Don't make me into something else."

"I haven't made you anything."

"People do. They help you and decide they know why you needed it."

"Why did you?"

He did not answer immediately. I could see him choosing between the story he had told himself and the one that would survive being spoken aloud.

"There was a driver," he said at last. "He damaged a truck. The company took it from his pay. Then the company charged him because the truck couldn't work. Then it charged him for another truck to do the job. His wages couldn't reach the end of the first amount."

"You collected it."

Hậu closed his eyes.

"For months. At first he'd have tea ready when I came. He wanted me to sit. He thought if he could make it a conversation, I might hear what he was saying. After a while he stopped boiling the water."

"What changed?"

"His wife had a brother. The brother had a little workshop. He signed something to help. Then we had two men to collect from."

He said *we* quietly.

I waited.

"I took an envelope from a room I was allowed into. Enough to settle it, I thought. I entered a payment. Vinh checked who had authorized the payment."

"Did it settle the driver's debt?"

"No."

"Did you keep any?"

He looked back at me. "Some."

"How much?"

He told me. It was less than I expected and more than he wanted it to be.

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to leave. Because my sister's tired of telling people her brother works in transport. Because I thought, if I was already stealing, I might as well steal enough to get out. Take your pick."

"Which one did you tell yourself first?"

"The driver."

"And afterward?"

He looked at his bandaged hand. "Mostly I told myself Vinh wouldn't count until Friday."

I sat back. He had done something decent, something selfish, and something stupid in the same motion. That did not make him unusual. It made it harder to discuss him as an entry on Vinh's phone.

"What's the driver's name?"

He told me, then asked why I wanted it.

"Because when I ask you about him again, I don't want you to say 'the driver.'"

"You going to pay him too?"

"I don't know yet."

"You can afford it."

"That wasn't what I said."

He looked at me differently then. It wasn't gratitude. He was beginning to understand that the man who carried him out of the warehouse might still refuse what he asked for.

The nurse returned to check him. I went into the corridor. When I took out my phone, there were messages from two people who wanted mathematics and one who wanted to know whether I could be at a breakfast meeting. I declined the meeting. I sent one answer to a company whose engineers had been waiting all afternoon. The second answer would take longer. I told them to expect it tomorrow.

Vinh called before I put the phone away.

"How is our investment?"

"He has a name."

"I know it. I gave you the number beside it."

"What do you want?"

"To remind you that the clock runs while you play nurse."

"I remember."

"Kiệt is meeting another buyer. If the archive leaves with him, your offer expires."

"When?"

"You should have asked that before paying for a distraction."

He ended the call.

I held the phone away from my ear and looked at the dark screen. He thought urgency would make me obedient. It might have worked if he had given me an instruction instead of a reason to dislike him more.

At the end of the corridor, a man in a cream shirt spoke to the security desk. He held a motorcycle helmet by the strap. There was nothing unusual about him except that he had passed the payment counter without looking at it and was trying to see past the guard.

When he saw me, he stopped speaking.

I walked toward him.

"Who are you here for?"

"A relative."

"What's his name?"

"Who the fuck are you?"

"The person asking first."

The guard stood up. I showed him my empty hands and lowered my voice.

"We're going outside."

"I am not going anywhere with you," the man said.

I looked at the helmet. A strip of tape held one cracked edge together. There was blood in the stitching beneath the strap. He had come directly from the warehouse, perhaps carrying the guard whose arm I had broken.

"Vinh sent you to find Hậu. You found me. Come outside and make your call."

The guard asked whether he should call the police.

"If he tries to go down that corridor," I said.

The man swore at me, but he came.

Under the entrance canopy, rainwater fell from a broken gutter into a bucket. He stopped with one foot outside the light. He had the wary, resentful look of a man whose evening had become somebody else's mistake.

"Vinh said to get his phone."

"Hậu's?"

"Company property."

"Then send him a letter."

"You want to turn every fucking thing into a performance?"

"I'm standing in a car park. You're carrying a helmet like an argument. We can both improve."

He raised the helmet. I put one hand against it before he could bring it down.

For a moment he thought I was simply holding it. Then the shell folded under my palm. The crack widened, the lining pushed inward, and the strap slipped between his fingers. I set the ruined helmet on the low wall beside him.

He stared at it.

"That was already broken," I said. "So we're calling it a repair."

"What the fuck are you?"

"Busy. Tell Vinh he can speak to me at midnight. Tell him to send nobody else here."

"He'll ask why."

"Take the helmet."

He did. I watched him walk into the rain carrying it with both hands, as though it had become something dangerous after it stopped being useful.

When I returned, the security guard was waiting.

"He is not coming back," I said.

"You don't know that."

"No. I don't."

We looked at one another. He was older than my father, with tired skin beneath his eyes. He had no reason to believe me, and he had not been paid enough to absorb my confidence.

I gave him my number. "If he does, call the police. Then call me."

"I was going to do the first thing anyway."

"Good."

Hậu was awake when I went back. He had heard enough through the door to know what happened.

"He wanted my phone."

"Do you have it?"

"They took it before they tied me down."

I looked toward the corridor.

Hậu said, "He wanted to know where they put me."

I pulled the chair closer to the bed.

"Then he failed."

"You won't always be here."

"Neither will you."

We discussed somewhere he could go after discharge. He rejected the first suggestion because his sister knew the owner, the second because Vinh's cousin had used it, and the third because he couldn't afford it.

"I can," I said.

"That's how this starts."

He lifted his injured hand a little.

I stopped talking.

At school in California, I had once finished a problem so far ahead of the class that the teacher thought I had misunderstood the instructions. She gave me another. I finished that too. By the end of the lesson she was angry with me for something neither of us could explain. Afterward Dad said people sometimes heard *I can do it* as *you should have done it already*.

Hậu was hearing something else. He was hearing a price that hadn't been named.

"Write it down," I said.

"What?"

"What I give you. What you accept. What you don't owe. If you want it witnessed, we'll get someone to witness it."

"That's ridiculous."

"So is arguing about a bed while you're in one you didn't choose."

He studied me. "You really want into the Circle."

"Yes."

"Why? You're smarter than them."

"That's one reason."

"You're rich."

"We've covered that."

"They'll make you do things."

"They'll try."

He turned his head toward the ceiling and let out a slow breath. "That's how you sound before you find out there are people you can't frighten."

I thought of my father taking a jar from my hand after I had tightened the lid too hard. He had opened it without noticing he was doing anything.

"I've met one," I said.

Hậu didn't ask who. He was too tired to pursue it, and I was not ready to give him my family as a further subject for worry.

At four, the nurse said he needed sleep. I stood to leave. Hậu caught my sleeve with his good hand.

"Kiệt won't be where Vinh told you."

I waited.

"There's an old furniture showroom. They took everything out last month. He uses the upstairs room because people keep thinking an empty building is empty."

"How do you know?"

"I used to bring him money."

He gave me the address. Then he said there would be six men.

"Are you helping me because I paid?"

His hand tightened on my sleeve.

"I'm helping because you asked me what I took and didn't stop listening when I answered. Don't make it something cleaner."

"I won't."

Outside, the rain had thinned to a mist. I sat in the car and answered Shawna's photograph.

*Make him confess.*

She replied almost immediately.

*He did. I am merciful. He's replacing the shipment. Why are you awake?*

*The investment is complicated.*

*Aiden.*

I looked at her name until the phone rang in my hand.

"Are you hurt?" she asked.

"No."

"Is someone else hurt?"

"Yes."

Silence. Then: "I'm coming back tomorrow. You will explain it with your actual mouth, in a room, without saying 'technically' once."

"I can do that."

"You can do a lot of shit. Start choosing."

I promised to call after breakfast.

Then I drove to the furniture showroom.

## 3. Spring

The archive had been stolen by a broker named Kiệt. He wanted to sell it back to the Circle and sell it again to the men who wanted the Circle's yards. He had hired a crew to protect him while he discovered how much each betrayal was worth.

His address was a building awaiting demolition. The ground floor had once held a furniture showroom. Rectangles of clean wall marked the places where display cabinets had stood. Somebody had left a child's blue sandal in the entrance.

I picked it up and set it on a dry shelf.

There was a light upstairs. There were also two men beside a parked car, pretending to be interested in something on a phone. When I approached, one put the phone away too slowly. He wanted me to see the effort he was making to look unthreatening.

"Shop's closed," he said.

"I can see that."

"Then what are you doing here?"

"Buying something."

He looked at the bag in my hand. "Leave that."

"It contains the offer."

"We have to check you."

I held out my free arm. He hesitated before touching me. His partner stayed by the car with his right hand inside the open window.

"You have a gun?" the first man asked.

"No."

"Knife?"

"No."

"Then why the fuck are you smiling?"

"Your colleague is wearing a seat belt while he threatens me from a parked car."

The man at the window looked down. His partner turned just enough to check. I moved past them toward the entrance.

"I'm early," I said. "Tell Kiệt."

They let me climb the stairs. Halfway up, I heard the sharp click of a released seat belt.

Kiệt waited on the second floor with six men.

I saw five.

That mattered.

The room smelled of damp wood and old adhesive. A table stood beneath a light suspended from a beam. Four chairs faced it, only one occupied. Kiệt wore a blue shirt with the sleeves rolled carefully above his wrists. He had the clean fingernails of a man who never touched anything that could be carried for him.

"Vinh sent a student," he said.

"He sent an offer."

I put a bag on the table. Inside was the amount Kiệt had paid for the stolen drive, plus interest.

"You walk away," I said. "You keep your profit."

He did not open the bag.

"And when he comes for me afterward?"

"He comes through me."

One of the men laughed. Kiệt didn't.

He had seen the footage of the warehouse door. Somebody inside the Circle had sent it to him. That told me how much my partnership was already worth.

"You think your word is enough?"

"I brought money because I knew you wouldn't."

Kiệt drew the bag toward him but still did not open it. "Who told you what I paid?"

"Does it cover it?"

"That's not an answer."

"Neither was your question."

He looked past me at the stairs. The smallest of his men stood there holding a length of black pipe. His fingers were white around it. The other men kept more comfortable positions. He was either the newest or the only one with enough imagination to consider what might happen.

"You're buying a drive," Kiệt said. "I'm selling a future."

"Then you're charging by the hour."

"I could sell it to Phước."

I knew the name. People in transport spoke it with the particular irritation reserved for someone who could make a delay more expensive.

"You could."

"He has businesses that don't depend on Bảo's permission. He has people who answer when he calls. He has lawyers who can speak politely for three hours without saying what a thing costs."

"Sounds exhausting."

"It sounds like somebody who'll still be here next month."

"Do you plan to be here next month?"

For the first time he looked angry.

"This isn't a chase."

"Then stop naming the people you might run to."

One of the men near the window moved. Kiệt held up his hand without looking. The man stopped.

"I ran a claims business," he said. "Before I did this. You know what that is?"

"I can work it out."

"I helped people recover money for cargo that had been damaged. I knew which forms belonged in which office. I knew when a company would pay and when it would lose the claim for six months until someone stopped asking. I did honest work."

"You say that like I argued."

"Bảo bought a share. A small share. Then one of his clients needed a claim settled quickly. Then another needed the documents changed. Then a third couldn't produce the cargo he said had been damaged."

Kiệt opened the bag at last. He looked at the money without touching it.

"I kept telling myself that after the next payment I could buy him out. Then I woke up and discovered I couldn't sell my own fucking desk without his approval."

"So you took the archive."

"I took something he couldn't replace."

"Can he replace you?"

Kiệt closed the bag.

"That's why there are six men here."

I looked around the room again. Five men. One beyond the thin partition on my left. A floorboard pressed against a nail when he moved. I had heard it twice.

"What happens to the people in the archive?" I asked.

"That depends who buys it."

"Do you care?"

"Do you?"

I didn't answer. He smiled at the first thing he thought we shared.

"You want a yard. He wants his money. Phước wants both. I'm the only man here being honest about selling something."

"Then sell it."

He studied me. Then he nodded toward a gray laptop.

"Open it. Show me what you've bought."

The archive was locked. I sat down.

"They said you do computers."

"They said less than they knew."

At twenty-one I had proved P equaled NP. The proof filled nineteen pages. Making it fast enough to use had taken another year. Then I had built the machine in my bag.

This one contained the particular obstacle my machine had been designed to remove.

I connected it. A small white progress bar appeared. There were no scrolling skulls, no warnings turning red. The fan started. Kiệt watched the bar as if it were a man approaching with a weapon.

"How long?"

"For the drive? Less than a minute."

"What mathematics?"

"I solved P versus NP."

He looked from the machine to my face.

"For this?"

"No. This is what I'm doing with it tonight."

"How long did that take?"

"Three years."

"You solved the thing with the prize?"

"Yes."

"And you're sitting in this fucking room?"

"You sent the invitation."

He stood up. The chair legs scraped across the floor. It was the first unplanned sound he had made since I entered.

"Do you have any idea what you could charge for that?"

"I have a very good idea."

"Then why would you take Bảo's—"

He stopped himself and looked at the door.

The sixth man fired through the plaster behind me.

I had heard the weight settle before the shot. His boot pressed a loose board against a nail. I moved with the noise, throwing my right foot under the chair.

The floor was wet. My shoe slipped six inches farther than I intended. My knee folded. For an instant the tendons behind it carried more force than they should have survived.

I had meant to leave the chair.

I rose eight feet.

The bullet tore through the place my throat had been. My hand closed around an exposed ceiling beam. The chair spun underneath me. I saw the five men looking upward and the sixth man pushing through the thin partition with his pistol still extended.

I understood the mistake while I was hanging there.

Spring. Too much compression. The wrong amount of floor.

For one clear instant I was above every decision in the room. I could see the man with the pipe taking a backward step. I could see Kiệt reaching for the laptop rather than the money. I could see the sixth man's wrist turn toward me. The face beneath it was almost calm. He had fired at people before and expected the world to behave as it had on those occasions.

I let go.

My heel hit the nearest gunman's collarbone. It broke beneath my weight. Before his gun touched the floor I had driven him into the second man. The third fired, deafening us all. Dust came off the beam into my eyes.

I closed them.

I had already seen the room.

Three steps, a table edge under my left hand, the nearest breath on my right. I struck once and felt a jaw move where it should have held. A blade opened my forearm. I caught the cutting wrist and took the man down against the concrete.

My knee sent a bright pain up through my body. I put weight on the other leg. The man under me tried to pull his arm free. I let him turn far enough to see his own hand, then pressed it against the floor until he stopped.

"Stay there."

He stayed.

Something hit my back. The pipe. The smallest man had found a reason to stop retreating.

I turned and caught it on the second swing. He tried to keep hold of it. For half a second we looked at one another across the length of metal, and I watched him understand the difference between what he had begun and what he could finish.

"Let go," I said.

He did.

I pushed him toward the stairs. He fell to his knees, recovered, and ran down them so fast his shoes missed two steps.

When I could see clearly again, the sixth man had Kiệt by the throat.

"Back off."

His pistol pressed into Kiệt's cheek.

I stopped. My pulse was steady. Blood ran over my fingers and fell from the smallest one.

"Who's paying you?" I asked.

The man said, "Put the pipe down."

"He hired you to protect him."

"He hired the others."

Kiệt made a sound through the arm around his neck. I could see his eyes moving toward the laptop. Even now, some part of him wanted to know whether the sale had completed.

The laptop chimed.

The man with the gun looked at it.

People who survive tell you they knew exactly what would happen. I had no such knowledge. I knew the loose partition was leaning. I knew the man had one ankle in the broken frame. I knew Kiệt's body blocked most of the shot he wanted.

I kicked the base of the partition.

It folded across his arm. His first shot went into the ceiling. I crossed the room before he could make the second. His hand struck the floor. I took the pistol, moved Kiệt behind me, and hit the gunman once with the heel of my other hand.

He lay still. He was breathing.

Kiệt crawled away. His face was wet with somebody else's blood.

"You jumped to the fucking ceiling."

"Move away from the guns."

"How?"

"Spring."

He stared at me. "That's a season."

"It's also the reason you're alive. Pick whichever definition helps."

I lifted the laptop onto the remaining table. The first opened file was a ledger. The second was a set of orders scheduled for midnight.

My name appeared in both.

Hậu's appeared beneath mine.

Vinh had entered the money I paid for him as the purchase of a worker. He had transferred the obligation to my account. There were forty-three other names in the same package, each with a photograph, a balance, and a person who could be hurt if the balance wasn't paid.

The partnership was real. So was the inheritance they meant to put inside it.

For several seconds I heard nothing except the fan inside the machine and the man on the floor trying to swallow. Then I began reading again.

There was the driver Hậu had told me about. There was his wife's brother. There was a woman whose name had been added because she owned part of the house where her son lived. Some balances were older than the children listed beneath them. A few were marked settled, but the people remained attached to future obligations in sentences written so casually they might have concerned spare tires.

Kiệt pushed himself onto a chair that still stood.

"Now you know what he bought," he said.

I looked at him.

"You mean what he sold."

"Same sheet of paper."

I went across the room and caught his shirt before he could move. I lifted him until the chair stood empty beneath him.

"Don't say that again."

He gripped my wrist with both hands. His feet moved without reaching the floor.

"I didn't write it."

"You brought it to an auction."

"And you're buying it."

I held him there. He could see that I was thinking. That frightened him more than the effortlessness with which I had lifted him.

He was right about the part I didn't want him to say. I had come to acquire a place in the Circle. I had negotiated for staffing authority. I had demanded to see the obligations because I wanted to know whether the investment was acceptable. The people had entered my arithmetic before they entered my conscience.

I lowered him.

"Get an ambulance for them," I said.

"What are you going to do?"

"You can ask after you make the call."

He called. He gave the address accurately. I listened until I knew he had.

My phone rang.

"Have you opened it?" Vinh asked.

I watched my blood move across the trackpad.

"Yes."

"Now you understand what a seat costs."

"I understand your accounting."

"Bring it. Midnight."

He ended the call.

Kiệt had stopped watching the men on the floor. He was looking at the screen.

"If you take that home," he said, "everyone there dies."

I closed the laptop.

"I'm going to dinner."

"Did you hear me?"

"Yes."

"Then why—"

"Because my parents live there. Because my wife expects me to eat a meal while she's away instead of calling coffee a food group. Because you don't get to frighten me out of my own fucking house."

Kiệt looked at my injured arm.

"I wasn't trying to frighten you."

"Then stop practicing."

I took the drive and my machine. I left the money where I had placed it. When Kiệt saw that, he reached out as if he might stop me.

"The deal still stands?"

"You keep your profit. You release what I ask you to release. And if Vinh comes for you, you call me before you try to buy six more men."

His eyes went to the broken partition.

"Five," he said.

"Learn to count everyone in the room."

Outside, morning had begun to turn the sky pale. The men beside the car were gone. The blue sandal remained on its shelf.

I stood on the pavement until my knee could carry me again. Then I called Hậu and told him I had found the driver.

## 4. Daylight Price

Dinner was twelve hours away.

Before then I needed a clean shirt, a doctor, a conversation with forty-four people I had never met, and an explanation for the second man who called while I was still parked outside the showroom.

"You opened it," he said.

"Who is this?"

"Phước."

He let the name sit between us. It had probably opened enough doors that he thought I should be able to hear the hinges.

"Kiệt has a fast phone," I said.

"He has several. They're his greatest vulnerability."

"I'm starting to think it's the people who answer."

Phước laughed. It sounded unforced, which made me listen more carefully.

"Come and have breakfast."

"I've already made promises about breakfast."

"Then lunch. You are entering a business with more than one owner. It would be a courtesy to meet the others."

"You own part of the Circle?"

"I own things it needs."

"That describes a fuel station."

"Come at eleven. You can decide what describes me."

He sent an address after ending the call. The building stood in an ordinary commercial street, far from the empty rooms where Kiệt conducted his version of business.

I drove to a place that served breakfast early and sat where I could keep my leg straight. The woman taking orders looked at the towel around my arm.

"Hospital," she said.

"Next."

"You people always say next."

"What people?"

"Men bleeding on my chairs."

She brought my food, put an extra stack of napkins beside it, and charged me for none of her advice. I ate slowly because my hands were beginning to feel the night. The coffee tasted better than anything I had drunk at meetings where men discussed what experience was worth.

Shawna called while I was finishing.

"Show me the meal."

I turned the camera.

"That looks like a meal someone else has finished."

"I finished it."

"Show me your face."

I did.

She was in a bright room with a rack of sample clothes behind her. Her hair was tied back. I could tell she had already frightened somebody that morning because she looked cheerful in the precise way she did after winning an argument she shouldn't have needed to have.

"You look like somebody attempted to mail you without a box."

"Long night."

"Your investment?"

"Yes."

"You have actual employees who can conduct actual meetings."

"Not this one."

Her expression changed.

"Then tell me enough that I don't have to invent something worse."

I told her about Hậu. I told her there had been men at the building. I said I had the thing I went there to collect. I did not tell her about the photograph of the shop until she asked why the Circle had come to our neighborhood.

For several seconds she said nothing.

"Did they photograph my place?"

"Not that I've seen."

"That isn't no."

"No. It isn't."

She walked away from the rack. I watched her stop beside a window and put one hand flat against its frame.

"You do not get to decide I'm safer because I'm on a plane."

"I haven't decided that."

"You decide things before you say them. Then you act surprised when someone notices."

I looked at the towel around my arm.

"I wanted you to finish your work."

"I will finish my fucking work. I'm good at it. That doesn't require you to hide yours."

"You're right."

"Stop saying that like you're returning a serve. Tell me what happens next."

I told her about the midnight meeting.

She listened without interrupting. When I finished, she asked whether Dad knew.

"Tonight."

"Good. And a doctor. Now. Not after you've purchased a war and discovered the warranty doesn't cover your stupid arm."

I promised.

At a clinic I let a doctor examine the cut and wrap it properly. She wanted me to remain while they dealt with it fully. I told her I had another appointment and received a look almost identical to Shawna's. I agreed to return. It was a poor compromise, and she made sure I knew she considered it one.

By eleven the heat had found every place the rain had left damp. I parked near Phước's address and walked the last block. My knee loosened as I moved. It also hurt more. Both facts seemed important, so I kept them separately in my head.

Phước's office occupied the upper floor of a building that sold imported furniture. The showroom below contained tables nobody was meant to eat at and chairs positioned too far apart for conversation. A young woman at the desk wore an expression suggesting that all visitors had disappointed her before arriving.

"Mr. Phước is expecting me."

"Name?"

"Aiden."

She looked up.

"You have something on your cheek."

I touched it. Plaster dust, held in place by dried sweat.

She offered a tissue. "He's upstairs."

At the top of the stairs a broad man asked for my bag. I looked at his hands, then at the narrow door behind him.

"You can ask him whether that's necessary."

"It is."

"Then you should ask him whether it's worth replacing this door."

The man studied me. From inside the office, someone said, "Let him bring it, Thành."

The broad man moved aside.

Phước stood by a window holding a small cup. He was perhaps fifty, with good posture and a face that seemed accustomed to being photographed from its better side. There were no guns on his desk. There were two telephones, a bowl of mandarins, and a framed drawing made by a child.

A woman sat at the other end of the desk with a folder open before her. She wore square glasses and had tucked a pencil through her tied hair.

"Vân," Phước said, indicating her. "She understands the yard better than the people who own it."

"I keep its accounts," she said. "Understanding would require them to tell me what they do."

I sat opposite her.

Phước offered tea. I accepted. Vân watched which hand I used for the cup.

"Bad morning?" she asked.

"Educational."

"Those cost more."

Phước smiled at both of us. "You see why I asked her here."

"Because she can speak in complete sentences?" I said.

"Because you need to know what you are buying."

He sat and opened his own folder.

There were diagrams of the yard, lists of equipment, employment figures, outstanding invoices. Vân corrected three numbers without being asked. Phước accepted each correction as though it demonstrated the quality of the service he was providing.

"The yard earns money," he said. "It could earn more. Bảo has neglected it while expanding other concerns."

"You mean the gambling rooms."

"Among other things."

"Why doesn't he sell it?"

"Because an asset is useful even when you run it badly."

Vân looked down at the papers. It was a small movement, but Phước noticed it and went on more carefully.

"There are personnel arrangements that complicate a clean transfer."

"Forty-four," I said.

Vân raised her eyes.

Phước did not. "There are ways to settle those."

"I have the total."

"Then you know it is manageable."

"For me."

"For the business."

"Let's keep the nouns where you put them."

He closed his folder, gently.

"You dislike the arrangement. Good. Change it. Take the obligation, settle the balance, and release the people. You'll own a productive company and have the gratitude of its employees."

"Will I need to buy them again next month?"

"That would be an unusual interpretation of—"

"Will Bảo stop acquiring people when he learns there's a buyer who pays full price to feel better?"

Phước looked at Vân. She didn't help him.

"You are young," he said to me. "You still expect a transaction to improve the entire world around it."

"I expect to know what I'm paying for."

"Then pay for control. You cannot control what belongs to someone else."

I set down the cup.

"Who owns the land?"

The question changed something in his face. For the first time he looked at me as though he had been asked about his own business.

"A holding concern."

"Whose?"

"Several parties."

"One of them you."

"One of them me."

Vân watched us over the top of her glasses.

"Then the fuel station sells the road too," I said.

Phước laughed again. This time there was work in it.

"I like you."

"You don't know me."

"I like what I know."

He opened a drawer and took out a single sheet. It was an offer to fund part of the acquisition. The rate looked generous. The language beneath it did not.

I read it twice, then gave it back.

"No."

"You haven't heard the advantages."

"I've read the ownership clause."

"Security."

"Yours."

He put the sheet away.

Outside the window, somebody shouted in the courtyard behind the furniture building. Vân turned toward the sound. Phước did not.

Another shout. Then a dull impact.

I stood up.

"A delivery dispute," Phước said.

"Then it needs a signature."

I went to the window.

Below us Thành held a younger man against the side of a small truck. Two cartons lay open on the paving. One contained broken glass. The younger man's shirt had torn at the shoulder, and he kept trying to point toward a woman standing beside the loading door.

"He didn't unload it," she said. "I'm telling you, he was still inside."

Thành struck him in the stomach.

I turned from the window.

Phước remained seated. "Do not misunderstand hospitality."

"Tell him to stop."

"He is handling a matter for the tenant."

"Tell him."

"And if I don't?"

I went through the door.

The stairwell was empty. I didn't use the last flight. I put a hand on the railing and stepped across the open space, dropping to the courtyard with enough control that the impact only cracked one tile.

Thành heard it and turned. The driver slid down the side of the truck.

"Leave him," I said.

"This has nothing to do with you."

"It does now."

He came at me with the practiced irritation of a man dealing with an interruption. He had heavy shoulders and a good reach. I could see why people would prefer paying him to discovering what happened when they refused.

I caught his fist and moved it aside. He tried to bring his knee up. I stepped close enough to deny it room and put him against the railing by the loading door.

The metal bent behind him.

He froze.

I moved my hand from his wrist to the rail and pulled the bar across his chest. It folded until it held him there, pinning both upper arms against his sides. His shoes scraped the ground as he tried to make space that no longer existed.

"Stop," I said.

He stopped.

The woman by the door was staring. So was the driver. Above us, Vân had appeared at the window. Phước stood beside her.

"You can have him back when he remembers why there are witnesses," I called.

Phước looked down without answering.

I crouched beside the driver. "Can you stand?"

He nodded. I helped him up. The woman brought his papers from inside the building and pointed to the place where another man's name had been written for the delivery inspection.

"He knew," she said, looking at Thành. "I told him."

The driver wanted to leave so badly that he could barely read the paper. I waited until he had it in his hand.

"Go," I said.

"The truck—"

"Take it."

He looked at Phước in the window.

"Go," Phước said.

The driver drove away. The woman went inside and closed the loading door. Thành remained attached to the railing, breathing carefully.

Vân came down first. She stopped beside the bent bar and examined it without touching it.

"He'll remember that," she said.

"He should."

"I meant Phước."

"So did I."

She almost smiled. Then she handed me a card with her number written on the back.

"You want the real accounts, call me. The real ones are mostly boring. That is how you'll know they're real."

Phước appeared in the doorway above the courtyard. He had brought my bag himself.

"You forgot this."

I took it.

"You move quickly," he said.

"Your man wasn't."

"You cannot run a company by arriving everywhere at once."

"You cannot run one well by pretending you aren't there."

He considered me for a moment. Then he looked at Thành.

"Can you release him?"

I pulled the bar back. Thành stumbled free, caught himself against the wall, and kept his eyes away from mine.

Phước said, "We will speak again when you have your chair."

"You sound certain I'll get it."

"I'm certain Bảo shouldn't have shown you the price."

I left him in the courtyard with the broken railing. On the street, the morning traffic had become midday traffic without needing our permission. A woman guided a child past the parked trucks. A man carrying two cups of coffee stopped to let them through.

Vân called before I reached my car.

"Don't sign anything Bảo calls standard," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"And the workers aren't all waiting for you to save them. Some will want you gone. Some will think you made it worse."

"Which are you?"

"I'm the one who knows how much is left for wages."

"Is there enough?"

"If people stop taking it."

I put her number into my phone.

## 5. The Wrong Passenger

Hậu was sitting upright when I returned to the hospital. Someone had brought him a clean shirt. He had managed two buttons and abandoned the rest.

"Who came?" I asked.

"My sister sent it."

"I thought you didn't want her called."

"I called her. That's different."

"It is."

He watched me put a bag of food on the table beside the bed.

"You're bleeding again."

"I noticed."

"Did Kiệt do that?"

"One of his appointments."

"He collects those."

I helped with the remaining buttons after asking whether he wanted help. He made me wait long enough to demonstrate that he had heard the question properly. Then he lifted his chin and let me work.

The shirt was cheap but carefully washed. There was a small repair inside the collar, where a seam had begun to split. I thought of Shawna teaching one of her assistants to finish a place a customer might never see.

"The inside is still part of the dress," she had said. "If you charge for the whole fucking thing, sew the whole fucking thing."

Hậu said, "Linh doesn't want you calling her."

"I haven't."

"She thinks you're another one."

"She isn't wrong to check."

He looked at my bag. "Have you got it?"

"Yes."

"And Dũng?"

"He's listed. So is the brother-in-law."

Hậu's eyes closed. He kept them closed for several breaths.

"I couldn't even get that right."

"You were trying to pay a balance that changed whenever they wanted."

"I helped change it."

There was nothing useful to say to that. I opened the food instead. He ate a little, slowly, with his good hand.

I told him about the forty-four people. I told him his payment had been entered as a transfer to me. He put down the spoon.

"I told you."

"Yes."

"You thought I was being dramatic."

"I thought you were frightened."

"Same fucking reason."

His discharge papers arrived in the middle of the afternoon. He had conditions attached to going, instructions to follow, and a nurse who seemed to believe he would ignore half of them. I gave her my number again. Hậu objected until she asked which of us was going to answer if he could not.

"Both," I said. "Put both."

At the desk he discovered someone had arranged a car for him.

"My cousin," he said immediately.

I looked at him.

"Linh called him."

"Does your cousin work for the Circle?"

"No."

"Did you?"

He looked away.

"I know what you're doing," he said. "Not everyone I know belongs to Vinh."

"Then call your cousin."

He used the hospital telephone. I stood far enough away to give him some privacy and near enough to see when his shoulders changed. He asked the same question twice. When he ended the call, he remained with one hand on the receiver.

"He sent a friend," he said.

"Whose friend?"

"I didn't recognize the name."

Outside, a man in a green polo shirt stood beside a silver car holding a folded piece of paper. When he saw Hậu, he lifted the paper and smiled.

"Your sister said you'd need help."

Hậu stopped.

"She doesn't have your number."

"Your cousin, then. He said—"

"Which cousin?" I asked.

The smile remained on the man's face for half a second longer than it should have. Then he looked at my bandaged arm.

"You're the one from last night."

"You're the wrong passenger," I said to Hậu. "Go back inside."

The man in the polo shirt put the paper away.

"No need for this. I have an address. We go to the address. He talks to somebody, then he goes home."

"Who's the somebody?"

"I'm a driver."

"Then drive."

He stayed where he was. A second man stood up from a low wall on the far side of the entrance. He had been looking at a phone. He put it away and began walking toward us.

Hậu whispered, "That's Thiện."

"Vinh's?"

"He works with him."

Thiện was tall and thin, with a face that might have seemed kind if you saw it doing something else. He stopped close enough that the driver moved aside for him.

"Hậu," he said. "You look better."

Hậu said nothing.

"We have to finish the paperwork."

"It's finished," I said.

Thiện looked at me with mild surprise, as though a chair had answered a question.

"We haven't met."

"Then your employer failed to circulate the helmet."

His mouth tightened.

"You have until midnight. That's what he gave you. This is separate."

"Nothing involving him is separate while he's standing with me."

"You can't buy one man and start claiming everyone who stands beside him."

"I'm not claiming him."

"Then why are you speaking for him?"

I stopped. Thiện saw it and pressed.

"Hậu can decide whether to get in the car. He's a grown man. You believe that, don't you?"

I turned to Hậu.

His face had gone gray beneath the bruises. He was staring at the car as if someone had opened a grave and asked him to choose whether it looked comfortable.

"What do you want?" I asked.

Thiện made a small, approving gesture.

"I want him to say what happens if I don't go," Hậu said.

Thiện's hand stopped moving.

"Nothing happens."

"To me?"

"To anybody."

"Say Linh's name."

The driver glanced at Thiện.

Hậu's voice rose. "Say you leave Linh alone. Say you don't call her school or go to her flat. Say you don't ask my cousin how much he needs this month."

People at the entrance had begun watching. The security guard came outside.

Thiện leaned closer to Hậu. "Lower your voice."

I put my hand between them.

"Answer him."

"Move your hand."

"You wanted him to speak. He's speaking."

For a moment Thiện considered trying to pass me. I saw the thought arrive in his shoulders and watched him decide against it.

"I'll call Vinh," he said.

"Do that in your car."

The guard asked them to leave the entrance. Thiện looked at the uniform, the people watching, and my hand still held between him and Hậu. He stepped back.

"Midnight," he said.

"I've bought a clock since we last discussed it."

He got into the silver car. The driver followed him. They left slowly, without looking as if they were leaving at all.

Hậu sat on the low wall. His good hand shook.

"I didn't mean to shout."

"You should do it more often."

"It never helped before."

"It helped me hear you."

I went to bring my car closer. When I returned, he was standing again. He had decided to do it himself while nobody watched.

We left by an ordinary road full of people finishing ordinary work. Hậu kept turning toward the rear window. I let him. After a while he said the silver car was behind us.

I had seen it at the last turn.

"Stay seated," I said.

"What are you going to do?"

"Take you somewhere you can rest."

"They'll see where."

"Not if they stop first."

He looked at my face. "Please don't do something fucking enormous."

"I'm open to suggestions."

"Drive to the police."

"And tell them what?"

"That they're following us."

"We can."

He stared at me. He had expected an argument. I had expected to make one. Then I thought of the guard saying I did not know whether the first man would return, and I understood that Hậu had spent enough of the day living inside other people's certainty.

I turned toward a busier road.

The silver car accelerated.

At the next junction a delivery van stalled diagonally across the space ahead of us. A man got out and lifted both hands in apology. The silver car stopped behind us too close to leave room. Another vehicle pulled beside it. Suddenly the people on the pavement were backing away from something they had understood before they could name it.

Hậu whispered, "Thiện."

I saw him in the mirror, stepping out of the silver car.

"Stay low," I said.

I got out before Thiện reached us.

"You're making this difficult," he said.

"You're blocking traffic."

"We just need to talk."

"Use your phone."

He had something inside his jacket. The driver from the hospital came around the other side of my car. A third man stood beside the van, holding a metal bar loosely enough to pretend it was part of his work.

There were people behind him. A woman with a shopping bag. A mechanic in stained shorts. Two men who had stopped their motorbikes because the road was blocked. I could get to Thiện. I could get to the driver. I could not know what either would do while I dealt with the other.

My mind gave me distances, weights, the likely speed of three possible movements. It did so with the cold generosity it brought to every problem. None of the numbers included what Hậu might do if he panicked.

So I asked him.

"Hậu. Can you hold the wheel?"

He looked up through the window.

"What?"

"Can you?"

"Yes."

"Keep it straight."

Thiện said, "Don't be stupid."

I put one hand beneath the open driver's door and the other against the frame. I lifted.

The side of the car rose from the road.

For an instant everybody stopped. Hậu's mouth opened. The man with the bar lowered it. Thiện forgot the thing inside his jacket and looked at the two wheels hanging above the concrete.

I moved the car sideways far enough to clear the nose of the silver one. The tires on the far side complained against the road. My knee felt as though someone had driven a heated wire through it. I kept moving until there was space, then lowered the car as gently as I could.

Hậu was gripping the wheel with one hand and staring straight ahead.

"That was enormous," he said.

"I couldn't find a smaller car."

Thiện reached inside his jacket.

I crossed the space between us and hit him before his hand came out. My palm struck his chest. He went backward across the hood of the silver car and struck the windscreen hard enough to whiten it with cracks. The object in his hand fell onto the road.

A pistol.

People screamed. The driver from the hospital ran toward it. I put my foot on it first and looked at him.

"No."

He stopped so quickly he nearly fell.

The man beside the van had dropped the bar. He stood with both hands raised, though nobody had asked him to.

I looked at the mechanic on the pavement.

"Call the police."

He held up his phone. He was already speaking.

Thiện slid off the hood and landed on one knee. He tried to stand. His breath failed him halfway through the movement.

I crouched beside him.

"Listen carefully. You can remember this as the afternoon you tried to take Hậu. Or you can come back and give it a worse ending."

He spat onto the road. There was blood in it.

"You think Bảo will let you sit after this?"

"I think he'd better buy a strong chair."

I stood and waited until the mechanic confirmed that someone was coming. The driver stayed where he was. The man by the van began explaining to nobody in particular that he had only been asked to park there.

Hậu got out of my car.

"Sit down," I told him.

"They'll ask what happened."

"I can tell them."

"So can I."

He walked to the pavement and stood beside the mechanic. His knees shook, but his voice did not when he began.

We lost almost two hours to statements and questions. I answered what I was asked. Thiện's explanation deteriorated each time someone pointed to the gun on the road. Hậu gave his own name, his own account, and the hospital's number. He did not say he had fallen.

By the time we reached the room I had arranged, the light was beginning to leave the street. I carried his small bag upstairs. He stopped me at the door.

"You still have the archive?"

"Yes."

"And you're still going?"

"Yes."

He looked at the bandage around my arm, the way I was standing on my left leg, and the car below the window with one door that no longer closed properly.

"I don't think you're lucky," he said.

"That's disappointing. I felt very lucky about the windscreen."

"I think you're going to make people believe things. Then they'll stand somewhere they shouldn't because they believe you."

I set his bag on the bed.

"Then keep telling me where they are."

He nodded after a while.

I left him with food, his discharge papers, and a telephone that belonged to nobody else. At the stairs he called my name.

"An."

I turned.

"Come back from the meeting."

"I intend to."

"Intentions don't get a fucking chair in the waiting room. Come back."

I promised him I would.

## 6. My Father's Hands

Our shop stood in a narrow lane that smelled of hot engines and rainwater. A woman next door was pulling down the shutter over her tailoring business. Two children argued about a bicycle light. A delivery rider sat on a low chair eating from a carton while my father finished his tire.

Dad's shirt was gray. It had once been black. His left hand held a wrench; his right thumb worked a small place near the rim.

Nobody looking at him would have thought the room needed more space for him to stand in.

I knew better.

He raised his eyes when I entered.

"Wash first."

I went to the tap. Pink water ran into the drain.

Mom appeared in the doorway from upstairs. She took in my sleeve, my eyes, and the way I was keeping my right heel off the floor.

"How many stitches?"

"I haven't counted."

"Then you haven't had them."

"I saw a doctor."

"Did the doctor see the end of the appointment?"

I turned off the tap.

She got the first-aid box and sat me under the good light. She had spent years keeping a printing business afloat. Her hands were quick and exact. She did not pretend the wound was smaller than it was.

"Hospital after we stop the bleeding," she said.

Dad finished with the rider. He charged him the amount they had agreed. The rider left with a working tire and no idea what sat upstairs in my bag.

Dad bolted the front door.

I stood up.

At the foot of the stairs I bowed to him. Deep enough to look at his hands instead of his face.

This was something our family had chosen. When I was sixteen, I had broken a man's arm and come home wanting praise. Dad had asked whether I could still enter a room as somebody's son. Since then, whenever I brought violence to the door, I stopped there and answered with my body.

He touched the back of my head.

"Come upstairs."

At dinner I told them about the Circle. I told them everything except how frightened I had been when the nurse asked whether I was family.

Mom set down her spoon when I showed her the photograph of the shop.

Dad kept eating until I finished.

"You want the yard?" he asked.

"Yes."

"You want those men working for you?"

"Some of them."

"And the rest?"

"They can leave."

He watched me for a moment.

"Will you let them?"

"If they leave other people behind."

He nodded once. He had heard the answer. He did not tell me it was a good one.

Mom asked about Shawna.

"She knows."

"What does she know?"

"That there is a meeting tonight. That they came to the lane. That Hậu was hurt."

"Does she know you want to stay in this business afterward?"

I looked down at my bowl.

"She knows I want the yard."

"That wasn't my question."

My parents had a gift for returning words to the shape in which they had been spoken. With other people, I could move a conversation a few inches to one side and let them mistake the movement for an answer. Here there was nowhere to put it.

"I'll tell her when she gets home."

"Before you sign something that makes her part of it," Mom said.

"Her business stays hers."

"Her life is hers too."

Dad put down his chopsticks.

"Your mother and I are not moving because someone took a photograph."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You were planning how to ask."

I had been. There was a building I could buy with a workshop on the ground floor and room for Mom's equipment above it. Better access. More space. A gate I could see from the street. I had built the argument while driving home and disliked how easily it fell apart when Dad named what was underneath it.

"It would be a better building," I said.

"For what?"

"The work."

"I can do my work here."

"For being safe."

He looked at the photograph. In it, he was bent over a wheel with both elbows drawn close to his body. It made him look smaller than he was.

"Do you think they could make me leave?"

"No."

"Then don't do it for them."

Mom resumed eating. "We can discuss a better building when it doesn't arrive disguised as a threat."

I took another mouthful and tried to stop arguing with people who had understood me before I entered the room.

When I was a child in California, I thought my father did not become angry because he could not hear the things that made me angry. Men spoke to him slowly in shops. They explained repairs he had understood before opening the casing. Once, a neighbor told him how fortunate he was to have a son who spoke English so well.

Dad said, "He also speaks too much."

I waited until we were home to tell him the neighbor was an idiot. He agreed and asked me to finish my homework.

That made me angrier.

"Why didn't you tell him?"

"He has lived with himself for fifty years. He should know."

"You let him talk to you like that."

Dad looked up from the fan he was repairing.

"Did he make me smaller?"

I said no because I wanted him to stop asking questions.

Then he took the motor housing in his hands and pressed a distorted edge straight. I watched the metal move between his thumbs. He had not intended it as a demonstration. He simply needed the fan to work.

I remembered that more clearly than anything the neighbor had said.

At sixteen, back in Ho Chi Minh City, I had finally found someone whose words could be answered with my fists. A man outside a billiards place had grabbed a younger boy by the hair and called me over to watch him apologize. I broke the man's arm. The boy ran. I came home with a torn shirt and a certainty that seemed large enough to occupy the stairs.

Dad asked what the boy's name was.

I didn't know.

He asked where the boy had gone.

I didn't know that either.

Then he asked whether I had helped the boy or merely enjoyed discovering that I could win.

I hated him for about twelve seconds. After that I began the more difficult work of hearing the question.

Now he said, "Hậu. You know where he is?"

"Yes."

"You know what he wants?"

"Some of it."

"Good."

One word. It landed harder than Vinh's entire offer.

At the hospital, Dad waited while the cut was dealt with properly. Mom came with us because she said two men could turn a simple instruction into an engineering debate. She was right. Dad asked one question about the swelling in my knee and I began describing the force involved in the jump. Mom looked at the ceiling.

"He needs his leg tomorrow," she told the doctor. "Please explain it as if he hasn't solved any mathematics."

The doctor did. I listened.

By the time we returned, there was less than three hours until midnight. Mom went upstairs to finish work she had set aside when I arrived. I could hear the printer starting, then stopping, then her voice on the telephone explaining that a color could not be corrected by calling it a different color.

Dad moved a stool away from the center of the shop.

"What happened to the car door?" he asked.

I told him.

He looked through the front window at the car parked outside.

"The frame?"

"Maybe."

"You could have used the other hand."

"To do what?"

"Support the weight instead of making the hinge do it."

I stared at him.

He began putting a wrench into the wrong drawer, noticed, and moved it to the right one. That was the level of attention he gave to something I had thought impossible until I did it.

"You weren't surprised," I said.

"The door is expensive."

"Dad."

"Yes?"

"The car."

He turned toward me.

"You were always going to get stronger."

"You could have told me how much."

"You would have tried it the same afternoon."

I could not honestly deny that.

He inspected the bandage on my knee without touching it. "You're tired."

"I can still work."

"That wasn't a complaint."

I asked him to stand with me in the shop.

"Your leg," he said.

"It holds."

"That wasn't what I asked."

I moved anyway.

I had worked out six ways to cross the space between us. I chose the fastest, feinted with my shoulder, and let the blow begin below his sight line. The force would have folded the steel workbench. I intended to stop it short of his chest.

His hand closed around mine.

I had not seen him move.

My feet left the floor. Not far. Enough that every calculation I had made about leverage became useless. His other palm rested on my damaged forearm without pressing the stitches.

He lowered me.

"Again," I said.

"No."

I tried to pull my hand free.

He let me spend my strength. I changed the angle. I drove from the floor. The bolt anchoring the nearby bench trembled as my heel struck its base.

My father's sleeve did not move.

I stopped.

He released me.

There are men you call strong because you have measured them. My father made measurement feel like something I had learned too early.

"Grandpa Hòa ever make you do that?" I asked.

Dad looked at my hand, then toward the ceiling, where Mom was still arguing with the printer.

"Your grandfather doesn't ask people to demonstrate things."

"That's not what I asked."

"I noticed."

I remembered a visit when I was fifteen. Hòa had been helping me with a geometry problem at a table near an open loading door. A pallet had slipped behind him. I had heard the timber snap, seen the tall load begin to tip, and started to stand. Grandpa had reached backward without turning his head. The load stopped. His pencil kept moving over my page.

Afterward he said the pallet had caught on something.

I had looked. There was nothing behind it but his hand.

Dad had arrived a minute later, examined the torn timber, and asked Hòa whether he wanted tea. Neither man mentioned the load again.

"What does he ask?" I said.

"Whether they understood what happened."

"And if they didn't?"

Dad gave me the look he used when I answered a problem before reading its last line.

"He waits."

"You could have broken it," I said.

"You need it tomorrow."

"You knew the jump would happen."

"I know your leg is hurt."

He crouched and checked the swelling. I watched the top of his head. A few gray hairs lay against the black.

"You can do much more than you think," he said. "You can also do much more damage. Learn the difference before someone makes you prove it."

He wrapped my knee. Then he put his hand on my shoulder, and for a moment all the men I had frightened that day seemed very far away.

"Why don't you do more?" I asked.

He looked at me.

"With this," I said. "With what you can do."

He took his hand away and sat on the stool.

"I did."

I waited.

"That isn't an answer you're getting before a meeting with men carrying guns."

"You think I'll copy you."

"I think you're already trying to turn it into permission."

Outside, someone knocked softly on the shutter. The tailor from next door had found the material I brought her and wanted to know what she owed. Dad looked toward the door. I went to answer it.

She held a folded length of cloth against her chest.

"Nothing," I said.

She frowned. "I pay my suppliers."

"Then pay what the original material cost. When the customer pays you."

She considered that and nodded. "Your wife has good taste."

"She didn't choose it."

"She chose you. Something must have transferred."

From behind me, Dad made a sound dangerously close to a laugh.

When she left, I turned toward him.

"You enjoyed that."

"Yes."

"People respect me in other buildings."

"Buy more buildings."

I laughed despite the pain in my back. He watched me until I had finished, and I understood that he had wanted to hear it. The day had tried to reduce me to the things I could do to other bodies. He wanted his son to make an ordinary sound in his own home before leaving again.

Upstairs, Mom was washing two cups.

"I need to be humble," I said.

"Yes."

"Because of him."

She looked toward the stairwell. Her expression changed. I had seen her look furious with him and exhausted by him. What crossed her face now belonged to years I had never witnessed.

"Because your father is extraordinary," she said. "And he spent a great deal of his life making sure you could grow up without being afraid of that."

She dried her hands.

"Someday I'll tell you about my grandfather."

"He's a hundred and four. He calls to complain when his phone updates."

"Yes," she said. "The phone has survived a great deal of disappointment."

I waited.

"Your father asked him for a second lesson," she said. "He had to learn where to put his feet before he got one."

From downstairs came the sound of Dad putting tools away.

"How old was your grandfather?" I asked.

"Old enough that people should have left him in peace."

"And Dad?"

"Young enough to think that was a useful advantage."

I tried to picture my father being held still by someone else's hand. The image refused to settle.

Mom took the bloodied towel from my hand.

"Come home tomorrow, An. If you want another lesson, you have to come home."

Before I left, I called Shawna. I told her plainly that I intended to take a position in the Circle and keep it. I told her that the yard was only part of what I wanted.

She listened. I heard the hotel air-conditioning through the phone and wished she were close enough to interrupt me with her face.

"I don't agree," she said when I finished.

"I know."

"No, you don't. You know I'm angry. Agreement is what we discuss after you come home. You do not get to confuse surviving with being right."

"I won't."

"You will. Then I'll remind you."

I closed my eyes for a moment.

"I love you."

"I love you too, you catastrophic fucking investment. Answer your phone."

I put it in my pocket and went downstairs.

## 7. The Chair at the Table

I went to the yard alone.

The hospital had discharged Hậu that afternoon. I had paid for a room where he could recover. He called twice before midnight. I told him to stay there. For once he listened.

The private yard lay among industrial roads serving the port region. Its name belonged to a company Bảo could afford to lose. Corrugated roofs covered repair bays. Rain stood between stacks of empty containers. Above the main gate a floodlight buzzed with a failing ballast.

The man at the gate checked my name against a list and then against a photograph on his phone. I stood still while he did it. Behind him, a woman in work clothes argued with a supervisor who kept glancing toward the office.

"You said the replacement was coming," she told him.

"It is."

"Then give me a time that lives on a clock."

The gate guard moved aside. I walked past them toward the office.

The woman looked at my bandages. "You're the new investor."

"Apparently."

"Good. We need a jack that doesn't try to kill somebody every third Tuesday."

"What happens on the other Tuesdays?"

"We don't use it."

The supervisor told her to get back to work. She looked at him, then at me, and chose to stay where she was.

"Your name?" I asked.

"Ngọc."

"Show me tomorrow."

"I'm supposed to go home tonight."

"Then show me when you come back."

She considered that answer without offering approval. I liked her more for making me wait for it.

The supervisor said, "They're expecting you."

He was perhaps forty, with a narrow build and the exhausted patience of someone who had spent the day explaining decisions he hadn't made. There was a clipboard beneath his arm. The top sheet listed drivers, departure times, and empty spaces waiting for signatures.

"How many people work this shift?" I asked.

"Depends what you count."

"People."

He looked toward the office again.

"Twenty-six in the yard. Others on the road."

"Are they getting paid?"

Ngọc laughed without humor. The supervisor's mouth tightened.

"They're working," he said.

"I heard you."

I gave him my number. He took the card as if it were another form he would eventually have to explain.

Vinh met me inside the office.

He wore a clean shirt. The tooth was gone from his cuff. There was a bruise along his jaw that had not been there at the warehouse, and he was holding one arm closer to his body than the other. I wondered whether Bảo had been disappointed by the helmet, the car, or the hospital visit.

"Busy day," he said.

"Yours could have been quieter."

He took a step closer. "You embarrassed Thiện."

"The gun did most of it."

"You don't understand what you started."

"Then explain it after you introduce me."

Bảo sat at the far end of a long table. He was smaller than I expected, with tired eyelids and an expensive watch worn loose enough to mark the wood when he moved.

There were six chairs. Five were occupied.

"Sit," Bảo said.

I sat.

He studied the bandage on my arm.

"Kiệt?"

"Alive."

"That will create work."

"He accepted my offer."

"I didn't."

I put the drive on the table.

"Then we'll discuss it when you make one."

Bảo smiled a little. He liked men who spoke that way until he had to share a room with them.

The men beside him introduced themselves. Lộc managed transport. Dần had interests in the gambling rooms. A heavier man called Tài described himself as an adviser and then said nothing else. The youngest was Đạt, who did not describe his work. His eyes moved to Vinh whenever Bảo paused.

Vinh placed the contract before me. My share was there. So were the workers, their balances, their relatives. He had printed the final pages on different paper.

I read every line.

Lộc began tapping the table with one finger. I looked at it until he stopped.

"Take your time," Bảo said. "We can afford patience."

"Your drivers can't."

"They aren't part of this meeting."

"They're in the contract."

Bảo's watch touched the table as he folded his hands.

"You went to see Phước."

"He invited me."

"And what did he offer?"

"An expensive explanation."

Dần laughed. Bảo waited until the laugh had ended.

"He would like you to think he controls the yard. He controls a piece of paper."

"So do you."

Vinh moved behind my chair. I heard the faint click of his belt against the wood.

"Careful," he said.

I turned a page.

At the bottom was the clause that made Hậu part of my investment. The wording had been cleaned since the archive. Somebody had replaced an ugly noun with a longer one, as if shame depended on how much ink it took to say a thing.

"Take the people out," I said.

Vinh leaned over my shoulder.

"They're part of the business."

"Then change the business."

One of the men at the table pushed his chair back. Bảo lifted one finger, and the chair stopped.

"You paid one balance," he said. "Why stop at one?"

He named the total.

I had more than enough money. He knew it. Buying them would teach him exactly what to acquire next.

"No."

"You can afford it."

"You can't afford to keep asking."

Lộc said, "You don't know what it costs to keep a yard operating."

"The wages would be a start."

"Those balances exist because people don't honor agreements."

"Some of them signed for money. Some signed because you had their brother in a room. Some never signed. Which agreement do you want to discuss?"

Lộc looked at Bảo. I had seen enough of that movement for one day.

"You can answer me yourself," I told him. "You're sitting in the chair."

Đạt shifted near the door. His right hand disappeared beneath the table.

Bảo said, "My people are loyal because I understand what they need."

"Is that why they keep asking your permission to breathe?"

"They ask because I give an answer. What do you give, apart from money and a performance in someone else's courtyard?"

I put the contract down.

"A different price for the same work. A door they can leave through. A manager who knows a broken jack is a problem before it falls on somebody."

"And when they steal?"

"I'll find out who stole."

"When they lie?"

"I'll find out what they lied about."

"When they make you look weak?"

I looked at Vinh behind my chair. He was standing close enough for me to hear him swallow.

"You seem very worried about appearances," I said.

For several seconds nobody moved.

Bảo reached for the drive. I let him take it. He turned it between two fingers and set it beside his watch.

"You want to be respected," he said.

"Yes."

"You think respect arrives because you announce the rules more clearly."

"I think it helps if I can follow them."

"You are not outside this room. You came here. You asked for a share. You want our money when it is paid to you and different names for the people who bring it."

He was close enough to the truth to make the room feel smaller.

I leaned forward.

"I want your chair," I said. "Eventually. I was prepared to buy the one beside it first."

Đạt made a short sound. Vinh stopped breathing for a moment.

Bảo looked at me without blinking.

"That is the first honest thing you've said."

"No. It's the first one you liked."

Outside, a vehicle engine stopped. Another sound replaced it: voices, several of them, raised together. Lộc turned his head toward the window.

Bảo looked at Vinh.

The office door opened. The supervisor stood outside with a split lip. I had seen him on my way in, counting drivers beneath an awning.

"They've stopped work," he said.

Vinh hit him across the face with the pistol.

I caught the supervisor before he fell.

"Why did they stop?" I asked.

He spat blood into his hand. He looked at Bảo first, then at me.

"They locked the gate. Nobody can leave."

Vinh aimed at his chest.

I drove the supervisor behind the door. The shot struck the frame, cutting wood across my cheek. I hit Vinh hard enough to carry him over the table.

For a moment all six chairs were empty.

Then the room filled with gunfire.

I took the table with me. Its edge caught two men at the knees as I heaved it onto its side. A bullet passed through the wood and opened my shoulder. Another struck the wall beside my face. I could smell the heat from it.

Bảo was already through a door behind his chair.

Lộc lay beneath one end of the table, shouting that he hadn't fired. Dần had made it behind a cabinet. Tài crawled toward the door with his hands over his head. They had spent the meeting looking like men who could decide what happened. Now the room belonged to the people who had reached for weapons.

Đạt tried to climb the fallen table. I seized his ankle and pulled him down. The back of his head struck concrete. He did not get up.

The next man came with a knife because his pistol had fallen under a chair. I broke his wrist, took the knife, and threw it through the light switch.

The room went dark.

Outside, the yard lights remained on. Vinh's silhouette moved against the doorway. I heard his shoes scrape as he brought the pistol level.

I crossed the space while he was deciding where my head would be.

His gun broke against the wall between our hands.

I held him there by the throat.

"Open the gate."

His eyes moved past me.

Something struck the building. The floor jumped. Glass came inward from the windows.

A forklift had driven its mast through the office wall.

Vinh lost his smile. Whoever had given the driver his orders had included Vinh in the loss.

I dropped him and turned toward the supervisor.

He was trapped beneath the frame of the outside door. The wall above him moved slowly, reluctantly, shedding dust from every joint.

I got my shoulder beneath it.

The building settled onto me.

For the first time that night I could not breathe.

The weight did not arrive all at once. It searched for places to rest. A beam pressed across my back. Something shifted higher in the wall, and a second load found the first. My feet spread on the wet concrete. I could feel every point where the ground refused to give me more.

"Crawl," I told him.

He pulled with both arms. One leg stayed caught.

I shifted the weight upward. The muscles across my back tore in small, distinct places. I felt each failure and the strength around it taking over. Dad had caught my hand as though none of this existed.

The supervisor stopped moving.

"Don't stop," I said.

"My boot."

"Then leave it."

"It won't—"

I drove upward again. The beam rose a fraction. I heard myself make a sound I had never heard from my own body before.

The supervisor's boot came free.

He crawled through the opening.

I followed as the wall came down. Concrete caught my left calf. I dragged the leg out, leaving cloth and skin behind. A beam struck where my spine had been.

The supervisor lay coughing in the rain.

Beyond him the forklift reversed. Its operator looked at the wreckage, then at me standing out of it.

He got down from the cab and ran.

I let him.

Ngọc came from a repair bay with two others. They pulled the supervisor farther from the wall. Somebody shouted for a phone. Somebody else wanted to know who was still inside.

"There are men under the table," I said. "Stay back until it stops moving."

"You're hurt," Ngọc said.

"Yes."

"Then fucking sit down."

Vinh emerged through the broken wall with blood covering half his face. He held a knife. He looked at the open yard and the men watching from the repair bays. He understood that no one would come to help him.

"Brother," he said.

He had never called me that before.

"Put it down."

"You saw what he did."

"I saw."

"We can talk."

"Put it down first."

He looked at the knife as though he had only just noticed it. His fingers loosened, then tightened again.

I knew the moment he decided. I also knew I could not make the choice for him. All day I had been moving other people's hands away from things. I was tired enough to understand why my father disliked watching me enjoy it.

Vinh lunged.

I caught his arm. His weight carried him close, and I drove my fist into his chest. I felt ribs give beneath it. His mouth opened. Nothing came out.

I lowered him to the concrete. I checked his neck.

There was no pulse.

Rain fell into his open eyes. I closed them with two fingers.

The men beneath the awnings had gone silent.

I stayed beside him for one breath longer than I needed. I had not meant the blow to leave him dead. I had also known what my hand could do when I threw it. Both things were true, and neither gave the other a place to hide.

The supervisor tried to stand.

"Where's Bảo?" he asked.

I looked toward the maintenance buildings at the back of the yard. An engine had started there. Beyond it, a rectangle of light disappeared as someone closed a door.

"Stay with your people," I said.

Then I stood up and went after Bảo.

## 8. What I Brought Home

Bảo had locked himself inside a maintenance building at the back of the yard. A small truck stood against the rear loading door. Its engine ran. He could have left through the gap beside it, but he wanted something from the room first.

I heard drawers opening.

Money. Paper. Something he believed he could start again with.

The steel door had three locks. I did not study them. I put my fingers under its lower edge and pulled.

The metal folded slowly toward me. The hinges tore from the frame one at a time.

Bảo shot through the opening.

I turned with the door. Two rounds struck it. A third entered my side, shallow enough that I felt its heat moving along the ribs.

He aimed again.

I threw the door.

It hit him across the legs and took him beneath a desk. His pistol went one way; the watch broke against the floor. I crossed the room, took the gun, and emptied it away from him.

He tried to move the door off his legs. He couldn't.

"The archive," he said. "You need me to explain it."

"I read it."

"You read numbers. I know the people."

"So will I."

He looked at the blood coming through my shirt.

"You're dying."

"Eventually. Open the yard."

He shook his head.

I crouched in front of him. I was tired enough that my hands had begun to tremble. He saw that and mistook it for uncertainty.

"Take my place," he said. "You've earned it. All of it."

He gave me a number. Then a name. Then the address of someone who had nothing to do with the yard.

I stopped him.

"You still think that's an offer."

Outside, somebody shouted. A man hit the locked gate with a length of pipe. The sound carried across the yard: three blows, a pause, three more.

Bảo listened. I could see him counting how many people were making noise and how many might still do what he asked.

"They'll tear this place apart if you let them out."

"Then you should have let them go home when their shifts ended."

"You're going to hand them everything because you won a fight?"

"I'm going to open a gate."

"You think that's a small thing."

"No. You thought keeping it closed was."

He leaned back against the desk. Pain had stripped the patient expression from his face. He looked older and less prepared to explain himself.

"You could have bought it," he said.

"I offered."

"You could still buy it."

"What part of the building are you planning to deliver?"

He looked at the door across his legs. For a moment I thought he might laugh. Instead he began telling me about companies, shares, people who would not recognize a new signature. He spoke quickly because he was afraid I would leave before he reached a fact that could save him.

I let him speak. I learned more in those two minutes than in the meeting. Not because he became honest, but because fear made him name the things he usually left vague.

Phước appeared twice.

So did a woman called Trinh.

When Bảo began talking about Ngọc's younger brother, I put my hand on the desk hard enough to split its edge.

"Enough."

He stopped.

I lifted the door off him. His legs lay at angles I did not want to examine. I pulled him clear of the desk and carried him outside.

"Where are you taking me?"

"To the people you know."

He began offering money before we reached the rain.

I laid him beneath the awning. The supervisor stood nearby with a cloth against his face. Behind him were drivers, mechanics, loaders, and two of the men who had guarded the office. They kept their hands where I could see them.

"Ambulances," I said. "For everyone who needs one."

"They're coming," Ngọc said.

The supervisor looked down at Bảo.

"He can still talk."

"Apparently."

"Then make him tell the gate men."

Bảo closed his eyes.

The supervisor crouched beside him. His voice stayed low.

"My daughter's been waiting since nine. She's with a neighbor who has to work in the morning. I asked you to let me leave. I asked three times."

Bảo looked away.

"Look at me," the supervisor said.

I recognized the words. I had spoken them in another room at the beginning of the night.

Bảo opened his eyes.

"Let them go," he said.

"They can't hear you."

"Tell them I said it."

The supervisor stood. "I have been telling people what you said for six years."

He walked away from Bảo and took out his own telephone.

I went to the gate.

It was a broad steel vehicle gate, chained through its frame. I could see people on the road outside, held back by two Circle men. One had a gun. He looked through the bars and saw my face.

"Leave it," I told him.

He placed it on the ground.

I gripped the bars on either side of the chain and spread my hands. The chain held for a moment. My shoulder began bleeding harder. Then the welded keeper tore from the frame with a shriek that went through my teeth.

I pushed both halves open.

Nobody moved.

The supervisor looked at me.

"We can go?"

"Yes."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow you decide whether to come back."

He lowered the cloth from his mouth.

"To work for you."

"If there's a yard left. If you want the job."

He looked toward the crushed office. Then he looked at the ambulances turning into the industrial road.

"You'll need a better office."

He did not smile. Neither did I.

The first person through the gate was a woman carrying a bag that had split at one corner. She moved carefully, watching the men outside as if they might change their minds. A driver followed her. Then three mechanics went together, walking close enough that their shoulders touched.

Others stayed.

An older loader approached me and asked about wages. He had worked there nineteen days without being paid. His voice was steady, but he kept rubbing his thumb across the end of his finger.

"Give your name to the supervisor," I said.

"He has my name."

"Then give it to me."

He did.

I repeated it back. He nodded, a little surprised that I had got it right through the noise.

"I can't pay everyone here this minute," I told him. "I can find out what you're owed."

"That's what they say."

"I know."

"No," he said. "You don't."

He walked away before I could answer. I watched him go and realized that my promise was not more useful to him because I had made it while bleeding. He needed money. I had given him a sentence.

Ngọc came back with a young man who had cut his foot climbing away from the office. She stopped beside me and pointed toward an ambulance.

"You're next."

"There are people inside."

"We know. There are people who do this for work. They're here. You can stop trying to be every fucking profession in the yard."

I looked toward the ruined office. People with equipment were moving through the opening. Lộc sat beneath an awning, holding his arm and speaking to an officer. Dần was nowhere I could see. Tài knelt on the concrete beside a stretcher.

"Đạt?" I asked.

Ngọc followed my eyes. "The one under the table?"

I nodded.

She didn't answer immediately. That was enough.

The supervisor returned. He had called his neighbor. I could tell from his face that someone had answered.

"My name's Sơn," he said.

"Aiden."

"I know. Everyone knows your fucking name tonight."

He held out my bag. Someone had found it beside the fallen table. The strap had torn, but the contents were inside.

"You need this?"

"Yes."

He handed it over, then caught my elbow when I tried to lift it with the wrong arm.

"You can pay the wages from a hospital bed," he said. "You can't pay them from here if you fall over."

I let him take me to the ambulance.

At the hospital I gave my name. Sơn had gone with the workers to give statements. Kiệt had agreed to release the records of their confinement. I sent him what he needed from the archive and kept the Circle's remaining accounts for myself.

When Bảo's phone rang in my pocket, I answered.

The man on the other end asked who was speaking.

"Your new partner," I said.

He took a long time to answer. When he did, he asked where I wanted to meet.

"Call in three days."

"Who are you?"

"The man who can tell you where your cargo is."

"Where's Bảo?"

"Busy."

The doctor took the phone out of my hand before I could hear what the man said next.

"So are you," she said.

The doctors worked on me until morning.

My father sat outside. He did not need to be told which chair could hold him.

There were parts of the night I remembered only afterward. Mom standing at the end of the bed while someone explained something she did not interrupt. Dad removing my watch because my hand had swollen. A policeman asking whether I understood a question. The cold surprise of realizing that I could be stronger than everyone in a room and still need them to keep me alive.

When I woke properly, Shawna was sitting beside me.

She had not changed out of the clothes she wore on the plane. Her suitcase stood against the wall. There was a dark line beneath each eye and a cup of untouched coffee near her hand.

"You're early," I said.

"You started bleeding before the agreed schedule. I adapted."

I tried to sit up. She put one finger on my chest.

"No."

"I can—"

"Finish that sentence and I will ask your father to tuck you in."

I lay back.

She looked at the bandages. Her mouth moved once before she spoke.

"How much of you is missing?"

"Less than it looks."

"That is not a unit of measurement."

I told her what I understood. She listened, asked three questions I had not thought to ask, and wrote the answers in the notebook she used for fittings.

"You brought work?" I asked.

"My clients still have bodies that need clothes. Yours is not the only fucking body in the city."

I smiled. It hurt. She watched me do it and shook her head.

Then she took my uninjured hand.

For a while neither of us spoke.

"Did you get the yard?" she asked at last.

"I don't know."

"Did you get the people out?"

"Yes."

She nodded once. It was not absolution. I knew her well enough to hear the difference.

"Did you kill somebody?"

"Two men."

Her fingers tightened around mine, then loosened deliberately.

"Do you know their names?"

"Vinh. Đạt."

She looked toward the window.

Outside, morning moved across the buildings. Somewhere a construction tool started and stopped. A woman laughed in the corridor, then lowered her voice when she remembered where she was.

"I told my supplier my husband was in hospital," Shawna said. "He asked whether I needed the delivery postponed. I said no. Then I stood in an airport toilet and tried to remember whether we'd argued about towels before I left."

"We did."

"I know. I remembered. You said the expensive ones felt exactly like the cheap ones."

"They do."

"They don't, you mathematical fucking peasant."

She pressed my hand against her cheek for a moment. Her skin was warm. I could feel how carefully she was breathing.

"You owe me an argument," she said. "A long one. You do not get to spend it dying."

"I won't."

"You don't know that."

I looked at her. "No. I don't."

She stayed until I slept again.

The last thing I saw was my father's hand on the back of her chair. He stood behind her without making either of us feel watched. In the yard I had lifted a wall until my body began to tear. Here, he held nothing at all, and the room felt steadier because he was in it.

## 9. Dead Men's Accounts

On the second morning, Vân arrived with a folder thick enough to qualify as a second patient.

Shawna looked at it, then at me.

"Absolutely fucking not."

"It's wages," Vân said.

Shawna moved her chair aside.

"Then five minutes."

"It'll take longer."

"Most things involving him do. Start with the people who need money today."

Vân sat. They had never met, but within thirty seconds they had agreed on an order of discussion that left me feeling like the junior person in my own hospital room.

There were three different wage lists. One came from the ordinary accounts. One came from Sơn's records of people who had actually worked. The third consisted of deductions entered by men who did not appear on either of the first two lists.

"There are payments missing," Vân said. "Some may have been made in cash. Some weren't. I won't call all of them theft until I know."

"How long?" I asked.

"To pay the amount everyone agrees on? Today. To know the rest? Longer."

"Do the first thing."

"From what account?"

I gave her the name of mine.

She took off her glasses.

"And what is that money?"

"Mine."

"A loan? An investment? A gift? You can't pour it in and decide later what everyone owes you."

Shawna leaned back in her chair. "I'm going to like you."

I looked at the figures again. The total was small compared with what I had offered for the yard. That made it easier to pay and harder to discuss honestly. If I called it a gift, people might hear a condition I had not spoken. If I called it a loan, they would hear the condition immediately.

"An advance to the company," I said. "Not to the workers. If the company can't repay it, that's my problem. Put that in writing."

Vân put her glasses back on.

"That I can do."

She worked while I watched the door. A police officer had spoken with me that morning about the shooting. I had answered questions about Vinh and Bảo, about the locked gate, about the people held against their will. I had not volunteered the Circle's entire business to anyone who asked whether I knew where a gun had been fired.

Shawna knew the difference. She watched me make it.

"Who owns the yard?" she asked.

"Several concerns," Vân said. "Some shares are clear. Some are disputed. The equipment has its own complications."

"So he hasn't bought it."

"No."

Shawna looked at me.

"You broke an office you don't own."

"It was falling on someone."

"That is an excellent reason to move it. It is a terrible deed of ownership."

Vân's mouth twitched.

I said, "I'm aware there is paperwork."

"Good," Shawna replied. "I was worried the new plan was to carry buildings home and declare them yours."

I reached for the water beside the bed. My hand shook hard enough to strike the glass against the tray. Shawna caught it before it fell.

Neither woman said anything. That was worse than the joke.

When Vân left, Shawna helped me sit higher. I hated needing help. I hated more that the hatred arrived before gratitude.

"You can be angry," she said.

"I'm not angry with you."

"I know. Your face isn't that complicated."

She put the glass in my hand again and held it until I had drunk.

"You're allowed to be a person for a few days."

"I've been a person all week."

"You've been a regional infrastructure event. Rest."

I did, badly.

Grandpa Hòa came just before lunch carrying a bag of fruit and the reading glasses he claimed he only needed because manufacturers had become dishonest about the size of print.

He had taught children, advised men who never admitted needing advice, and acquired a collection of former acquaintances whose names changed the atmosphere in certain rooms. Mom called the last group old friends. Dad called them people who should have listened. Hòa asked after their gardens and never helped me decide which description explained them.

He put the fruit beside my water.

"You look expensive," he said.

"I'm healing quickly."

"Then I expect a discount."

He sat. He was seventy-seven, narrow in the shoulders of a shirt he had owned longer than my car. Nothing about him asked the room to make space. I had watched people make it anyway.

"Your mother told me what happened."

"Which parts?"

"The parts she knows. Your father supplied the places where you probably improved your account."

"I don't improve facts."

"You arrange them very attractively."

From the corridor came a metallic crash. A trolley had struck the wall outside my door after its front wheel broke free. Two heavy cases slid from the top. Hòa stood, reached through the doorway, and stopped the nearer case before it hit the nurse beside it. With his other hand he caught the trolley itself, lifted its damaged end, and set everything upright against the wall.

He did not hurry. That was the part that stayed wrong when I replayed it. I had seen the cases fall. He had been sitting when they started. He had occupied each necessary place before the noise of the first impact finished reaching me.

The nurse stared at him.

"That wheel has been making a noise all morning," he said.

She nodded.

"There should be another trolley," he continued. "This one has expressed its opinion."

She went to find help. Hòa returned to the chair and picked up his glasses.

"How much did those weigh?" I asked.

"Too much for her foot."

His phone rang before I could ask a better question. He read the name and answered without looking relieved by the interruption.

"We're at the hospital. Yes, he's awake."

He held the phone toward me.

My great-grandfather's voice came through the speaker, enormous and irritated by the small device carrying it.

"An. Did the other man have a better plan, or did you forget yours?"

"Which other man?"

"That's an expensive answer already."

Hòa looked out at the nurse returning with a colleague. Both were examining the trolley as if it had concealed a feature from them.

"They say you're hurt," Tín said.

"They keep saying it."

"Listen once. Saves everyone time. Give your grandfather back his phone. He gets charged for your speeches."

I handed it over.

Hòa listened for a while, agreed to bring something on his next visit, then ended the call.

"He sounds well," I said.

"He frightened a delivery man this morning by helping carry a refrigerator. Apparently the man preferred six stairs at a time to remain a theoretical option."

"Could you do that?"

Hòa peeled the label from an orange.

"The delivery man? No. I don't know where he lives."

He left before the joke could become an answer. The repaired shape of the trolley remained outside my door for twenty minutes, and I kept looking at it after it was gone.

Sơn came in the afternoon. He wore the same trousers as the night of the fight, cleaned but still torn at one knee. His daughter had drawn a red line around the rip with a washable marker. He noticed me looking and said she had been checking where he hurt.

"How old?"

"Eight."

"What's her name?"

"Diễm."

I remembered it.

He gave me a list of people who wanted to return and another of people who wanted their tools collected so they would not have to come back. The second list was longer than I expected.

"They think I made it worse," I said.

"Some. Others just want a different road to work."

"You?"

"I want to know whether my daughter has to wait for me next Friday."

"She doesn't."

"You can't promise that. You don't know the work yet."

I looked at him. The room had become full of people explaining the limits of what I could promise.

"Then tell me what I can promise."

He sat down.

We talked about shifts, damaged equipment, late trucks, men who arrived drunk and men who never arrived late because being late had once cost them a month's wages. He named them. He refused to let the useful ones become saints or the difficult ones become excuses.

"Ngọc fights about everything," he said.

"I noticed."

"She's usually right about the machinery. She's less often right about the order in which we can fix it."

"Give her an order she can see."

"I tried. Bảo kept changing it."

"Then give me the order too. If I change it, make me say why."

Sơn looked at the bed, the bandages, the chair Shawna had left for a few hours to visit her atelier.

"You might mean that today."

"Ask me again tomorrow."

He agreed. It was the first agreement I made after the meeting that felt as though both people had read it.

Before leaving, he said a woman had come to the yard asking for me.

"Whose woman?"

He looked at me sharply.

"Her own. Đạt's mother."

I put down the list.

"Tell her where I am."

She came the following morning.

Her name was Hạnh. She carried a photograph in a plastic sleeve, the kind people used to keep a document dry on a motorbike. Đạt was younger in the picture. He wore a shirt too formal for his expression and stood beside a little boy who had put both hands into the wrong pockets.

She set it on the blanket.

"My son."

"I know."

"You know his name."

I looked at the boy.

"His son?"

"His nephew. He liked to carry him on his shoulders."

She sat without waiting for an invitation. Dad stood near the door. Mom had gone to find another chair, but Hạnh waved it away when she returned.

"I don't need everyone sitting with me," she said. "I need him to look."

I looked at the photograph.

Đạt had been armed. He had come over the table toward me in a room full of shots. Those facts remained true, but they did not occupy the photograph. In it, he was a young man trying not to smile too much while a child leaned against his leg.

"Did he suffer?" she asked.

"I don't think for long."

"You don't know."

"No."

She nodded, as if an honest uncertainty was the first usable thing anyone had given her.

"They say you pulled him down."

"I did."

"They say he had a gun."

"He did."

"He told me he was guarding an office."

I waited.

"Maybe he thought that was true. Maybe he thought I was stupid. Mothers get those two things confused when they want to sleep."

My mother stood very still beside the window.

Hạnh touched the edge of the photograph.

"Who pays for his funeral?"

"I will."

"And what do I sign?"

"Nothing."

"What do I owe you afterward?"

"Nothing."

"What do I have to say about you?"

I met her eyes.

"Whatever you want."

She looked at me for a long time. I felt the instinct to explain rise in my throat. I could tell her about the supervisor, Vinh's gun, Bảo leaving through the rear door. I could arrange the night into a sequence in which my action became the only one that made sense.

She had not asked me to make sense of it. She had asked who would pay.

I stayed quiet.

At last she slid the photograph back into its sleeve.

"He was useful to them," she said. "Now everybody wants to explain how little they knew him."

"I won't do that."

"You barely knew him at all."

"Then I won't pretend otherwise."

She stood. Dad moved aside before she reached the door. She looked at him, perhaps recognizing a father without needing the introduction.

"Your son is alive," she said.

"Yes," Dad answered.

There was no defense in his voice. She nodded and left.

I watched the doorway after it had emptied.

"I could have done it differently," I said.

Dad came to the bed.

"Perhaps."

"You would have."

"I wasn't there."

I hated the gentleness of that answer. I wanted him to tell me exactly how much stronger I needed to be before nobody died by mistake. He would not give me a number to hide inside.

"I still want the Circle," I said.

He looked at me without surprise.

"Then say that to your wife as plainly as you said it to me."

He left the photograph's damp outline on the blanket untouched.

By the fourth day, Bảo was under guard and several of the workers had given accounts that could not be dismissed as a dispute about wages. His representatives contacted people who had formerly answered to him. Some answered back. Some began calling me instead.

I took the calls.

Shawna listened to one from a man who ran a gambling room. He wanted to know whether the money he had been sending to Bảo should be held or delivered elsewhere.

"Hold it until we meet," I said.

"Can I keep operating?"

"Are the people there by choice?"

"Of course."

"Then be prepared to show me what you mean by that."

Afterward Shawna closed the door.

"This is the part you want."

"Yes."

"Not just the yard."

"Not just the yard."

She sat at the foot of the bed. There was no joke ready in her face.

"I won't put my business inside it."

"I didn't ask."

"You will want to help. You will call that different. I'm telling you now so we don't have to discover the difference while somebody is waiting to be paid."

"Your business stays yours."

"And I want to know where money comes from when it enters our home."

I nodded.

"Say it."

"I'll tell you."

She looked down at my hand, then back at me.

"I married a man who thought he could improve anything by understanding it. I didn't marry an office that people visit to ask whether they can keep their teeth."

"I'm still here."

"Then keep showing up."

When Phước called that evening, I answered with her beside me.

"The chair seems uncomfortable," he said.

"I'm having a different one made."

"I could help you settle the remaining matters."

"Which remaining matters?"

"You have a yard full of people expecting wages. You have damaged buildings on land controlled by other parties. You have clients who need to know whether the Circle still exists. And you have a body that, however impressive, is currently keeping a hospital profitable."

"You sound well informed."

"I'm available."

"Those aren't the same thing."

He laughed softly.

"You'll find out how much time Bảo spent keeping you from meeting people like me."

"I'm looking forward to meeting them."

"Then come back to work."

I ended the call. Shawna raised an eyebrow.

"Friend?"

"No."

"Good. Your taste has been uneven this week."

I reached for her hand. This time I held it without shaking.

## 10. First Payroll

I returned to the yard with a walking stick my mother had chosen because it was ugly enough to discourage me from treating it as an accessory.

Ngọc saw it and grinned.

"New weapon?"

"Medical equipment."

"So was the forklift, briefly."

I looked at her.

She shrugged. "If we can't laugh about the office, we'll have to look at it."

The ruined building had been cleared enough to reveal how little stood behind the expensive furniture. Two temporary rooms had been brought in for administration. One contained a desk, three chairs, and a fan that worked. The other belonged to Vân, who had made that decision before asking whether anybody objected.

Sơn stood outside with the shift list.

"Twenty-one came back. Seven haven't decided. Eleven want their tools collected. The rest we haven't reached."

"Then keep trying."

"We're trying."

I heard the correction and accepted it. He had been working while I slept in a bed that people paid to keep clean.

At the gate, two men were replacing the torn keeper. One looked up as I passed and held out the piece of steel I had pulled free.

"You want it?"

"Why?"

"Somebody will."

"Sell it for scrap."

He looked disappointed. I wondered how much he had intended to ask for it.

Inside Vân's room, the fan moved warm air across a table crowded with papers. There were no decorative photographs. There was a calendar, a calculator, and a mug full of pens that had all been tested.

"We have temporary authority to continue ordinary operations," she said. "The other owners prefer that to watching the business stop. That is not the same as agreeing you own it."

"I understand."

"I'm going to say it until your face catches up."

I sat.

The ordinary wage payments were ready. The deductions were harder. Some men had borrowed voluntarily. Others had signed balances they could not read or could not refuse. A few had received advances and disappeared. There were people whose work had been entered under somebody else's name because a supervisor wanted to keep the difference.

"Pay the undisputed part first," I said.

"Already done."

"Then why did you bring me in?"

"So you could hear yourself agree before the complaints arrive."

She gave me the first complaint.

A loader called Dũng claimed four additional nights. The name made me look up. He was the driver Hậu had tried to help, now doing whatever work the company offered while his original balance grew.

"He worked them," Sơn said from the doorway.

"Three," Ngọc said behind him.

Sơn turned.

"The fourth he came in but went home. He was sick. I drove him to the gate."

"He says four."

"Then he's counting the night he threw up beside the tires."

Vân looked at me.

"Ask him," I said.

Dũng came in holding his cap. He had the cautious face of a man who had learned to say as little as possible until he knew which answer was expensive.

I asked about the fourth night.

He looked at Ngọc, then at Sơn.

"I came."

"Did you work?"

"Some."

"How much?"

He told us. It was less than an hour.

"Then we pay that and discuss sick time separately," Vân said.

He looked frightened. "You said no more deductions."

"We said no invented deductions," I answered. "You worked three nights. You came for part of a fourth. We can say both things without making the first three disappear."

He waited.

Nobody threatened him. Nobody added a penalty because his first answer had been better for him than the second. After a while his shoulders lowered.

"All right," he said.

Vân wrote the amount. He checked it twice and left.

Ngọc watched him cross the yard.

"He used to argue," she said. "Before all this. About everything. Football, food, whether his truck sounded better after he changed a part. Now he apologizes when the rain starts."

I looked at the empty chair.

"Tell Hậu he came back," I said to Sơn.

"Hậu knows. He called me."

"Did he come?"

"No. Dũng doesn't want to see him."

I nodded. That was another decision I could not improve by arriving faster.

At noon Ngọc took me to see the jack.

It stood in a repair bay beside two other pieces of equipment that looked old enough to remember better management. She showed me what failed, where it failed, and what people had done to keep it working because stopping work had always cost more immediately than danger.

I began to lift the assembly so I could see underneath.

She slapped my hand.

I looked at her in surprise.

"No," she said.

"I can hold it."

"Congratulations. Will you be holding it next Thursday when somebody else crawls under it?"

I took my hand away.

"We replace it."

"We take it out of use first. Then we replace it. People hear 'replace' and think they can squeeze one more job out of the old thing."

"Do it."

She called two men over and made them help mark it clearly. Only then did she look back at me.

"You really don't know how this place works."

"Not yet."

"Good. Keep the last word. Most owners drop it."

She gave me a list of what needed attention. It filled both sides of the paper.

"You said a jack."

"I was being seductive."

I laughed and felt the stitches in my side object. She smiled, satisfied that at least one piece of damaged equipment had responded as expected.

Lộc arrived just after lunch.

He stood by the gate in a clean shirt, his arm in a sling. The men working nearby stopped talking when they saw him. He had spent enough time speaking for Bảo that his silence now seemed suspicious.

Sơn asked whether he should send him away.

"Bring him in."

Lộc entered the temporary office and looked at the plain desk.

"It's smaller."

"So was the bill."

He sat carefully, protecting his injured arm.

"I didn't fire."

"I remember."

"I told them you had brought what they wanted."

"You also told me the balances were agreements."

He looked at the floor.

"Some were."

"We're finding those."

"You'll need somebody who knows the clients."

"Are you asking for work?"

He looked up, offended despite himself.

"I have interests here."

"Then discuss them. Don't arrive dressed as a favor."

For a while he said nothing. Outside, a reversing vehicle sounded its warning. People moved without running. The ordinary noise made the office feel less like the one he remembered.

"Bảo made decisions," Lộc said. "I kept the trucks moving."

"Did the trucks move people who wanted to leave?"

"Sometimes."

"Did you know?"

He rubbed the edge of his sling.

"Sometimes."

I waited until he looked at me.

"If you work here, Sơn controls the shifts. Vân controls the accounts. You tell them what you know. You don't give orders through someone else's telephone."

"You'd put me under him?"

"I'm putting you in a job. Decide whether you want it."

He looked toward the doorway, where Sơn stood within hearing distance. The humiliation mattered to him. I could see him weighing it against the more frightening prospect of having nowhere left to belong.

"I can bring three clients back," he said.

"Bring the terms first."

He agreed.

After he left, Sơn came inside.

"You trust him?"

"No."

"Then why?"

"Because he knows things we need to know. Because he's more useful where you can hear him. Because being afraid of what I might do is not the same as having a reason to work."

Sơn leaned against the doorframe.

"And because you like deciding whether he gets to stay."

I looked at him.

"Yes."

He nodded slowly. "At least you heard it."

The first payments went out while we were still talking. Phones began sounding across the yard. One man checked his twice, then handed it to a colleague to make sure the amount meant what he thought it meant. Another sat on a stack of tires and called home without moving anywhere private.

Ngọc looked at her screen, put it away, and went back to work. Ten minutes later she bought cold drinks for the people in her bay.

I stood outside the office watching them until Vân told me to sit down before I bled on the new step.

The next problem arrived in a flatbed truck.

Two men got out with papers that said they had come to recover equipment against an unpaid obligation. One wore a tie loose around his neck. The other carried a clipboard and looked at the machines before looking at the people.

Sơn brought them to me.

"Whose obligation?" I asked.

The man with the tie named a company I had seen in Bảo's archive.

"And whose equipment?"

"The company's."

Vân took the papers. Her face changed as she read.

"This is for another yard," she said.

"Same concern."

"Different assets."

"Our instructions are to recover equivalent value."

She looked at me over the page. I could hear Phước's voice explaining that he owned things the Circle needed.

"You can leave copies," I said. "You can't take a machine."

The man with the clipboard shifted his weight.

"Do you have authority to stop us?"

"Do you have authority to take it?"

He pointed to the paper Vân held.

"Then you're both reading different documents," I said. "We'll have someone examine them. Meanwhile, your truck stays empty."

"This will add costs."

"Send them to the person who gave you the wrong address."

The man with the tie glanced toward the gate. He had finally noticed the newly welded keeper and the piece of old steel lying beside it.

"We are not here for a confrontation."

"Good. The yard's had enough this month."

They left the papers and drove away. Nobody was struck. Nothing was bent. Sơn watched the truck disappear and looked faintly disappointed with how much stress had fit inside an event that would make a poor story afterward.

Phước called twenty minutes later.

"I hear you delayed a collection."

"Wrong address."

"Those distinctions become expensive."

"Then people should learn them."

"Come see Trinh. She can arrange a cleaner position for you."

"Why don't you arrange it?"

"Because you don't like me."

"Does she?"

"She likes being paid. You'll find it refreshing."

That evening, Shawna met me at her atelier.

She had moved a fitting to accommodate a client whose event had changed dates. Three assistants worked around a half-finished dress. Nobody stopped because I entered. One asked me to hold a roll of cloth while she cleared a table.

I held it.

Shawna watched from across the room, smiling slightly.

"You look useful," she said.

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain about eight liters of medical advice you haven't followed. Sit after she's done."

I told her about the wages, Lộc, the men with the papers.

She listened while pinning a sleeve.

"You saved the company from running out of money today," she said. "Can it pay next month without you?"

"It will."

"That wasn't yes."

I looked at the cloth in my hands.

"I'm working on it."

She nodded. "There he is. The man who learns things. I was worried the other one had eaten him."

I stayed until closing and carried the rolls to the storeroom exactly where she told me to put them.

## 11. The Woman with the Contract

Trinh asked to meet at Shawna's atelier.

I said no.

Shawna asked why I was refusing appointments she had made six months earlier.

"She's part of the yard problem," I said.

"She's also part of the dress problem. The dress problem existed first."

"Phước sent me to her."

"Did he order a dress?"

"No."

"Then he doesn't get a fitting. You can come after she's finished."

I arrived at five and waited in a chair near the front window while my wife told a woman who controlled more money than most men I had met that she was standing incorrectly.

Trinh looked into the mirror.

"I always stand like this."

"Then the dress will always pull there."

"Can you change it?"

"I can change the dress or you can stop trying to have an argument with your left shoulder. Both are available. One costs more."

Trinh laughed and adjusted her posture.

She was in her forties, with short hair and a face that did not waste much movement. A man had accompanied her but remained outside. She had brought no entourage into the fitting room, and she listened when Shawna explained why something could not be done invisibly.

I watched that more closely than the dress.

Afterward Trinh changed back into a dark suit and came to sit opposite me. Shawna put a tray of tea on the table.

"You have twenty minutes before I need this room," she said. "If either of you bleeds, stains, or threatens anything, we charge by the meter."

"Your wife has excellent terms," Trinh said.

"I've signed them."

"Have you read them?"

Shawna smiled and went back to work.

Trinh set a slim folder between us.

"Phước says you dislike financing."

"I dislike surrender with an interest rate."

"Most people dislike the rate first."

She opened the folder. There were no dramatic totals circled in red. The first page named the parties with claims on the yard. The next named the equipment owned separately from the land. The third described what would happen if ordinary business stopped while everybody argued over the first two.

"I hold an interest through one of the operating companies," she said. "Bảo held another. Lộc holds a smaller one than he tells people. Phước has influence over the land and some of the credit. There are people behind each of those positions who would prefer you did not learn they exist."

"And you?"

"I prefer you learn before you destroy something I have paid for."

"The office wasn't planned."

"The forklift rarely is."

I read the pages. She waited without tapping the table or explaining what I should be impressed by.

"You can sell me your interest," I said.

"I could."

"Will you?"

"You haven't asked the price."

"You haven't decided whether to name the real one."

She leaned back, looking pleased.

"Phước underestimated the reading part."

"He was distracted by a railing."

"Thành is telling people it had rust."

"It does now."

Trinh took a pen from the folder and wrote a figure on a blank sheet. It was too high for the income the yard could currently produce and too low for the freedom she would be giving up if I made it produce more.

"Why?" I asked.

"Because Bảo has made this concern difficult to hold. Because Phước makes it difficult to leave. Because you're young enough to work very hard before noticing that both men were charging you for the privilege."

"And because you want me between you and Phước."

"That would be convenient."

"For you."

"I am the person selling."

I looked through the window at the man waiting beside her car. He was eating something from a paper bag. He did not appear to be watching the street for enemies.

"Does he know what you do?" I asked.

"He drives. He knows where I ask to go."

"Does he get to refuse?"

"He refused a trip last week because his daughter was ill. I drove myself."

"Then why work with Bảo?"

Trinh put the pen down.

"Because the business made money. Because when I asked about a cost, somebody gave me an answer that let me stop asking. Because I own more than one concern, and it is easy to call distance a lack of knowledge. Which explanation would make you feel best about buying from me?"

"None."

"Good. Use that."

She did not lower her eyes. I respected that, though it did not make the answer better.

"Lê Đức Hòa is your grandfather," she said.

I stopped turning the pen.

"Why?"

"Your wife mentioned him during a fitting. I wondered whether it was the same man."

"Which man?"

Trinh studied me for a moment.

"A man who once attended a meeting about a building that several people had already decided would fall down. By evening, the building was still standing and nobody wanted to chair the next meeting."

"What did he do?"

"I wasn't in the room. The people who were became remarkably careful about describing it."

"He's a teacher."

"Yes," she said. "That was the word they used."

She moved the contract toward me.

"I'm selling you an interest in this concern, Aiden. Your family is not additional security, and I suggest you make sure nobody else tries to price it that way."

I looked at the signature line. For the first time in the meeting I wanted to ask a question whose answer would not help me buy anything.

I kept it for Hòa. I already knew he would find some ordinary object to examine while deciding how much of the answer I had earned.

"I'll buy at this price," I said, writing a different figure. "Subject to the people who actually examine these arrangements agreeing that what you're selling exists."

"I employ some of those people."

"So will I."

"You don't need to say it like a threat."

"I'm trying to be charming."

"Practice with the wife. She has invested more patience."

Shawna called from the back room, "The returns are inconsistent."

Trinh laughed again. I found myself laughing with her and made a point of remembering that friendliness did not improve a contract.

We discussed what I would take, what she would keep, and what neither of us could promise while other claims remained unsettled. I refused a clause that would let her make decisions through a person she appointed. She refused a price reduction based on damage I had caused. We agreed that the wages would remain outside the argument about ownership.

It took longer than twenty minutes.

Shawna came back and took the tea away.

"The room is booked," she said.

Trinh closed the folder immediately.

"We can finish elsewhere."

"We can finish tomorrow," I said.

She looked at me, then at Shawna.

"Good. You may be teachable."

At the door she stopped.

"There is a woman named Xuyên. She runs a food distribution business outside the city. The yard has handled loads for her for years. Bảo began using her payments to settle obligations that weren't hers. She has stopped trusting anyone who calls from this number."

She tapped the contact details in the folder.

"Why tell me?"

"Because Phước is offering to move her business elsewhere. If he succeeds, you'll have bought a yard that loses one of its steadier clients before your first complete month."

"Can I speak to her?"

"You can try. She has become very good at ending conversations."

After Trinh left, Shawna stood beside me at the window.

"Do you trust her?"

"More than Phước."

"That is a low fucking ceiling."

"She knows what she wants."

"So do you. That's why you're both difficult."

I turned toward her.

"I'm buying into the operating company. A real interest. Clear terms. Vân will have someone independent examine it."

"Good."

"That isn't the part you object to."

"No."

We watched a woman across the street struggle to fit a large package onto a motorbike. Another woman came out of the shop to help. Together they argued about the angle, tried it one way, then another, and made it work.

"I know some of my clients have money I wouldn't want to earn," Shawna said. "I'm not pretending every dress is washed clean by my intentions. But I sell them work. I don't sell them permission to be cruel."

"Neither do I."

She looked at me.

I heard how quickly I had answered.

"I intend not to," I said.

"That's a sentence we can work with."

I asked whether she wanted me to leave the Circle's other businesses alone.

"I want you to decide whether you need them or enjoy them," she said. "Then stop using the first answer to cover the second."

"I enjoy being the person they call."

"I know. You answer a ringing telephone like it personally recognizes your greatness."

"Sometimes it does."

She shook her head, smiling despite herself.

"That. Keep that ridiculous fucking face. I know how to be married to that man. The one who comes home speaking in threats can sleep at his office."

I kissed her. She let me, then pushed me gently away from the sleeve she was pinning.

"Work," she said.

I called Xuyên from the car.

She answered on the fourth attempt.

"If this is about the invoice, I have paid it."

"It's about why you're being asked twice."

"Then ask the person asking."

"I'm trying to find out what he said."

"Who are you?"

"Aiden Trần. I'm managing the yard now."

Silence.

"The one who put Bảo in hospital."

"Among other things."

"I don't care how strong you are. My customers still need their deliveries."

"So do I."

"You don't have my customers."

"Not if we keep having this conversation."

She made a short, irritated sound that might have been the beginning of amusement.

"Come tomorrow. Bring someone who knows the work. You don't get to stand in my yard and tell me a truck is a truck."

"I'll bring Sơn."

"He can come. You can follow him."

She ended the call.

When I told Sơn, he looked relieved for the first time that day.

"She'll listen if the load arrives."

"Will it?"

"It should."

"Why should?"

He showed me the schedule. A delivery had been reassigned while the yard was closed after the shooting. The new carrier belonged to a concern Lộc said had worked with Bảo before. The driver had called that morning to say there was a delay, then stopped answering.

I looked at the carrier's name.

Phước had mentioned it in the courtyard as casually as he mentioned the weather.

"Where's Lộc?"

"Getting the client terms you asked for."

"Call him in."

Lộc came with a folder and the careful look of a man returning to a room where his old position no longer fit. I asked about the carrier. He said it had been a reasonable choice under the circumstances.

"Who made it?"

"I did. Before the meeting."

"At whose suggestion?"

He rubbed the edge of his sling.

"Phước's office arranged the contact."

Sơn looked away.

I kept my eyes on Lộc.

"Find the load."

"I'm trying."

"Then keep trying where we can hear you."

He sat at the spare desk and began making calls. The first three were polite. The fourth became louder. On the fifth, he stopped speaking and listened for a long time.

When he ended it, he looked at me.

"They want the old balance settled before they release it."

"Whose old balance?"

He named Bảo's company.

Sơn said, "That's not Xuyên's problem."

"They know," Lộc replied.

I put my walking stick against the desk.

"Then we'll explain what is."

## 12. The Dry Road

Xuyên met us beside a warehouse with a roof that had been patched in four different colors.

She wore rubber sandals and carried a notebook with an elastic band around it. Three men unloaded sacks behind her. One stopped to look at my walking stick. She told him the sacks would not become lighter while he watched a stranger.

He went back to work.

Sơn introduced me.

"I know," she said. "He's taller in person."

"The paperwork makes everyone smaller," I said.

"Mine makes them heavier. Come inside."

We sat at a table beneath a fan. She put two invoices in front of us. The amounts were identical. The account names were not.

"I paid the first. Then someone said the first concern no longer handled the delivery. He said I could recover that payment separately. Meanwhile the second needed paying before the load moved."

"Who called?" Sơn asked.

She gave him a name. He recognized it and wrote it down.

"The goods are mine," she said. "They were paid for before your people touched them. My customers have paid deposits. If I miss the deliveries, I lose the deposits, the next orders, and the people who believed I could do a simple fucking thing twice in a row."

"We'll find it," I said.

"I already know where it is."

She opened the notebook and showed me an address.

"Then why—"

"Because knowing where something is does not make the gate open. I have visited. My manager has visited. We have brought papers. We have brought copies of papers. We have been told the man who can decide is eating, sleeping, away, sick, in a meeting, and coming back shortly. If he ever returns, he will be the busiest dead man in the province."

Sơn hid a smile behind his hand.

I read the address.

"We'll go."

"You will take my manager. You do not know what belongs to me. You will also bring back the driver. He has been there since yesterday, and his wife has been calling my office because somebody told her this is my fault."

"What's his name?"

"Bình. He used to work your yard. He knows Sơn."

Sơn looked up sharply. "Why didn't he call me?"

"They took his phone when he shouted."

I stood.

Xuyên pointed at the stick.

"Can you walk far enough to be useful?"

"I can be useful from here. Walking is extra."

"Then try doing both without turning my cargo into a story."

Her manager, a quiet man named Tâm, rode with us. He held a folder in both hands and spoke only to correct a turn or identify a company we passed. Sơn sat beside him, calling people who might tell us what the other yard expected to happen.

Lộc had reached somebody in Phước's office. The answer was that the load could be released if outstanding matters were resolved. Nobody would say whose matters without adding another name to the conversation.

"They want you to offer," Sơn said.

"I know."

"If you pay, the load moves."

"And the next one?"

He looked out of the window.

"Xuyên's customers don't care about the next one yet."

That was the difficulty. Phước could make the long problem belong to me and the immediate one belong to someone who couldn't afford to wait. Every refusal would sound principled until a woman with a notebook explained who had missed a payment because of it.

I called him.

"Your carrier has my client's cargo."

"Our client," he said.

"Then tell them to release it."

"There are balances."

"Between you and Bảo."

"Between concerns. You cannot take the profitable parts and leave every obligation attached to a man in a hospital."

"Put the claims on the table. Keep Xuyên's goods off it."

He was quiet for a moment.

"You sound as if you own something."

"I'm learning from people who do."

"Then learn that force is expensive when you apply it to commerce."

"So is delay. You keep sending me invoices for yours."

I ended the call before he could turn it into another invitation.

The yard sat beside a road bordered by low workshops and patches of bare ground. Its gate was open. Inside, a truck waited beneath a loading canopy. I recognized Xuyên's cargo markings from the papers Tâm carried.

A man in a white cap came out of the office.

"No collection today."

"Then stop storing it," I said.

Tâm showed him the papers. The man barely looked.

"We have instructions."

"Bring the person who gave them," Sơn said.

"He's not here."

"Amazing," I said. "Xuyên thought he'd died."

The man in the cap looked at me for the first time. His eyes moved to the bandages visible above my collar.

"You should be resting."

"People keep interrupting."

Behind the canopy, a door opened. Bình stepped out carrying an empty bottle. He was broad through the shoulders, with a face darkened by years on the road. When he saw Sơn, he stopped walking.

"They told me you weren't answering," he said.

Sơn went toward him. The man in the cap stepped between them.

I moved beside him.

"Don't."

He looked at my hand on the walking stick, then at my other hand. Whatever he had heard about the yard, he decided not to test which one hurt more.

Sơn reached Bình. They spoke quickly. Bình pointed toward the office, then at the truck. He had been told he would be responsible for the load if he left without it.

"You're leaving now," Sơn said.

"With it?"

"We're working on that."

Bình looked at me. "You're the one who moved the gate."

"It was badly positioned."

He nodded as if that were a satisfactory professional explanation.

Two more men came from the office. One carried a short club. The other kept both hands empty. The empty-handed one looked more prepared.

"You can take the driver," he said. "The load stays."

"At least we're negotiating," I replied.

"There is no negotiation."

"Then you gave me the driver for free. That was generous."

He didn't smile.

Tâm stepped forward with the paperwork again. "These goods belong to our company. We have paid for transport. We will record that you are refusing release."

The man took the folder from him and threw it into a puddle.

For one moment the whole yard became quiet.

Tâm looked down at the spreading papers. He did not move. His face changed in a way I recognized from the tailor's face when she showed me the torn cloth. It wasn't only anger. It was the exhaustion of seeing careful work treated as something a stronger person could destroy for emphasis.

I set the walking stick against a post.

"Pick them up," I said.

The man with the club moved first.

He swung at my injured side. I stepped inside the arc, caught his forearm, and drove him backward into the post beside my stick. The wood shook. The club fell. I put him on the ground before the other man reached me.

The second man was faster. He struck low, expecting me to protect the bandages. I let the first blow glance off my hip and caught the next against my forearm. Pain went through the old cut. I ignored it long enough to take his balance.

He tried to turn away. I lifted him clear of the ground by his belt and collar and put him on the roof of the office porch.

Not through it. On it.

He landed spread across the corrugated sheet, which bowed beneath him with a long metallic complaint. His hands grasped at nothing useful.

"Stay," I said.

He looked down at me.

"How the fuck—"

"You can finish the question when somebody brings a ladder."

The man in the cap ran toward the truck.

I thought he intended to take the keys. Instead he climbed into the cab and started it.

Bình shouted and ran after him. Sơn grabbed his sleeve before he could reach the moving vehicle. The truck lurched forward, scattering people away from the canopy.

There were too many bodies near its path.

I moved before I had finished deciding how.

The loading platform rose beside me. I stepped onto its edge, felt the old wood bow, and used the returning force as it straightened. My knee objected. The world dropped away beneath my feet.

This time I knew I was jumping.

I still went farther than I intended.

I cleared the side of the truck, struck the far edge of the roof with both hands, and nearly slid off into the narrow gap between it and the wall. For an instant I hung by three fingers while the truck gathered speed.

Luck arrived in the form of a tied cover that should have been removed before departure. The loose end whipped around my wrist. It gave me enough purchase to pull myself onto the roof.

I lay flat while the vehicle went through the gate.

The road beyond was mercifully clear for several lengths. Then a motorbike turned into it from a side lane, and I heard the driver sound the horn with the panic of a man discovering that a plan had other people inside it.

I moved along the roof toward the cab. The truck swayed. My injured shoulder failed under a shift of weight, and I struck the metal hard enough to lose a breath.

Below me the brakes shrieked.

The driver had seen what lay ahead and wanted to stop being brave.

That decision saved the rider. It nearly threw me under the truck.

I caught the upper edge of the cab as my body moved forward. Metal cut into my fingers. For half a second all my weight and the vehicle's remaining motion argued through my hands.

I pulled.

The cab roof folded upward around my grip.

The driver looked up through the opening and saw me hanging over him, bleeding onto his dashboard.

He took both feet away from the pedals.

The truck rolled to a stop against the soft verge.

For several breaths neither of us moved.

Then I said, "Turn it off."

He did.

I climbed down so badly that Bình, arriving at a run, had to catch my elbow.

"You could have waited," he said.

"For what?"

He looked at the torn roof.

"I'm still deciding."

The man in the cap began explaining that he had only meant to move the truck to another part of the yard. Sơn listened until the explanation reached its third version, then told him to sit on the verge and wait for the people who had been called.

Tâm arrived carrying his wet papers.

"The load?" I asked.

He went to inspect it with Bình. When they came back, neither looked pleased.

Several sacks had split during the abrupt movement. The rear section of the load had shifted against a sharp edge. Some goods could be recovered. Others could not be delivered in the condition Xuyên had promised.

I called her myself.

She listened without interrupting.

"Is Bình out?"

"Yes."

"Can Tâm speak?"

I gave him the phone. He described the damage more accurately than I could. I watched his face while she answered.

When he handed it back, she said, "You will pay for what is lost."

"Yes."

"You will also come explain the delayed deliveries to the people who already think I lied."

"Yes."

"And you will stop promising to solve everything by arriving."

I looked at the truck, the torn cab roof, the driver sitting on the verge with his head in his hands.

"I didn't promise that."

"Your people did when they said you were coming."

I had no answer ready.

That evening, I stood in Xuyên's warehouse while she divided the recoverable goods from the losses. Bình called his wife. Tâm spread documents beneath a fan. Sơn arranged the next deliveries with the patience I had failed to bring to the first one.

Xuyên came to stand beside me.

"Phước called," she said.

"What did he offer?"

"To make sure the next load arrived without excitement."

"Did you accept?"

"I told him the driver was home. He didn't ask about that part."

She handed me the corrected list of losses.

"Pay this. Then we'll talk about next month."

I read every line before agreeing.

## 13. Hậu's Balance

Hậu asked me for money on a Thursday.

He said it so quickly that I knew he had spent hours deciding how slowly to say it. We were sitting in a small eating place near the room where he had recovered. His injuries had begun changing color. He could use both hands, though one still moved carefully when he reached for a glass.

"How much?" I asked.

He named a sum large enough to be deliberate.

"For what?"

"To pay somebody back."

"Who?"

He looked toward the street.

"You said I could ask."

"I didn't say I wouldn't."

The owner brought our food. Hậu waited until she had gone. He had chosen a table close to the road, where passing engines would make it difficult for anyone to hear a whole sentence. I wondered whether he was protecting me, himself, or the person whose name he hadn't said.

"Phước," he told me.

I set down my spoon.

"When?"

"After you went to the yard. While you were in hospital."

"He called you."

"Someone called first. Then him."

"What did he want?"

Hậu rubbed one thumb against the side of his glass. The movement left a clean place in the condensation.

"To know whether you were going to keep the company. Whether you needed somebody to help you. Whether you had people you trusted."

"What did you say?"

"That I didn't know."

"Was that before or after he paid?"

His face changed.

"Before."

I let the word remain where he had put it.

He said, "He knew about Linh. He knew what she owed for the course. He knew my room. He said people like you help someone and then become busy. He said he could give me work that didn't involve collecting."

"And what did the work involve?"

"Listening."

"To me."

"To people around the yard."

"For him."

Hậu looked at the food he had not touched.

"Yes."

I wanted to stand up. I wanted to take the table and put it somewhere the conversation couldn't continue. The urge was physical, immediate, embarrassingly simple. I could feel how much easier it would be to do something with my hands than to remain seated while Hậu explained what he had sold.

So I kept my palms flat on my knees.

"How much did you tell him?"

"That Sơn was bringing people back. That Vân was sorting wages. That your wife didn't like the other businesses."

My fingers closed.

Hậu saw it.

"I didn't tell him where she worked."

"He already knows."

"Then I didn't give him anything he couldn't—"

"Finish that sentence carefully."

He stopped.

The owner looked over from the counter. I lowered my voice.

"You don't get to decide a thing is harmless because he might have learned it another way."

"I know."

"Why are you telling me now?"

"Because he asked about the drivers. Which ones would leave if somebody offered more. Which ones were angry with you. Which ones had family who needed help."

"And that was different?"

"Yes."

"Explain."

Hậu looked at me at last.

"Because I knew what would happen next. I could pretend I was talking about business before that. I couldn't keep pretending when he asked which man's mother was ill."

I sat back. The anger did not leave. It became more useful, acquiring edges I could think around.

"You spent the money."

"Some."

"On Linh?"

"On the room after you said I could choose another. On clothes. On what I owed her. I told her I had work."

"Does she know now?"

"She's coming."

I looked toward the road.

"You invited her here?"

"I asked her to hear me say it. If I told her afterward, I'd make it smaller."

For the first time since he named Phước, I believed the decision in front of me belonged to Hậu.

Linh arrived carrying a tote bag and a helmet. She was in her twenties, with her brother's eyes and none of his habit of lowering them. She put the helmet on an empty chair, looked at my face, and asked whether Hậu had told me.

"Most of it," I said.

"Good. Now tell him you're not paying."

Hậu closed his eyes.

"Linh—"

"No. You keep finding a new person to owe because it lets you postpone being the person who made the first decision."

"You don't understand how he asks."

"I understand how you answer. I've been hearing it since I was seventeen."

She sat beside him. The owner brought another bowl without being asked. Linh thanked her and waited until she was gone.

"He paid me back," she told me. "Some money I'd lent him. Then he asked for half of it again because something had changed. Something always changes in a direction that requires my account."

Hậu stared at the table.

"I wanted to get out."

"Then stop hiring people to open the door while you keep a foot inside."

I looked between them. There was a whole life in the way they argued, years of rooms and meals and excuses I had entered halfway through. I had carried a man from a warehouse and thought the difficult part began there. Linh had been carrying versions of him for much longer without anyone calling her extraordinary.

"What work do you do?" I asked her.

"Accounts. Mostly small businesses. Classes at night when my brother's schedule of disasters permits it."

"She'd be finished if—" Hậu began.

"Don't turn my life into a list of things you almost helped with."

He stopped.

She looked at me.

"He says you aren't like them."

"Sometimes I am."

That surprised her enough to make her study me again.

"Then tell him which times. He needs the warning."

I turned to Hậu.

"I was going to hurt you when you mentioned Shawna."

He didn't move.

"I know."

"I don't want you listening to private conversations around my family. I don't want you working where Vân keeps the accounts. I don't want you promising anyone that I will pay because they know your name."

He nodded.

"And I won't pay Phước to buy the information back. He already has it."

"Then he'll come for me."

"He may."

Linh watched my face without blinking.

"I will tell him that hurting either of you will cost him," I said. "That is a threat I can choose to make. It is not a balance you owe me."

"And what does Hậu owe?" she asked.

"Whatever he agrees to repay from money he earns. No family attached. No work he hasn't accepted. He can make that offer or refuse it."

"Phước won't accept."

"Then he can tell me himself."

Hậu rubbed both hands over his face, carefully around the places that still hurt.

"I keep ending up here."

"You walked here this time," I said. "Keep that part."

We finished the food. It had gone cold. Nobody complained.

Afterward Linh went back to work. She refused money for the meal because she had already paid at the counter while I was on the telephone. Hậu watched her leave with an expression I understood better than I wanted to.

"Dũng won't see me," he said.

"I know."

"I want to explain what happened."

"He was there for most of it."

"Not the envelope."

"You want him to know you tried."

Hậu nodded.

"Does he need to know, or do you need him to know?"

He looked away. "You're starting to sound like somebody's father."

"Mine's worse."

"I'd like to meet him."

"No, you wouldn't. You're having a difficult enough week."

He laughed, briefly. Then he asked whether there was work he could do that did not put him near money or people's families.

I told him to speak to Sơn about collecting abandoned tools for the workers who did not want to return. Hậu knew where many of them had gone. He would ask before visiting. He would take somebody with him. He would not describe the visits as collection.

"They'll hate seeing me," he said.

"Some will."

"And if they send me away?"

"You leave the tools and go."

He considered that for a long time before agreeing.

I called Phước after Hậu left.

"Your new employee resigned," I said.

"Which one?"

"Hậu."

"He doesn't work for me."

"Then you'll have no objection to hearing less from him."

Phước sighed, almost fondly.

"You keep gathering people who need things. Then you become offended when they need something you haven't thought to provide."

"Don't contact his sister again."

"I haven't threatened her."

"I didn't ask whether you had. I told you to stop."

"You are very comfortable making rules for businesses you don't own."

"You were comfortable asking which driver's mother was ill. We've both made mistakes. Yours can end today."

There was a pause.

"And the money?"

"Hậu will make an offer. You can answer it. You don't get a person as security."

"That is not how every obligation works."

"It's how this one works."

He laughed softly.

"You don't know how much of the Circle already belongs to me."

"Then tell the Circle. See who agrees."

I ended the call and went home to tell Shawna what Hậu had said.

She was sitting on the floor sorting fabric samples into boxes. I joined her carefully. She heard me out, then put down the sample in her hand.

"You wanted to hit him."

"Yes."

"Because he talked about me."

"Yes."

"I appreciate the affection. I don't need it delivered through somebody else's jaw."

"I didn't."

"Good. That's the part I'm appreciating."

I picked up a sample and put it in a box. She removed it and put it in another.

"Different weight," she said.

I tried again.

She watched me get the next one right.

"Do you still trust him?"

"With some things."

"That sounds more useful than trusting him with everything because you saved him."

I looked at the boxes. "Linh thinks I shouldn't pay."

"Linh sounds tired of men making each other's decisions affordable."

I sat with that while we worked.

Later, Hậu sent me a photograph of a pair of tools on a doorstep. He had delivered them and left before the owner came out. There was no caption asking whether he had done enough.

I put the phone away and helped my wife finish the boxes.

## 14. The Closed Room

Dần's gambling room occupied the upper floor of a business that looked less profitable from the street than it was after midnight.

He met me on the stairs. His smile arrived before the rest of him.

"You should have called."

"I did. Yesterday. You said tonight."

"Of course. I meant before you came."

"Then you'd have to invent a third time."

He laughed and led me upstairs.

The room was cooler than the street. There were tables, soft chairs, a bar that sold very little, and people who had dressed to make losing money look intentional. A woman at the desk looked at Dần before allowing me through. He gave a small nod. I noticed that everyone who worked there was watching for the same movement.

"Busy," I said.

"People like familiar places."

"Even after their owner disappears."

"Especially then."

He took me to a small office behind the main room. An envelope lay on the desk. He had prepared it before claiming to be surprised by my arrival.

I sat and opened it.

"Bảo's share," he said.

"What does that pay for?"

"The usual arrangement."

"I'm new. Explain the usual arrangement."

He looked at the closed door.

"People who make trouble are dealt with. Disputes are settled. Money owed is recovered. Nobody opens a competing room across the lane and tells our clients they're safer there."

"And if they do?"

"We call."

"Who?"

"We used to call Vinh."

I looked at the envelope again. It contained a week's share, larger than I expected and smaller than Dần wanted me to believe was possible.

"How often did Vinh come?"

"When he needed something."

"Then he wasn't working for this money."

Dần smiled carefully. "That's one interpretation."

I put the envelope back on the desk.

"I'll take the share we agree. You can tell me when you want out of the arrangement. You don't offer me somebody's family when a player owes you money. You don't bring somebody here because they can't refuse."

"And if they lose voluntarily?"

"Then they owe you what they agreed to lose. We discuss how you recover it without pretending their sister was at the table."

He sat back.

"You make it sound almost respectable."

"It isn't. I'm making it clear."

"Bảo liked clear things too."

"He liked clear answers to questions he hadn't asked."

Dần looked at the envelope. "And what do I get when someone refuses your version of clarity?"

"Me."

He had been waiting for that answer. I saw the relief and disliked how much I enjoyed providing it.

Outside, a voice rose above the low noise of the tables. Dần looked toward the door with the exhausted expression of a man whose difficult employee had arrived at the worst possible moment.

"Mẫn," he said.

"Employee?"

"Sometimes."

"That's becoming a popular job description."

We went back into the main room.

Mẫn stood beside a card table with one hand on a dealer's shoulder. He was broad, handsome in a heavy way, and drunk enough to want people to notice that he was still coordinated. The dealer sat very still. A stack of chips lay scattered across the felt.

"Count again," Mẫn said.

"I did."

"Then count fucking better."

The dealer looked at Dần. Dần looked at me.

I approached the table.

"Take your hand off him."

Mẫn turned. His eyes moved over my shirt, the bandage visible at the wrist, the way I stood with one leg straighter than the other.

"I'm discussing a mistake."

"Use your mouth."

He smiled. "You must be the new one."

"You've got one finger left before this becomes expensive."

He took his hand away slowly, leaving it in the air as though he had merely changed the direction of a gesture.

"I heard you were good with numbers."

"They're easier than people."

"Then sit. Tell us what the mistake is."

I looked at the dealer. He described the last few hands and the amount Mẫn believed had been paid incorrectly. Two players confirmed part of it. A third kept quiet until I asked him directly whether he had been awake for the same game.

"It's correct," I told Mẫn.

"You weren't here."

"You all agree on what happened. You only disagree on whether arithmetic has to apologize when you lose."

Somebody laughed. Mẫn looked toward the sound and the laughter stopped.

"Sit," he said again.

"Why?"

"Because you look comfortable telling other people what their money means. Let's see you use yours."

Dần began to intervene. I held up a hand.

"Clear the old dispute first."

Mẫn pushed the disputed chips toward the dealer.

"There. Happy?"

"He is. I'm curious."

I sat.

We played. The room did what rooms always did when they expected violence and were offered a game instead: it moved closer while pretending not to. People who had been absorbed in their own hands suddenly needed drinks from a bar behind my chair. Two staff members stood where they could see both exits.

Mẫn played aggressively. He wanted each decision to force me into an answer about courage rather than money. It was a familiar mistake. People often brought the argument they wanted to have and expected the cards to support it.

I watched him, the other players, the dealer, the small involuntary movements that followed each new piece of information. My mind kept the cards without effort. It had always done that with things I bothered to look at. The harder work was choosing how much confidence to show while the numbers changed.

I won the first hand.

I lost the second on purpose only in the sense that I declined to pay more to discover what I already believed. Mẫn celebrated it as if I had apologized.

On the third, he tried to make me look at his face while his hands gave away what he wanted. I watched both.

By the sixth, there was enough money on the table that Dần had stopped smiling.

"You can end it," he said quietly.

"So can he."

Mẫn heard me.

"Afraid I'm going to lose more than you can collect?"

"I'm afraid you'll keep blaming the furniture."

He pushed more into the middle.

The last card was not something I could guarantee. I knew what remained possible, what had already gone, and how much Mẫn wanted me to believe. None of that entitled me to a favorable piece of paper.

It came anyway.

For a moment I felt almost insulted by the ease of it. Then I remembered hanging from the truck by three fingers and decided luck was allowed to be useful twice in one month.

I laid down the hand.

Mẫn stared at it.

"No," he said.

"That's a fascinating response to a card."

"You saw something."

"Several things. Most of them were in front of me."

He stood so quickly his chair struck the woman behind him. She cried out and stepped away. I rose before he could decide that hurting someone nearby improved his position.

"Sit down or leave."

He reached for the dealer.

I caught his arm halfway across the table.

He swung at me with the other hand. I turned enough to let it pass and put him face down against the felt. Chips jumped beneath his chest. The table shifted several inches across the floor.

"You lost," I said.

He tried to push himself up.

I pressed one finger between his shoulder blades. The wood beneath the felt gave a small, sharp crack.

He stopped moving.

Around us, the room had become silent.

"You can stand when you understand that everyone here saw it."

"Fuck you."

"That isn't understanding. That's a weather report. Try again."

He lay there breathing hard. I waited. The dealer kept both hands flat in front of him, staring at a point just beyond Mẫn's head.

At last Mẫn said, "I lost."

I let him up.

He stepped back from the table. His face had gone dark with humiliation. I knew men who could absorb a broken bone more easily than an audience.

"Who do you work for now?" I asked.

He looked at Dần.

"Answer him," Dần said.

Mẫn said, "People who pay."

"Then get paid somewhere else tonight."

He left with two men who had been pretending not to know him. I watched all three go.

The woman struck by the chair had bruised her ankle. Dần had someone help her sit. I asked whether she wanted a doctor. She said she wanted the man who hit her chair to stop coming there.

"He won't," Dần said.

I looked at him until I knew he meant it.

Back in the office, he poured himself a drink and did not offer me one.

"That will cost us clients."

"Which clients?"

"The ones who like him."

"They can go with him."

"Easy to say when you're collecting the share."

I picked up the envelope.

"I'm collecting less than Bảo did. The difference buys you room to choose. Use it."

He looked at the money in my hand.

"And your winnings?"

"Pay the dealer what Mẫn tried to take from him. Pay for the woman's visit if she wants one. The rest is mine."

"You don't want to make a gesture?"

"I made one. You need a new table."

Dần laughed despite himself. Then his face became serious.

"Mẫn has been meeting people from Phước's concerns. He says you're temporary."

"Does he?"

"He says you can break a room but you can't own everyone who passes through it."

"For once we agree."

"He means he'll bring them back."

I put the envelope inside my jacket.

"Then call me before they arrive."

Downstairs, the night air felt warm after the cooled room. I stood beside my car and counted what I had taken. It was money from a business I could not make lawful by improving its manners. People had lost it because they wanted to win more. Dần had paid me because he wanted someone more frightening than the men who frightened him.

I had wanted that job.

I still did.

At home, Shawna was awake. She looked at the envelope, then at my face.

"Gambling?"

"The room's share. Some winnings."

"Did you play?"

"Yes."

"Did you have to?"

"No."

She nodded, accepting the answer without approving it.

"Keep it separate from the atelier," she said.

"I will."

"And wash. You smell like expensive people making cheap decisions."

I put the money away and went to the tap.

## 15. Names on the Land

The notice arrived before the replacement jack.

Vân read it once, called someone, read it again, and put it on my desk with the expression of a woman delivering a dead animal someone else had insisted was a pet.

"The land arrangement is being challenged," she said.

"By Phước."

"By a concern he influences. He'll object to the shorter sentence."

I read the notice. It required nothing immediately except attention, which was how expensive problems often introduced themselves. There were dates, references, and enough uncertainty to make any customer reconsider leaving goods in our yard.

"Does he have a claim?"

"He has a claim worth examining. That's what makes this useful to him. Not every paper he sends is false."

I leaned back.

The fan clicked once as it turned. Outside, Ngọc argued with the supplier delivering the new jack. She wanted the demonstration before signing for it. The driver wanted a signature before unloading. They had reached the stage where both were saying the same sentence with more syllables.

"We can't stop work every time he asks a question," I said.

"We also can't assume an answer because you dislike the man asking."

I looked at Vân.

"You enjoy saying things like that."

"Only when they remain necessary."

We spent the morning with people whose work was to distinguish a claim from an instruction and an instruction from something that could actually be enforced. I paid for their time. I listened. I asked questions when the answers became too comfortable with words that did not name who would lose money.

By noon, I understood enough to know that I could not solve the matter by writing a large number on a small piece of paper. Trinh's interest was real. The land was another problem. Bảo had allowed both problems to overlap because it made every person involved need him to interpret the overlap.

Now he was unavailable, and Phước had begun charging for the interpretation.

Sơn came in after the meeting.

"The workers want to talk."

"About the notice?"

"About the people who came after it."

Three drivers had been offered work at another yard. Two mechanics had received calls. The offers were good enough to be real. They included an explanation that our place might close while the new manager argued over rights he did not yet hold.

"Are they going?" I asked.

"They want to know whether they're allowed to tell you they're considering it."

I put the notice down.

"Get everyone who wants to speak."

We met beneath the largest repair roof. A few men stayed near their machines, close enough to hear without making the meeting feel like a commitment. Ngọc stood with her arms folded. Lộc remained near the back. Hậu had come to return tools and looked as if he wished he had chosen another afternoon.

Sơn explained the notice as clearly as he could. Vân corrected one point, then stopped herself from correcting a second because the first version was accurate enough for people who needed to decide whether they would be paid next week.

I told them what money was available for wages and what work was confirmed. I did not include orders I hoped to win.

A driver named Hải raised his hand. I asked him not to wait for permission if he had something to say.

"Will there be another fight?"

"I don't know."

He nodded once, unhappy but unsurprised.

"That's why I'm leaving. I have two places that need money from me every month. Neither can wait while you find out."

"When?"

"End of the week."

"Tell Sơn what needs handing over. You'll be paid for what you've worked."

He looked at the other drivers.

"That's all?"

"That's all."

Ngọc said, "And if the other place belongs to Phước?"

"Then he still needs a driver."

Lộc shifted behind her. I watched him hear an answer he had not expected me to give.

Another man asked whether I would pay a higher rate to people who stayed. Vân began to answer, but I stopped her gently.

"If the work supports a higher rate, we'll discuss it. I won't pay extra for pretending you're not frightened."

"It's not pretending," he said. "We'd be taking a risk."

"Then tell me which work carries it and we'll price the work. Staying loyal is too vague to put on your wage slip."

He considered that. Ngọc nodded, slowly.

The older loader who had challenged me at the gate stood from a low stool. His name was Mười. I had learned it properly while Vân settled the wages.

"You broke him," he said, pointing toward the place Bảo's office had stood. "Now someone else will come. Then you break him. What happens when the thing between you is one of us?"

Nobody looked away from me.

"I try to move you before I strike," I said.

"We shouldn't have to wait for that."

"You're right."

"Then stop bringing them."

I could have explained that Phước had wanted the yard before I entered it, that Bảo had built the obligations, that the men who locked the gate had not asked for my assistance. All true. None answered what Mười had asked.

"I can't promise they'll stop coming," I said. "I can tell you that if the yard stops being a place you choose to work, I've failed at the first thing I offered. You can leave now. You can come back later. Neither will add a balance to your name."

Mười looked at Sơn, not at me.

"Write that where people can read it."

Sơn said he would.

The meeting ended unevenly. Some people returned to work. Some stayed to ask Vân questions. Hải spoke quietly with another driver about the new place. Hậu waited until most of the yard had moved away before approaching me.

"You wanted him to stay," he said.

"Yes."

"You looked angry."

"I was."

"He saw it."

I watched Hải collect papers from Sơn.

"I didn't stop him."

"He'll remember your face longer than the sentence."

I turned toward Hậu. "Are you working for Phước again, or have you become expensive for free?"

He almost smiled.

"Tools are delivered. Nobody wanted to hit me today. I'm celebrating."

"That's a low bar."

"I'm clearing it carefully."

Shawna called before I could answer.

Her event venue had canceled.

The reason was a booking dispute. Then it was maintenance. Then the manager apologized for having used the wrong explanation and said a previous arrangement had priority. She had asked for the explanation in writing and received something that did not match any of the three spoken versions.

"Who owns it?" I asked.

"I knew you'd ask that before whether I'm all right."

I closed my eyes.

"Are you?"

"Furious. Fully operational. Who owns it is the second question, and the answer involves a man whose assistant has already called me to say this could be resolved over dinner."

"Phước."

"His assistant didn't put that on the invitation."

"I'll handle it."

"No. You will tell me what you intend before you handle anything with my name on it."

I looked at the notice on my desk through the office window.

"He's putting pressure on the yard. This is part of it."

"Then he can learn that my business is a different fucking address."

"How?"

"I'm finding another venue. I'm documenting what this one did. I'm calling the clients myself before somebody gives them a version in which I forgot how calendars work. What are you doing?"

"Coming to see you."

"Bring food. Anger is not a lunch."

At the atelier, her assistants had moved the whole schedule across a wall covered in paper. Shawna stood in front of it with a pen between her teeth, looking for places where one change would pull five others loose. She removed the pen when I entered.

"Nobody is canceling," she said. "Two clients offered spaces. One is too small. One is beautiful and has the electrical capacity of a fucking candle. We'll find something."

I set down the food.

"You shouldn't have to."

"Correct. Help me with what I have to do anyway."

I spent two hours measuring spaces from photographs and plans she had already obtained, comparing costs, calling people only after she approved the call. It was work I could do well. It also forced me to wait for other people's information rather than filling the gaps with certainty.

By evening she had two possible alternatives.

Then Phước called me.

"I hear your wife's event has met a complication."

I stepped outside.

"You don't get to speak about her as a complication."

"I'm offering a solution. The original venue remains available under the right arrangement."

"Then tell the manager to honor the booking."

"Let's discuss the yard first."

For several seconds I said nothing. I watched a little boy across the street try to spin a bicycle wheel while his father held the frame. The boy kept touching the spokes before they stopped. His father moved his hand away each time, patiently, without making the lesson larger than it needed to be.

"Tomorrow," Phước said. "I have a meeting outside the city. You can come with your people. We'll make the positions clear."

"Send the address."

"Good. I knew we could be practical."

"We're going to be very practical."

I went back inside and told Shawna exactly what had been said.

She listened, then tore the old venue's name from the planning wall.

"We're not going back there," she said.

"Even if he restores it?"

"Especially then. I am not wearing his fucking favor at my own show."

I looked at the torn paper in her hand.

"I'll go to the meeting."

"I know."

"I'll call before and after."

"And during, if somebody asks you to behave like an idiot. I can provide a competing opinion."

I took her hand. She let the paper fall into the bin and held mine with both of hers.

"Don't make me another reason you enjoy hurting someone," she said.

I promised I would remember the difference.

## 16. The Last Vehicle

Phước chose a meeting room attached to a warehouse outside the city. He said the other owners would be there.

Trinh declined to attend until she saw an agenda. Vân declined to attend until she saw documents. I began to understand why sensible people survived long enough to charge for their time.

I went with Sơn and Bình.

"You don't need to come," I told Sơn.

"You're going to discuss whether my people have a place to work. I should hear what you promise."

"I can tell you afterward."

"That's how Bảo did it."

He won the argument without raising his voice.

Bình drove. A second car followed with two men Dần recommended after I asked for people who could listen longer than they could boast. Their names were Quang and Hiếu. Both had worked security for years. Neither pretended that made the journey safe.

Before leaving, I called Shawna. She had secured a different venue for the show. It needed work, but the owner had given her a direct answer and a real date.

"I don't need his room," she said.

"I'll tell him."

"Don't make it a message from me. He doesn't deserve correspondence."

I smiled.

"Call after," she said.

The meeting began forty minutes late and lasted twenty-three.

Phước had brought two representatives whose employers existed mostly in sentences that grew longer when challenged. Neither had authority to settle the matters they wanted me to accept. Both could explain, at length, why delaying my acceptance would create further costs.

Sơn listened for ten minutes before asking whether anyone intended to discuss the actual work done at the yard.

One representative said operational details were not relevant to the ownership position.

"The place operates," Sơn replied. "That's why there's a position to own."

Phước smiled as if a child had asked a pleasing question.

I stopped him before he answered.

"Talk to him properly."

The smile disappeared.

"I invited you to resolve a dispute."

"Then bring people who can resolve it."

"You don't like the terms."

"You brought me a surrender and called it a discussion. I've seen the dress before. It fit Kiệt badly too."

Phước folded his hands.

"You have money. You have force. You are discovering that those don't automatically become title. I am offering a position you can hold."

"A position you can take back whenever I disagree."

"A position within a structure."

"Build it without me."

I stood. Sơn gathered the papers we had brought and left theirs untouched.

At the door, Phước said, "Tell Shawna the original room remains available."

I turned.

"She has another."

"At what cost?"

"Less than listening to you explain generosity."

We left him seated between the men who could not decide anything.

Bình was waiting with the engine running. Quang and Hiếu followed us out in the second car. We were several turns from the warehouse before Sơn spoke.

"He wanted you angry."

"He succeeded."

"He wanted you to do something in that room."

"He should have brought better furniture."

Sơn didn't smile. He was looking behind us.

The road narrowed where work had begun on a drainage channel. Temporary barriers stood beside an open strip of earth. Beyond them, low industrial buildings gave way to scrub and unfinished foundations. It was an ugly, ordinary place to be delayed.

Bình slowed before the car ahead of us stopped.

"That's wrong," he said.

I saw the second vehicle move behind Quang's car. Then a man stepped from the first one holding something long against his leg.

There was no useful moment in which to pretend it might be harmless.

"Down," I said.

The first shot broke the window beside Sơn.

Bình turned the wheel and drove into the soft edge of the road. The car tilted. Another shot struck the rear. I pushed Sơn below the window line and felt glass pass across the back of my hand.

Behind us, Quang's car hit the stopped vehicle hard enough to move it. He had chosen impact over remaining still. It gave Bình a little more room and cost Quang the front of his car.

I saw Hiếu come out low, pulling at Quang's door.

Then the man with the long gun turned toward them.

I kicked my own door open and moved.

The ground outside fell farther than I expected. My foot struck the edge of the drainage cut. For a fraction of a second, the injured knee gave way and I was falling sideways toward the concrete lip.

I caught the barrier with one hand. It tore free from its base. I let its weight continue moving and used it to turn my body clear of the fall.

The gunman saw me come up holding a section of steel fence.

He fired.

Something struck the metal near my face. Another round tore through the space below my arm. I swung the barrier across his body and felt the impact through both shoulders. He went backward into the stopped car. The gun fell beneath it.

I left him there and turned toward Quang.

Hiếu had the door open. Quang's leg was trapped beneath the dashboard. Blood ran down the side of his face. He was still trying to reach for something near his seat.

"Leave it," I told him.

"There's another—"

I heard the shot before he finished.

Hiếu fell against the car. His hand stayed on Quang's sleeve for a moment, then slipped away.

I looked down the road.

Mẫn stood beside the rear vehicle with a pistol in his hand.

He looked pleased to be recognized.

"No table tonight," he called.

I moved behind Quang's open door. Mẫn fired again. The door jumped against my shoulder.

"Can you pull?" I asked Quang.

"My leg."

"When I lift."

I put my hands beneath the bent section trapping him. The first effort moved nothing. The second sent pain through my side where the old wound had not finished deciding whether it was closed.

I pushed harder.

Metal rose. Quang screamed and pulled himself clear. I caught him before he struck the road and dragged him behind the car.

Hiếu lay where he had fallen. I touched his neck. There was a pulse, faint and fast.

"Stay with him," I told Quang.

"How?"

"Talk. Keep him here."

I looked toward Bình's car. Sơn had crawled out on the far side. Bình was moving toward us, holding a heavy tool he had taken from the rear compartment. He stopped when he saw Hiếu.

"Ambulance," I said.

"Sơn's calling."

Two men came from the front vehicle. One had a knife. The other carried a gun low, trying to see around the car without exposing himself. They were not boys playing at a frightening occupation. They moved with the confidence of adults who had done versions of this before.

I stood where they could see me.

The man with the knife hesitated. The other raised the gun.

I threw the torn barrier into him.

It struck his upper body and took him off his feet. I crossed the space behind it and caught the knife arm before the first man decided whether to advance or run. He twisted toward me. I drove his hand against the car until the blade fell, then struck him once beneath the jaw.

He collapsed without protecting his head.

Behind me, Bình shouted.

I turned and saw Mẫn moving toward Sơn.

There was too much space between us. My mind counted it with infuriating precision. I knew how long a step would take, how far Mẫn needed to turn, how much of Sơn remained visible beside the tilted car. The answer was not favorable.

So I changed the question.

I ran at the stopped vehicle between us, planted one foot against its front wheel, and drove upward. The suspension compressed. The car rose beneath me and threw part of the force back.

I went over the roof.

For one instant the road lay flat beneath me, every person fixed inside a shape I could see completely. Mẫn looking up. Sơn beginning to turn. Bình with the tool in both hands. Hiếu's blood dark against the pale dust beside Quang's knee.

I landed on Mẫn before he finished raising the pistol.

We struck the road together. The gun fired into the empty ground beside us. His elbow broke under my weight. He tried to bite my face. I put one hand across his mouth and pushed his head against the concrete.

"You should have left when you lost," I said.

He made a sound through my fingers.

I wanted to press harder.

Then Bình's tool struck a man behind me, and I remembered there were other people in the problem.

I took Mẫn's gun and moved away from him.

The remaining men had begun retreating toward the vehicles. One pulled the first gunman upright. Another tried to get a door open with a hand that no longer worked properly. I could have reached them before they left.

Hiếu needed an ambulance. Quang needed help. Sơn stood with blood running from his upper arm, trying to tell someone on the telephone which stretch of road we were on.

I let the men go.

Mẫn stayed on the ground, holding his broken arm against his chest. He watched the vehicle leave without him. The expression on his face changed from pain to disbelief, then to a fear too private to perform for an audience.

I put the empty weapon out of reach and went to Hiếu.

Bình knelt beside him. He had taken off his shirt and was doing what the voice on Sơn's telephone told him to do. I followed the same instructions. They were simple enough to obey and difficult enough that obeying them occupied all the strength I had left.

Hiếu opened his eyes once.

"Quang?"

"Here," Quang said. "I'm here. Stop worrying about my fucking car."

Hiếu tried to smile. He couldn't make it hold.

The emergency vehicles arrived while we were still speaking to him. People took our places with practiced hands and asked us to move. I stepped back and discovered that my own legs were shaking.

Sơn sat on the verge. His shirt sleeve was wet with blood. He looked up at me.

"Call your wife."

"You first."

"I called the neighbor. Diễm's there."

The same neighbor. The same daughter waiting while a man who promised a different arrangement stood beside another broken vehicle.

I called Shawna.

When she answered, I could not make the first sentence clever.

"There was an attack."

She went quiet.

"Are you alive?"

"Yes."

"Who isn't?"

I looked toward the ambulance.

"I don't know yet."

"Tell me where."

I did.

She said she would meet us at the hospital. She did not tell me she had work to rearrange. I could hear her already moving through the room, giving instructions that had nothing to do with what she wanted to be doing.

Mẫn was taken away separately. As they lifted him, he called my name.

I went close enough to hear.

"You think he wanted you dead?" he said.

I looked at the road, at the glass, at the people still working around Hiếu.

"I think you did."

Mẫn showed his teeth.

"He wanted you tired."

I stood there after they carried him away, letting the words settle into the shape of what had happened.

Phước had not needed the whole road. He had needed me to arrive at its end with fewer people willing to travel beside me.

## 17. A Driver Missing

Hiếu died before morning.

Quang told me because he had asked the staff to tell him first. They had worked together long enough that he believed there were still duties attached to being the man in the driver's seat, even when he could no longer stand without help.

"He kept asking whether I got out," Quang said.

I sat beside the bed.

"You did."

"He knew. He'd seen me. He kept asking anyway."

There was a bandage around Quang's leg and another beneath his hair. He had placed Hiếu's watch on the table beside him. The face was scratched. One hand had stopped between numbers.

"I'll have it repaired," I said.

"His wife can decide."

I nodded.

For a while we listened to the sounds beyond the curtain. Someone asked for water. Someone complained that a charger had disappeared. Ordinary requests continued making their way through a building that had failed to give us the one thing we wanted.

"I recommended the road," Quang said.

"They chose where to stop us."

"I could have stayed farther back."

"Perhaps."

He looked at me sharply. He had expected me to disagree harder.

"I could have done things differently too," I said. "We'll learn what we can. We won't get him back by taking turns inventing a version where we were cleverer."

Quang picked up the watch, turned it once, and set it down again.

"I want work when I can do it."

"You'll have it."

"Not because you feel guilty."

"I will feel guilty whether you work or not. That's my problem."

He laughed once, bitterly.

"You're a cheerful fucking employer."

"Ask the payroll department. They have concerns."

He looked toward the curtain. "I'm going to have to tell her what happened."

"I'll be there if she wants me."

"She might not."

"Then I won't."

Hiếu's wife did want to see me. Her name was Ly. She asked practical questions first: where his things were, what happened to the car, who had called his parents. Then she asked whether he had been frightened.

I told her he had tried to help Quang. I could not know whether fear had been absent from that.

She accepted the answer with a stillness that made me wish she had shouted.

"He said this would be a meeting," she told me.

"I thought it would be."

"He believed you."

"Yes."

She looked at the watch in her hand.

"Then remember what that cost."

I paid what needed paying. I made arrangements that would remain after people stopped arriving with envelopes. I did not put the Circle's name on any of it. Ly had enough names to carry without mine asking to be read beside her husband's.

Three days after the attack, Bình disappeared.

He had gone to collect documents relating to Xuyên's damaged load. Tâm expected him at nine. By eleven, his wife had called Sơn. By noon, nobody could produce a clear account of who had arranged the collection.

At one, Lộc stopped saying he didn't know.

We were in Vân's office. Sơn had one arm in a sling. Ngọc stood by the window, listening to the yard grow louder as the news traveled from person to person.

"I gave him the contact," Lộc said.

"Whose?" I asked.

"A man who handled deliveries for the old company."

"A name."

He gave it.

Sơn closed his eyes.

"That's Phước's carrier."

"He said the papers were ready."

"He said that before they held Bình the first time."

Lộc spread his good hand over the desk.

"I couldn't know they'd do it again."

I looked at him until he stopped defending the sentence.

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"What exactly?"

"Whether there would be any difficulty."

"And he said no."

Lộc nodded.

"How convenient for everybody except the man you sent."

Ngọc turned from the window.

"Stop talking about him like he's a package. Where is he?"

Lộc took out his phone. He called twice. Neither call was answered. On the third, he left a message that began politely and ended with a request to stop making him look like a fool.

"That's what you're worried about?" Sơn asked.

Lộc looked at him with sudden anger.

"I'm worried about a great many things. You think it's easy to remain here while everyone waits for the next person to decide I belong somewhere else?"

"Bình belongs at home," Sơn said. "Start there."

I sent Lộc outside and asked Hậu to come in.

He arrived with dust on his trousers from moving tools. He listened to the names, the address, the description of the man who had called. When I finished, he shook his head.

"They won't keep him at the place on the paper."

"Where?"

"I don't know yet."

"Can you find someone who does?"

He looked at Sơn.

"Maybe."

"No promises on my behalf," I said. "No money offered that you can't explain. No going into a room because someone says you'll be safe."

"You've removed most of my old techniques."

"Develop a new career."

He nodded and went outside to make a call.

Bình's wife arrived at three. Her name was Lan. She had left work without finishing what she was doing and still wore the apron. A relative waited at the gate with her bag. She came directly to Sơn because she knew him and did not yet trust the man whose name everyone kept saying.

I stayed back while they spoke.

When she turned to me, she asked why Bình had gone alone.

"He was collecting documents," I said.

"Wasn't he collecting documents when they kept him before?"

"Yes."

"Then why did anybody believe them?"

I had asked the same question more aggressively. Hearing it from her made the aggression feel less useful.

"We should have checked more carefully."

"Who is we?"

"The people managing this yard. Me."

She looked toward the office.

"Bring him back. You can explain the company afterward."

I promised to tell her what I learned as soon as I learned it. I did not promise that learning it would be enough.

Phước called while she was still there.

I answered with the speaker on after telling him who was in the room.

"There seems to be confusion about a driver," he said.

Lan stepped closer.

"His name is Bình. Where is he?"

Phước paused. He had expected my anger, not her question.

"He is safe. Some matters need clarification."

"Put him on the phone."

"He isn't with me."

"Then how do you know he's safe?"

Silence.

I looked at the telephone and almost smiled. Not because any part of it was funny, but because Lan had reached the weakness in his sentence faster than the men he paid to build stronger ones.

"Ask the people holding him," I said. "Call back when he can speak."

"You are making assumptions."

"I'm listening to his wife. You should try it."

I ended the call.

Lan sat down for the first time. Her hands trembled when Sơn gave her water. She spilled a little and apologized. Ngọc wiped it away without saying anything.

Hậu returned near evening with the name of a storage compound and the warning that he could not verify it himself. A former colleague had heard Bình arguing there. The man wanted nothing from me except not to be named in front of Lộc.

"Why Lộc?" I asked.

"He thinks Lộc tells Phước more than he admits."

Sơn looked at the door.

I kept my face still.

"We don't accuse him on that," I said.

"No," Hậu agreed. "We just stop assuming he's stupid every time being stupid helps the same man."

That sentence stayed with me.

We passed the information to the people already looking into Bình's disappearance. Lan made her own report. I did not ask her to keep quiet for the Circle's benefit. If the business could only survive by making her wait silently, I would have inherited more of Bảo than I intended.

But I also called Dần, Trinh, and the clients Phước had begun approaching.

I asked them to come to the yard the following evening.

"For what?" Dần asked.

"To hear what it costs when we each negotiate alone."

"That sounds like politics."

"Bring a calculator if it makes you feel better."

Shawna found me in the office after dark. She had brought clothes because I had not gone home to change after the hospital. She put them on the desk and stood beside the notice concerning the land.

"How many people are you carrying now?" she asked.

"I haven't counted."

"You count everything."

I looked at the door Lan had gone through an hour earlier.

"Not that."

She sat beside me. For a long time she did not ask me to leave, and I did not explain why I had to stay. We could hear Sơn speaking quietly to someone on the telephone in the next room. Outside, the last vehicle of the evening moved through the repaired gate.

When its engine faded, the yard felt too large.

## 18. Everyone at the Table

We held the meeting in a repair bay because the temporary office couldn't hold everybody and I had developed a dislike of rooms with only one useful door.

Ngọc moved the machinery back. Sơn found chairs that did not match. Vân put a clean sheet over a table and then removed it because she said we were discussing accounts, not concealing a body.

"Give it time," Dần said when he arrived.

"If that is a joke, improve it," Vân replied.

He sat where she pointed.

Trinh came with the people examining her proposed sale. Xuyên sent Tâm and arrived herself half an hour later, carrying the notebook with the elastic band. Lộc sat near the end of the table. Hậu stayed outside until Sơn told him he should hear what involved the people he had contacted.

There were no speeches about brotherhood. I did not ask anyone to trust me because a man had been taken. Most of them had lived long enough to distrust invitations that used an emergency to make disagreement embarrassing.

I began with the facts we could support.

Bình had gone to collect documents. He had not returned. Phước claimed to know he was safe while denying that Bình was with him. The same carrier had held Xuyên's goods against another concern's balance. Men connected with that carrier had appeared around the yard after the land notice. Mẫn had been present during the attack on the road.

"Connection is not instruction," Trinh said.

"No," I answered. "I'm not asking you to pretend it is."

"Then what are you asking?"

"Stop letting him discuss each of us as if the others have already agreed."

Xuyên opened her notebook.

"He told me Trinh was selling because she had accepted that the yard was finished."

Trinh looked at her.

"I'm selling because I prefer being paid to being held in an argument."

Dần said, "He told me the land would change hands and the new owners would want a different arrangement for the rooms."

"Which rooms?" one of Trinh's advisers asked.

Dần smiled without answering.

I said, "Some matters are separate from the ordinary transport business. We can keep the distinction without pretending it doesn't exist."

Vân gave me a brief look of approval. It disappeared when I looked pleased about earning it.

Lộc leaned forward.

"People make offers. That's business. You make offers too."

"Did you make one on Bình's behalf?" Sơn asked.

The table went quiet.

Lộc looked at me. I let him answer Sơn.

"I gave him a contact."

"You gave the same people another chance to keep him."

"I said I didn't know."

"You keep saying that as if not knowing was an accident you couldn't prevent."

Trinh put a hand on the table.

"We can decide whether Lộc is incompetent after we know where the driver is."

"Thank you," Lộc said bitterly.

"Don't thank me yet. Competence might be worse for you."

I looked at the papers before me. There were enough numbers to occupy an ordinary person for days. I could hold them all at once: claims, interests, payments, the cost of losing the yard for a month, the likely value of keeping its work together. The arithmetic was easy. Every difficult part had a person attached.

"You don't need to sell to me," I told Trinh. "You need a position that pays you without requiring Phước to approve your departure."

"Correct."

"Dần needs an arrangement he can understand and someone who answers when a man like Mẫn arrives. Xuyên needs deliveries that don't become collateral for strangers. Lộc needs his interest recognized before everyone decides he only brought liabilities. The workers need wages and the right to leave."

"And you?" Xuyên asked.

"I want the yard. I want the Circle's business. I want enough control that the people carrying out my decisions know whose decisions they are."

"You want a great deal," she said.

"I'm not applying for a small disappointment."

Dần laughed. Trinh almost did.

I put an offer on the table. It did not resolve every claim. It separated enough of them that Phước would have to argue with more than one person at a time. I would commit my money to the operating concern, with terms examined independently. The existing owners would decide what they sold. Xuyên's payments would be handled according to the work actually done for her. Workers' claims would not become a discount in the sale.

The illegal businesses would be discussed separately with the people who ran them.

"You can't make those clean by moving them to another page," Trinh said.

"I didn't say clean. I said separate. I won't make Sơn explain Dần's gambling receipts when he needs a tire order paid."

Sơn looked at Dần, then at me.

"That would save time."

Dần spread his hands. "Everybody becomes respectable when I enter the room. It's a gift."

We argued for two hours.

Trinh rejected the first repayment schedule. I rejected the condition she wanted in exchange. Xuyên wanted compensation for the second loss before discussing future work. I had already paid for the damaged goods; she meant the orders she had lost afterward. I asked for the figures. She produced them without smiling.

Lộc wanted authority over clients he brought back. Sơn refused to accept instructions that changed shifts without his knowledge. Ngọc said any agreement that made her test equipment after a client had promised a deadline would fail before the ink dried.

People grew angry. They stood, walked away, came back. Vân wrote only what someone actually agreed to. That made the pages fill more slowly than the room filled with words.

I had enough money to make several objections disappear. I refused to use it for all of them. If everyone stayed because I paid more than the next man, I would spend the rest of my life answering bids for my own company.

At last Trinh said, "This can be examined. I won't promise more tonight."

"That's enough for tonight," I said.

My phone rang.

Phước.

I told the table who it was before answering.

"You're busy," he said.

"Yes."

"I hear you've invited people to discuss property you don't own."

"They brought their own property. It made the chairs heavy."

"Put me on speaker."

"You're already there."

A pause. Then his voice changed, becoming warmer.

"Trinh. We should have spoken before this became a gathering."

"We spoke last week," she said. "You had many reasons I should wait. I became curious what happened while I did."

"And Xuyên. I understand you were inconvenienced."

"You understand too little," she replied.

Dần leaned back, enjoying the conversation until Phước said his name.

"You know how difficult it becomes when people forget old arrangements."

Dần's smile faded.

"I'm here to hear a new one."

Phước was silent for a moment.

Then he said, "Aiden has promised you an independence he cannot guarantee."

"I promised to name what I can guarantee," I said. "Start with Bình. Put him on the phone."

"That matter is being resolved."

"He's a man. Not a matter."

"You have a talent for making ordinary disagreements sound criminal."

"You have a driver who hasn't gone home. Put him on."

We heard movement. A muffled conversation. Then Bình's voice, rough and tired.

"Sơn?"

Sơn moved so quickly his chair fell backward.

"I'm here. Where are you?"

"They want me to sign something."

"What?"

"That the road was an accident. That we started—"

The voice disappeared.

Phước came back.

"He's confused. There have been several incidents involving your people. We're establishing the facts."

I looked around the table. Whatever doubts remained about the larger business, nobody looked confused about what we had just heard.

"He leaves now," I said.

"You will stop interfering with the settlement of the yard's obligations. You will suspend this gathering's attempts to transfer interests until all parties can be present. Then we can make sure nobody suffers from a misunderstanding."

Xuyên closed her notebook.

"I am present," she said. "I understand you very well."

Phước ignored her.

"Aiden. You can be sensible."

I thought of my father catching my hand. I thought of Shawna telling me to choose what I did with it. I thought of Bình standing beside a damaged truck, relieved to have called his wife.

"You have one hour to let him leave," I said.

"You don't give me—"

I ended the call.

For several seconds the room was quiet.

Then Vân said, "We need to preserve exactly what was said and tell the people handling the report."

Sơn was already calling Lan. Trinh's advisers began speaking quietly. Xuyên stood and went to the yard's edge, where she could make her own calls without adding to the noise.

Lộc stayed seated.

I looked at him.

"Do you know where?"

He shook his head.

"Look at me when you answer."

He did.

"I think I do," he said.

The room seemed to draw closer.

"Then start being useful."

He named a compound that matched Hậu's information.

Sơn turned toward him with an expression I had never seen on his face. For a moment I thought he would strike Lộc with his uninjured hand.

Instead he said, "You will tell Lan yourself how long you've known."

Lộc lowered his eyes.

Outside, rain began striking the corrugated roof. People moved the chairs away from a leak without ending the meeting. The table remained where it was, covered in unfinished agreements that had suddenly become easier to understand.

## 19. Night Collection

The hour passed.

Phước did not call back. Bình did not arrive. The people looking into his disappearance had the address and the account of the conversation, but nobody could promise that he would remain at that address while they worked.

At eleven, Hậu received a message from the former colleague who had first named the compound.

Vehicles were being prepared. Someone had told Bình to wash his face.

I stood up.

Sơn was already reaching for his keys.

"You stay with Lan," I told him.

"Don't start."

"She needs someone who'll answer when she asks where he is. Hậu comes because he knows the place. You stay because she knows you."

He looked at me, then at the phone in his hand.

"Call before you go in."

"I will."

"Don't make that another intention."

I called Shawna first. I told her what we knew and what I intended to do. She did not ask whether she could stop me. She asked who would be with me, whether the people already handling the report knew the updated information, and where she should wait for the next call.

"Home," I said.

"Then you know where to come."

Hậu sat beside me on the drive. He wore a plain shirt and carried nothing in his hands. He had refused money for coming.

"Don't look grateful," he said after a while. "It makes me feel like you're about to give a speech."

"I was thinking about the road."

"Good. Keep doing that."

The compound stood beyond a row of workshops, its outer wall patched where an older gate had been closed. Lights burned above an entrance wide enough for a truck. Two vehicles waited inside, one with its rear door open.

Hậu looked at the man beside the entrance.

"Chính," he said.

The man heard him and turned.

For several seconds neither moved. Then Chính came closer, looking at Hậu's healed bruises and the places where they had not healed cleanly.

"You shouldn't be here."

"I know."

"Who is he?"

Chính knew. He asked because he wanted Hậu to decide what answer would make the next minute possible.

"The man who paid Vinh," Hậu said. "The driver leaves with us."

Chính's eyes moved toward the open vehicle.

"It isn't my decision."

"Then don't make it harder."

"There are people inside."

"There are people outside too," I said. "Tell them I'm here."

He looked at me.

"You could wait."

"So could Bình. That's been the problem."

Chính went inside. Hậu watched him until he disappeared around the nearest building.

"He used to cover for me," he said.

"At work?"

"When I drank. When I didn't want to go to a house again. Sometimes he went instead. I called that helping me."

I looked at him.

"Was he the one who messaged you?"

Hậu nodded.

Chính returned with another man.

The newcomer had a square face and thick wrists. He wore a watch with a scratched metal band and carried no visible weapon. He stopped beneath the light and looked me over without pretending he was doing anything else.

"You can speak to him," he said. "Then you leave."

"He leaves too."

"After the papers."

"Then bring the papers outside. We can all read."

His mouth tightened.

"You think that makes you funny."

"I think it makes this slower than it needs to be."

He glanced at Hậu.

"You brought a collector to tell us how to treat someone."

"I brought a man who knows what you call it."

Hậu stayed beside me. He did not defend his old job. I respected that more than anything he might have said.

The man stepped away from the light.

"Come in, then."

I told Sơn where we were before following. I did it where the man could hear. He watched me end the call and looked irritated by the existence of people outside the compound who knew what he wanted kept inside it.

Bình sat in a room used for storing office furniture. There were chairs stacked against one wall and a cabinet with two drawers missing. A paper lay on the table before him. His right hand was swollen. His face looked cleaner than it had on the telephone, which made the bruises around his mouth easier to see.

When he saw Sơn wasn't with us, he looked at Hậu, then at me.

"Lan?"

"At the yard. Sơn's with her."

His shoulders lowered a fraction.

"They said she didn't want to hear from me until I sorted it out."

"They lied."

He looked down at the paper.

"I signed one."

"Then you'll tell people why."

The square-faced man shut the door behind us.

"He admitted what happened. You don't get to rewrite it because you dislike the answer."

I took the paper from the table. It described the attack on the road as an argument between drivers and made several people disappear from the account. Mẫn was not mentioned. Neither were the guns that had been brought before anybody argued.

I put it down.

"Stand up, Bình."

He pushed against the chair with his uninjured hand.

The man by the door stepped toward him.

I moved between them.

"Sit," the man said.

"He's finished sitting."

The first blow came from behind the stacked chairs.

I heard a shoe move and turned enough that the short club struck my upper arm rather than the back of my head. Pain burst through the old injury. I caught the club before the man could draw it back and pulled him out of hiding with it.

He hit the edge of the table and folded across it.

The square-faced man reached inside his shirt.

I drove the table into him.

It struck him below the ribs and trapped him against the wall. The drawer cabinet fell sideways. A shot went off somewhere beneath the noise, too close for me to know immediately where it had entered the room.

Hậu pulled Bình down beside the chairs.

I kept both hands on the table and pushed.

The man behind it tried to bring his weapon up. I could see the effort in his shoulder. I pushed harder until the table's edge bent and the wall behind him cracked along an old seam.

"Drop it."

He did not.

His arm came around the edge.

I struck him across the throat with the side of my hand.

He stopped fighting. The gun fell from fingers that had suddenly lost the instruction to hold it. I caught him as the table shifted away and lowered him to the floor.

He was breathing badly.

I called for help, then looked toward the door.

Chính stood there with a pistol held in both hands.

Hậu rose between us.

"Don't," I said to him.

He didn't move away.

"Chính. You called me."

Chính's hands shook.

"I didn't call him."

"You knew who'd come."

"I didn't know this."

"Then stop it here."

Chính looked at the man on the floor. He looked at Bình crouched beside the chairs. He looked at me, standing with blood on my sleeve and a piece of broken table beneath one foot.

"They'll say I let you take him."

"You can say you let him leave," Hậu replied.

For a long moment Chính did not lower the gun. I watched the pressure in his fingers and the uncertain movement of his eyes. I could get to him. I could not promise Hậu would be clear of the result.

Then Chính took one hand away from the weapon.

He lowered it.

Hậu let out a breath I had not realized he was holding.

"Good," he said. "That's enough."

Outside, engines had started. Men were leaving the compound through another entrance. Someone shouted that the police were coming. Somebody else told him to shut up and drive.

Chính stayed.

I helped Bình to the door. He leaned against me, then straightened because he wanted to walk through the gate on his own feet. I let him choose how much of my weight he needed.

At the entrance he stopped and looked back.

"My phone."

Hậu went to find it with Chính. They returned with the phone, a wallet, and the documents Bình had originally come to collect.

He took the documents first.

"Xuyên will want these," he said.

I almost laughed. Instead I took the folder and told him she could wait until he had spoken to his wife.

The arriving officers found us at the gate. We told them who was inside and who needed urgent help. I gave my account. Hậu gave his. Chính stood apart until someone asked his name, then spoke it clearly.

Bình called Lan from the car.

He said her name once and stopped. For the rest of the conversation he mostly listened. His face changed as he heard her: relief first, then shame, then a tired anger that belonged to being allowed to feel something after days of asking permission to move.

When he ended the call, he looked at me.

"She says I'm not working tomorrow."

"She's right."

"She says you're not allowed to argue."

"I respect competent management."

Hậu sat in the back with his hands flat on his knees. I caught his eyes in the mirror.

"You stayed in front of the gun," I said.

"I noticed."

"Don't make a habit of it."

"You should print that on the company shirts."

I smiled, then let the smile go.

"You did well."

He looked out of the window. "I knew him before tonight. That's all."

"Sometimes that's enough to do something I can't."

He did not answer. But his shoulders eased, and for the first time since I carried him from Vinh's warehouse, he looked like a man who had returned from a room carrying something of his own.

## 20. Lộc's Vote

Lộc came to the office before I asked for him.

He had removed the sling. His arm still moved stiffly, but he seemed determined to arrive without anything that could be mistaken for an appeal.

"Bình is home?" he asked.

"Yes."

He nodded and remained standing.

I waited.

"I knew they'd ask him to sign."

"What did you think would happen if he refused?"

"They said it would be a conversation."

"You keep finding comfort in the same fucking sentence."

He looked at the chair opposite me.

"Can I sit?"

"You can stand while you finish."

He swallowed.

"Phước said the claim on the yard would destroy my interest if everybody forced it into a dispute. He said he could preserve what belonged to me. He said you had enough money to lose this place and call it experience. I didn't."

"So you helped him take Bình."

"I told myself I'd make sure he came back."

"Did you call after he disappeared?"

"Yes."

"Did you tell Sơn where he might be?"

Lộc shook his head.

"Why?"

"Because then you'd know."

There it was. Nothing complicated enough to admire. He had been frightened of losing money, then frightened of being seen protecting it. By the time a man failed to come home, he had already invested too much in appearing uninformed.

I stood up.

Lộc took a step back before I moved toward him.

I saw the fear and felt the satisfaction arrive. I did not pretend it wasn't there.

"You sent one of my drivers into a room so somebody could keep your share comfortable."

"He wasn't your driver before—"

I put one hand against his chest and walked him backward until he reached the wall.

The temporary building trembled.

He stopped speaking.

"Do not try to become precise about ownership now."

His breath shortened beneath my palm.

I could feel the ribs under his shirt. I remembered Vinh's chest giving way and knew exactly how little additional effort would turn this conversation into another fact somebody else would have to explain to a family.

I took my hand away.

"You're finished managing people here," I said.

Lộc looked down at his shirt, then back at me.

"My interest—"

"Will be examined with the rest. You don't lose property because I put my hand on you. You lose authority because you used it on Bình."

He leaned against the wall, breathing carefully.

"What do you want me to do?"

"Tell Lan what you did. Tell the people handling Bình's report what you knew. Give Vân the records you kept separate because they made you look better. After that, decide whether you want to sell."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you'll own an interest in a company where Sơn tells you when a truck can move and Vân tells you what money can leave. You can learn to enjoy reading their reports."

He looked at me as if I had offered him a much more permanent injury.

"You'd keep me here like that."

"You can leave. The shares don't need a bedroom."

For a while he said nothing. Then he asked whether he could see the proposed terms.

I gave them to him.

He read every page. He asked questions. I answered the ones I could and referred the rest to the people whose work it was to make the answers reliable. I did not hurry him. If I wanted an agreement that survived the room, I could not make fear do all the signing.

At noon he went to see Lan.

I did not go with him. Sơn told me later that she listened for less than a minute before asking whether he had slept while Bình was missing. Lộc said badly. She said that was not the same as no.

He stayed until she had finished.

That afternoon he gave Vân two folders and a list of contacts he had not previously mentioned. No stolen password, no miraculous machine, no bright screen making people honest. Just a frightened man opening the drawer he had hoped nobody would ask about.

It was enough to clarify several claims Phước preferred blurred.

Trinh came the following morning with the final version of the interest she was prepared to sell. She had taken advice, changed two conditions, and raised one cost after discovering something we both wished Bảo had written down honestly the first time.

"You can still refuse," she said.

"I know."

"You look as if refusing would personally offend your ancestors."

"Mine are difficult to impress."

"Then make money. It is easier than dignity to record."

We signed the parts that were ready. Other matters remained unresolved. I did not emerge owning every inch of ground beneath the yard. I acquired a real stake and a defined place in the business, enough to make the next conversation concern something I actually held.

It felt less dramatic than lifting the gate.

It also felt harder to undo.

Lộc agreed to sell part of his interest under terms he had time to examine. He kept enough to care whether the place survived and lost enough influence to understand that caring would no longer entitle him to command.

When the papers were put away, Sơn looked at me.

"Can we order the other parts now?"

"Which parts?"

Ngọc appeared in the doorway with a list.

"The ones I didn't put on the first seductive page."

I took it.

"You people are relentless."

"You bought a working concern," Vân said. "This is the concerning part."

We laughed. For a few minutes the office felt like a place people might choose to enter for reasons other than fear.

Then Trinh asked to speak privately.

She waited until the others had gone before placing another paper on the desk.

"Phước is trying to make his departure expensive."

"Departure from what?"

"The arrangement he expected to keep controlling. He hasn't lost all his rights. He has lost the convenience of having everyone approach him separately. He will want the remaining claims to feel larger than the business can bear."

"And the driver?"

"That has made people cautious about being seen beside him. Caution is not the same as leaving him."

I looked at the paper. It named equipment and balances, some valid, some doubtful, some already being examined elsewhere.

"He'll send men to collect."

"I expect so."

"With the wrong papers again."

"With enough papers that someone can argue while the men begin moving things."

I folded the page.

"Tell anyone whose property is actually here to speak to Vân. They'll get an answer. Anyone who comes to take something by force will speak to me."

Trinh studied my face.

"You enjoy the second sentence."

"I've earned the right to enjoy one sentence this week."

"Enjoying it won't make it inexpensive."

After she left, I went to Shawna's atelier.

The new venue had changed the whole show. What had begun as a small private presentation was becoming something more ambitious because she had found enough room to invite clients who would otherwise have remained on a waiting list. That meant more staff, more preparation, more cost, and less room for an error she could explain away quietly.

"I need to discuss money," she said.

I sat immediately.

"Your face," she said, "is why people put bells on dogs before dinner."

"I like being useful."

"You like being asked. We're about to discover whether those can coexist."

She showed me her plan. She had already secured part of the cost through bookings and commitments from clients. She wanted a short loan for the remaining preparation, with clear terms and a repayment date based on income she could reasonably expect.

"I can give it to you," I said.

"I know. That's why I brought a plan instead of a bowl."

I read it.

The arithmetic worked. The risk was in timing, people, a supplier meeting a promise, a client not changing her mind because a cousin had offered a better idea. I recognized the shape of it. Money was the easy part; the difficult parts had faces.

"From the licensing income," she said. "Not the room money."

"Yes."

"You don't get creative control."

"I was going to suggest—"

"No."

"You haven't heard it."

"I married it. No."

I laughed and agreed to her terms.

She put the papers away, then took my hand.

"This is how I want us to talk about power," she said. "You can help me without making me smaller."

"You make that difficult even when I try."

"Good. Practice on harder clients."

My phone rang before I left.

Sơn said three vehicles had come to the gate with men claiming equipment. The night shift had not yet begun. Ngọc had stopped them from entering, and the man leading them had said she would be moved if she continued obstructing work.

I stood.

Shawna saw my face change.

"Which sentence?" she asked.

I understood what she meant.

"The expensive one."

She let go of my hand.

"Then make sure you remember the rest."

## 21. The Second Gate

The men at the yard had brought enough paper to make the first lie look administrative.

Ngọc stood in front of the lead vehicle with a wrench in her hand. Sơn was beside her, holding his telephone where the man speaking to him could see that the conversation was not private. Behind them, workers had stopped moving freight and begun moving people away from the gate.

I parked outside and walked in.

Thành saw me first.

He had recovered from the railing. His shirt covered the bruises, and two men at his shoulders gave him somewhere to put his confidence. He watched me cross the space and made a small gesture to the man holding the papers.

"This is him."

The man with the papers wore a hard hat though nobody else did. He had the official-looking expression of someone who had learned that a clipboard could borrow authority from almost any uniform.

"We have come to recover property."

"Then Vân will examine what you've brought."

"We've already waited."

"You can wait where you are."

Vân came from the office and took the papers. The men watched her read. She asked for one supporting document, then another. The man with the hard hat gave her the first and said the second was unnecessary.

"Then you'll find it easy to leave a copy," she said.

He looked at Thành.

I said, "She asked you."

Behind the vehicles stood drivers who had not been told the whole job. I recognized one from a client visit. He kept looking toward the repaired gate, then toward the equipment the men had come to take. He wanted to know whether the thing he had been paid to transport would require him to become part of a fight. I watched him decide not to ask in front of the people who had hired him.

Vân separated one page from the rest.

"This machine is theirs under the current arrangement," she said. "The others require clarification. The new jack is ours outright. That truck belongs to a client. They have no basis to take it."

The hard-hat man pointed at the list.

"Equivalent value."

"You've used those words here before," I said. "They didn't grow authority on the way back."

I told Ngọc to prepare the machine Vân had identified. She began to object, stopped, and looked at the page herself.

"We need it," she said.

"We'll replace it. It isn't ours."

She held my eyes for a moment, then went to arrange the move safely. That mattered more than I expected. The yard had watched me break things for weeks. Now it watched me let something valuable leave because the answer was inconvenient but clear.

Thành disliked the effect.

"You're making a performance of cooperation," he said.

"You're making cooperation difficult to perform."

The legitimate recovery should have ended there. The machine could be moved, the driver could be paid, the remaining claims could be examined. Several people present understood that. I saw it in the way they began putting down hands that had been waiting for instructions.

Then the hard-hat man's phone rang.

He listened, turned his back, and walked a few steps away. When he returned, he told the nearest driver to begin with the truck.

The client's truck.

Ngọc was beneath its raised side, helping another mechanic free equipment that had been placed too close to the wheels. She heard the instruction and slid out far enough to shout.

"Nobody moves this."

Thành went toward her.

I stepped into his path.

"Stop."

He hit me.

It was a good punch. Clean, fast, thrown by a man who had decided he would rather be injured again than spend the rest of his life explaining the first time. I let it strike the side of my face because moving farther would put Ngọc behind me in his reach.

My head turned a few inches.

Then I looked back at him.

He understood immediately that the punch had cost him more than it cost me.

I caught his shirt and drove him into the side of the lead vehicle. The panel folded around his shoulder. He tried to strike again. I trapped the arm against his body and lifted him until his feet left the ground.

"You got one," I said. "That was generous."

One of the men behind him drew a weapon.

Sơn shouted. Workers began running toward the repair bays. The driver in the lead vehicle climbed out and left his door open, wanting nothing more to do with the job.

I threw Thành into the armed man.

They went down together. A shot struck the ground. The hard-hat man dropped his papers and ran toward the rear vehicles.

Everything became movement.

Ngọc crawled clear of the truck. The mechanic beside her, a young man called Phúc, caught his sleeve on a projection beneath it. Another driver, frightened by the gunshot and trying to move away, released a vehicle that had not been prepared to move. The load shifted. Steel supports scraped across concrete.

I heard Ngọc shout Phúc's name.

I reached the truck as its weight began settling into the space he occupied.

My hands found the frame. I drove upward.

For a moment the vehicle continued coming down. The force passed through my arms and shoulders into legs that had not finished healing from the road. I felt the old wound in my side open. Warmth ran beneath my shirt. I kept my eyes on the space under the truck because looking at the weight would not change where the man was.

"Pull him," I said.

Ngọc went back in.

The suspension shifted. My right foot slid across oil on the concrete. I caught myself by driving the other foot against the base of a fixed post. The post bent. The truck stopped descending.

I had thought, in the ruined office, that my body had reached the largest demand it could answer. Now it found another answer in the places that hurt most. There was no clean equation for it. I could name the force, estimate the load, measure the space Phúc needed. None of those things explained why my arms continued rising when every ordinary limit said they should fold.

Ngọc tore the caught sleeve free. She dragged Phúc out by the shoulders and kept moving until they were clear.

"Out," she shouted.

I shifted the vehicle sideways and let it settle onto clear ground. The impact shook the bay. A window in the temporary office cracked.

For a moment I stood beneath the roof with both hands hanging at my sides, unable to close them.

Then I smelled smoke.

At the far end of the yard, a dark plume rose beside a storage room. People were already moving away from it. Someone had turned a private recovery into a destruction job when the recovery stopped going their way.

I did not see how it started. I saw what the men near it did next. One ran toward the rear gate carrying a bag. Another pushed a worker aside to reach a vehicle. Neither looked surprised by the smoke.

"Get everyone outside," I told Sơn.

He was already doing it.

Ngọc pulled an alarm and shouted instructions to people who knew the yard better than I did. They moved without waiting for me to become an expert on fire. The ordinary competence of the place began saving itself, and I understood how close I had come to buying a company while thinking only about the strength of its owner.

Thành had struggled upright near the lead vehicle. The man with the gun lay beside him, stunned. Thành looked from the smoke to the workers moving through the gate and seemed to realize that somebody had expanded the plan without asking whether he intended to be inside it.

"Who gave that order?" I asked.

He shook his head.

"Tell me."

"I don't know."

The hard-hat man called from the rear vehicle. Thành turned toward him.

That was enough.

I went after the man who had carried the papers.

He ran through a narrow space between two parked trucks. I followed, faster than he expected and slower than I wanted. My breathing had become difficult. He heard it and decided to stop running long enough to use the knife he had kept out of sight while he explained property rights.

He turned in the gap and struck at my stomach.

I caught his wrist. The blade touched my shirt, then stopped.

He pushed with his other hand. I watched the effort gather in his face. His whole body strained toward a point my fingers would not let him reach.

"You should have brought the missing document," I said.

I twisted the knife away and put him against the truck. His face struck the metal. He sagged, still conscious, and tried to reach for his telephone.

I took it before he could make the call.

The screen showed Phước's name.

I answered.

"He's busy," I said.

Phước was silent.

Behind me, the first emergency vehicle turned through the open gate. The workers had cleared the entrance for it. Sơn stood beyond the boundary counting people. Ngọc held Phúc upright while someone examined his shoulder.

"The recovery became disorderly," Phước said at last.

"Your men started a fire."

"You don't know that."

"Then come down and tell them what you do know."

"I have no reason to—"

"You're running out of people willing to stand here and say that for you."

I ended the call and gave the phone to Sơn to keep with the papers and the account of what had happened. The hard-hat man remained where I put him until the arriving officers took charge. Thành stayed too. He had seen the rear vehicle leave without waiting for him.

The fire was contained before it took the repair bays. The storage room was lost. So were parts, records we had copies of, a cabinet full of things people kept at work because taking them home every day was inconvenient. Those last losses were small on an inventory and large when their owners began naming them.

Mười had kept an old rain jacket there. It had belonged to his brother. Ngọc lost a set of tools she had bought one at a time. A driver had a photograph tucked inside a box. Vân wrote down what she could replace and what she could not.

I walked through the damage after the smoke thinned. Nobody called me a hero. They asked whether the roof above the next room was safe, whether tomorrow's work would happen, whether the claims people would make them prove every small possession had existed.

I answered what I knew.

When I reached the gate, Sơn was waiting.

"Everyone's out," he said.

"Everyone?"

He read the names again. I listened to each one. The last name landed with more force than the truck had.

"Good," I said.

My legs gave way before I could say anything else.

Sơn caught one arm. Ngọc caught the other. They lowered me onto a low concrete block beside the entrance I had once torn open by myself.

"You can stop now," Ngọc said.

"There's—"

"There are people here. We know. We are some of them. Stop."

I sat while the yard moved around me.

Shawna arrived before I called. Someone had sent her a photograph of the smoke. She came through the gate carrying my father's old jacket and an expression that made two men move aside without being asked.

She crouched in front of me.

"Everyone?" she asked.

"Everyone."

She closed her eyes briefly, then opened them and looked at the blood beneath my shirt.

"And you?"

"Still included."

"Keep it that way."

She put the jacket around my shoulders. It smelled faintly of machine oil and home.

## 22. The Circle

The yard reopened in sections.

Ngọc decided which sections. Sơn decided who worked where. Vân decided what we could pay immediately and what would have to wait. I discovered that ownership involved being told no by people whose judgment I had deliberately purchased.

"This is a terrible system," I told her.

"It was your idea."

"I have better ones."

"Bring one that repairs the storage room without spending the next payroll."

I did. It took the rest of the morning.

The fire had changed how people answered Phước's calls. Some stopped answering. Others asked questions they had previously accepted payment not to ask. The men who came to recover equipment had their own accounts to give. Thành, discovering that loyalty had not reserved him a seat in the departing vehicle, became more interested in describing whose instructions he had followed.

None of it transferred every contested right into my hands. The legitimate claims still had to be handled. We handled them. Some cost more than I wanted. Some cost less than Phước had promised they would. Several became simpler when their owners realized that the yard could keep working without asking him to interpret every sentence.

The Circle's other businesses moved faster.

Dần brought two men to see me. One ran another room. The other arranged private loans for people who preferred speed to questions. They had paid Bảo, then held their payments while the next answer became clear.

"Phước says he has the senior position," the lender said.

"Does he answer when you call?"

"Sometimes."

"What does he give you?"

The man considered that for a long moment.

"Permission to continue."

"You already continued."

Dần smiled.

We discussed what I would take and what I would do for it. I did not call it a service every time it looked like protection. I did not pretend the men had approached me because they admired clear accounts. They wanted disputes settled, debts addressed, and competitors discouraged from treating their businesses as available property. I wanted a share and the power that came with being the person they called.

We agreed on both.

"And when someone refuses?" the lender asked.

"You tell me what he refused. You don't decide his wife owes you an answer."

"You've said that before."

"Then you'll remember it when it becomes inconvenient."

He nodded.

The money began arriving. I took it.

Phước asked to meet on the seventh day after the fire.

He chose a private room above a riverside restaurant. This time Trinh knew the appointment. So did Dần, Sơn, and the people who had been examining the legitimate arrangements. Phước could still surround himself with claims. He could no longer make the rest of us meet him in separate rooms and call that consensus.

I went with no bag and no walking stick.

My knee still hurt. It did not need to announce itself at the door.

The restaurant had closed between services. Chairs were stacked along one wall. A cook sat near the rear entrance peeling something into a bowl. He looked up as I passed and returned to his work without asking who I was.

Upstairs, Phước stood by the window.

Two men waited near the door. I had not seen either before. One looked at my hands. The other looked at the stairs behind me, as if expecting the rest of an explanation to arrive.

"Sit," Phước said.

"We can stand."

He turned from the window.

"You have made a difficult month."

"You should see my medical bills."

"I offered you a place."

"You offered to own the place I stood."

"You don't understand what it takes to keep these people together."

"They're together. You're the one meeting me alone."

His eyes moved briefly toward the men at the door.

I smiled.

"I noticed them."

Phước went to the table and opened a folder.

"There are interests I am prepared to release. Claims I can settle. People whose cooperation would make the next year easier for you."

"Send the legitimate claims to the people handling them. The others end now."

"You can't simply announce that."

"I just did."

He looked at me with real anger for the first time. Not irritation at a bad negotiation. Anger at a man who had begun speaking as if the conversation no longer required his permission.

"That is theft."

"You mean the cut you took for letting people continue doing what they already did?"

"The structure I maintained."

"You sent men to burn the part I maintained. We're past naming yours politely."

He closed the folder.

"You are very young."

"I've heard. It keeps improving."

"You think because you can lift a vehicle, ordinary consequences don't reach you."

I thought of Hiếu's watch. Of Lan waiting in the office. Of Ngọc's tools burned in a room I had promised would be a better place to work.

"They reach me," I said. "That's why I'm here."

One of the men at the door moved behind me.

I turned my head enough to see him.

"Stay there."

He kept moving.

His hand came out with a short blade. He was close enough to believe surprise had already done the difficult work. I caught his wrist, brought it down against the edge of the table, and took the blade when his fingers opened.

The second man drew a gun.

I threw the chair between us.

It hit his forearm and carried the weapon aside as he fired. The shot broke the window behind Phước. Glass burst outward toward the river. I crossed the room, caught the gun in both hands, and bent the man's wrist until he let go.

He tried to strike me with his free hand.

I put him through the wooden partition beside the door.

The wall broke around his shoulders. He landed on the other side, coughing beneath pieces of panel. The first man was on his knees beside the table, holding the arm he could no longer straighten.

Phước had not moved.

I set the empty gun on the table.

"You keep making me meet your staff."

He looked at the broken window. A curtain moved in the warm air coming off the river.

"Those men protect me."

"Then give them easier work."

I went close enough that he could see the marks the last month had left on my face.

"Bình goes home every night he chooses. Hậu and his sister hear no more from you. My wife's business is not a lever you pull when you dislike an answer. The yard's workers are not names you trade to make your claims feel larger."

"And your people?"

"They'll hear the same rules."

"You won't keep them."

"You can wait and find out from somewhere else."

He laughed, quietly and without humor.

"You want me frightened."

"I want you to understand. Fear is the method you've been insisting on."

He looked past me toward the stairs. I could hear someone below calling for help, another voice asking people to remain outside. The shot had ended the privacy he believed the room could guarantee.

"Your father still works in that little lane," he said.

I stared at him.

He mistook the silence for success.

"You can't stand beside every door."

I laughed.

It surprised both of us. The sound came out before I could decide whether he deserved the explanation.

"You think he's the easy one."

Phước's face changed.

"You've shown me how poorly you understand my family," I said. "Don't spend anyone else's life testing the rest."

I took the folder from the table, looked at its first page, and put it back.

"Send it through the people who know how to examine it. If there's a real claim, it gets a real answer. Your place in the Circle is finished."

"And if I disagree?"

I looked at the men he had brought to make disagreement expensive.

"You already did."

I left him standing beside the broken window. Downstairs, I gave my name and waited for the people who had been called. I described the weapons and the men upstairs. I did not pretend I had gone there to discuss the lunch menu.

Later, when Dần asked what had happened, I told him Phước would no longer be collecting through the Circle.

"He agreed?"

"He understood the proposal."

"That's not the same thing."

"You're becoming very precise. Vân will be proud."

Dần laughed, then grew serious.

"People will test it."

"I know."

"They'll come because you made him look weak. They'll think there's room."

"There is room. They can ask what it costs."

He looked at me for a long time.

"You really are keeping it."

"I told you I would."

That evening, we put a desk in the rebuilt office.

It was plain, heavy, and strong enough that Ngọc said she wouldn't have to replace it after the first difficult conversation. Sơn carried in two chairs. Vân came to the doorway and told us where the light would fall if we left the desk facing that direction.

We moved it.

I sat after everyone had finished explaining the room to me.

The yard worked beyond the window. Engines started. A driver laughed at something another man said. Someone swore at a stubborn fitting. No one asked the gate for permission to leave.

The Circle kept its name.

I took the chair.

## 23. Shawna's Night

Shawna's show began six weeks after the first meeting with Vinh.

By then I could raise my left arm above my shoulder again, though doing it quickly remained an argument with my body. I had acquired a desk, a share in a working business, enemies who answered carefully, and an opinion on three kinds of replacement tire I had never expected to develop.

My wife had acquired a better venue than the one Phước took away.

She reminded me of that whenever I looked too pleased with the desk.

On the afternoon of the show she put me in a dark suit and made me stand in front of the mirror while she examined the shoulder.

"You changed shape," she said.

"I've been exercising."

"You've been damaging infrastructure. The distinction matters to the sleeve."

She adjusted something I could barely see and stepped back. I thought the suit had fitted before. Watching her face, I understood that fitting and being finished were separate subjects.

"There," she said.

"I look good."

"You looked good in the first version. This version makes it less irritating that you know."

She had designed her own clothes months earlier and changed them twice while the rest of the show changed around her. What she wore now was simple enough that people would mistake its precision for ease. I knew how many evenings lived inside it. I had watched her remove a line of work because it pleased her but did not belong in the whole thing.

I told her she looked extraordinary.

She stopped moving for a moment.

"Thank you," she said. Then she handed me a list. "Now be useful while I'm still willing to like you."

The venue had high windows, a long central space, and enough character to create problems for anyone trying to make a room behave identically from every chair. Shawna had used that instead of fighting it. Guests would move through the first part of the presentation rather than remain seated while everything happened at a distance. The later show would gather them near the center, where her staff had spent two days making the light fall where she wanted it.

I helped carry things. I followed directions. When I finished one task early, she gave me another without pretending that speed was a reason to be finished for the day.

Dad arrived with Mom before the guests.

He wore a shirt Shawna had chosen. He looked at the room, at the supports beneath a platform, at a door that did not close cleanly. I saw him begin deciding what could be repaired before anyone noticed.

Mom touched his wrist.

"You are a guest."

"The latch—"

"Is invited too. Leave it."

He left it for eleven minutes. Then one of the assistants caught her sleeve on it, and he asked Shawna whether she wanted it fixed.

"Please," she said.

Mom looked at the ceiling as if the building had betrayed her personally.

"Two minutes," Dad said.

He took less.

Trinh came early enough to see the staff preparing. She wore the dress she had ordered and stood exactly the way Shawna had taught her. When I mentioned it, she told me that advice was easier to follow when the person giving it could show the cost of ignoring it in a mirror.

"Vân would like that," I said.

"Vân doesn't need a mirror. She has a calculator."

Vân arrived with Ngọc. Neither looked entirely comfortable being called a guest. Ngọc had spent the afternoon getting machine oil out from beneath her nails and announced that the room had better appreciate the sacrifice.

Sơn came with his daughter, who had been invited by Shawna after hearing that she drew clothes in the margins of schoolwork. Diễm carried a little notebook and asked whether she could draw what she saw. Shawna gave her a place where she would not block anyone and treated the question as seriously as she treated Trinh's fitting.

I watched Sơn watch that happen.

He caught my eye and looked away quickly, as if gratitude were something he preferred to discuss privately.

Dần came without an entourage after I told him the guest list meant the people on it, not everyone who had ever laughed at one of his jokes. The dealer from his room came too, with his boyfriend, Kiên, who had helped arrange transport for some of the equipment. His name was Thắng. I had learned it after the night with Mẫn and wondered why I hadn't asked sooner.

They stood near the front windows holding drinks and looking like men who had taken an evening off rather than escaped a dangerous room. I was pleased by that in a way I did not explain to Dần when he came to stand beside me.

"Your wife gathers interesting people," he said.

"She chooses them herself."

"I noticed. She put me next to an accountant."

"That was mercy. She could have put you next to two."

The guests grew louder as the room filled. Women greeted one another, examined details, compared expectations with what had actually been made. Some knew nothing about the yard. Others knew more than they said. A few looked at me with the curiosity people reserved for a story that had become large enough to approach in person.

A man I had met once at a business dinner came over and asked whether it was true that I had lifted a truck.

"Part of one," I said.

"How much?"

"The part that was causing difficulty."

He laughed and asked whether he could introduce a friend who had a debt problem.

"Tomorrow."

"It will only take a moment."

"Then he'll have no trouble remembering it until tomorrow."

The man followed my eyes toward Shawna, who was speaking with a client near the center of the room. He smiled in a way that suggested he understood marriage as a temporary interruption in more important business.

"Of course. We let them have their night."

I turned fully toward him.

"She built it. Nobody's letting her have anything."

His smile weakened.

"I meant—"

"Find a better meaning. There are drinks near the window."

He left me alone.

The first part of the presentation went smoothly. Then a piece of equipment serving the central space stopped working, taking part of the planned light with it. I heard a staff member swear behind a screen. Two assistants moved quickly toward Shawna. For a moment I saw the whole room begin searching for the person whose alarm would tell them how worried to be.

Shawna did not become that person.

She asked two questions, changed the order of the next sequence, and sent one assistant to speak to the guests while another worked with the technician. Then she looked at me.

"Can you help him understand the load?"

"Yes."

"Help. Don't conquer it."

I went behind the screen.

The technician was tired and angry with the machine rather than frightened of me. That made the conversation useful immediately. He told me what had changed since the rehearsal. I looked, listened, and worked through the relationship between the demands being placed on equipment that had been asked to do more than its original arrangement supported.

The answer was ordinary. It did not require the machine in my bag, a mathematical revelation, or anyone leaving the ground. We changed what needed changing with the people responsible for it. The technician did his work. I carried what he asked me to carry.

The lights returned in time for the revised sequence.

Nobody applauded us. They were looking at the clothes.

I went back to my place and watched my wife make a room pay attention.

There were moments in the show when I could see the exact decisions she had argued over at home. A line she had simplified. A color she had refused to substitute when a supplier said the difference was small. A piece that had seemed impossible to finish until she removed something she liked and made the rest stronger. I had watched those choices happen while thinking of them as her work beside mine. Now I saw how much of herself she had put into them, and I was embarrassed by the difference between loving her and attending properly to what she loved.

My phone vibrated twice.

I turned it off.

At the end, people stood before she expected them to. For one fraction of a second her face lost the practiced composure she wore through fittings and negotiations. She looked young, surprised, and almost angry with how much it mattered.

Then she saw me.

I stood with everyone else.

Afterward the room filled with work again. Orders, questions, changes, people wanting to know what could be made for them and how soon. Shawna did not pretend enthusiasm was a contract. Her staff recorded what had actually been agreed, and she made time for the people who had come to look without being ready to buy.

Diễm showed her a drawing.

Shawna crouched to examine it and asked why she had changed the sleeve. The girl explained. Shawna listened. Sơn stood behind his daughter with his hands in his pockets, looking as if somebody had returned a part of the evening he had been missing for years.

When the guests finally left, I helped clear the room. Dad stayed too. Mom supervised both of us until Shawna told her she could sit. Then the two women sat together, discussing the show in the detailed, unsentimental way people discuss work they respect.

Near midnight, Shawna found me stacking chairs.

"You behaved," she said.

"I was magnificent."

"You were occasionally useful and mostly quiet. I understand how you could confuse the feeling."

I put down the chair.

"You were magnificent."

She looked at me. This time I let the words remain without improving them.

She kissed me in the empty room, with the last of the staff laughing somewhere behind a screen and my father pretending to find a complicated problem in the arrangement of folding tables.

"I wanted you here," she said.

"I'm here."

"All of you."

I held her a little closer.

For that evening, I was.

## 24. Invitations

By the beginning of 2027, people had learned to invite me before deciding what they wanted.

Some invitations concerned mathematics. A few concerned businesses that had examined the work rather than the man who owned it. Others arrived on paper thick enough to make a request for money feel like an honor. There were dinners, openings, proposed partnerships, introductions to people who had been described as important so often that nobody remembered to explain what they did.

Shawna sorted them into two piles one evening.

"Which are which?" I asked.

"These people want you. These people want a photograph proving they survived beside you."

"How can you tell?"

"The second group mentions your contribution before saying what you contributed to."

I examined the piles. She was right more often than I wanted her to be.

The yard was making money without needing me to fill every gap from my own accounts. Not enough to make Vân cheerful about surprises, but enough that she had begun planning replacements instead of merely postponing failures. Xuyên sent work regularly again. She still deducted the agreed cost of one lost customer from the terms we had negotiated and seemed pleased that I did not ask her to call it forgiveness.

The Circle's other businesses made more.

I knew the amounts. I knew who paid them. I knew the difference between a client buying convenience and a man paying because he had decided that disagreeing with me would be worse. That distinction did not stop the money from entering the account. It only stopped me from pretending I had built a cleaner occupation than the one I chose.

At a dinner for a charitable project, the host introduced me as an innovator and community leader.

Shawna leaned close.

"Which hand did the community vote for?"

"The one holding your chair."

"Democracy survives another evening."

She looked beautiful. She had finished repaying the loan for the show ahead of the date we agreed, then become annoyed when I looked proud in a way she said resembled a man praising a horse for returning to its own stable.

"I was proud of the work," I told her.

"Then talk about the work. The money is arithmetic."

I had married somebody who used my own weapons accurately.

The dinner took place in a large room with round tables and lights designed to make everyone look less tired than they were. I recognized donors, owners, a few people who had worked with Trinh, and one man who had quietly moved his business away from Phước after the fire. He came over to shake my hand and spoke as if we had always been on the same side of every question.

I let him finish. Then I asked whether the workers at the concern he had bought had received the wages held during the transfer.

His smile changed.

"Those details are being resolved."

"Vân knows someone who can help resolve them quickly."

"That won't be necessary."

"Good. I'll ask them next week."

He left us shortly afterward.

Shawna watched him go.

"You enjoyed that."

"Yes."

"At least you didn't pretend you were asking about his health."

Near the entrance, Thắng and Kiên were speaking to a member of the event staff. Kiên had supplied transport through his own work and been invited by an organizer. Thắng came with him. Their names appeared on the list, but someone had decided the table assignment needed to be reconsidered after recognizing where Thắng dealt cards.

I went over when I saw Kiên's face tighten.

"Problem?"

The staff member looked relieved to have someone else take responsibility.

"A seating adjustment."

"Where are they sitting?"

She named a table that stood close to a service entrance and far from the place indicated on their cards.

Thắng looked embarrassed. He said it didn't matter.

Kiên said it did.

I asked who had requested the change. The staff member looked toward an organizer, who saw us looking and suddenly became occupied with a telephone.

"They can sit with us," I said.

"Your table is full."

"Then I can move."

It was remarkable how quickly space appeared once my inconvenience entered the arithmetic.

I watched the organizer approach, smiling too broadly, and understood exactly what had happened. I had not improved the room's judgment. I had applied a larger weight to it. Thắng knew that too. He thanked me quietly, and I told him to thank Kiên for refusing to be moved without an answer.

Back at the table, Shawna shifted her chair to make room.

"Apparently we're popular," she said.

"Only with furniture," I replied.

Kiên laughed and relaxed enough to begin talking about work. Thắng took longer. When he did, he described a customer at Dần's room who had spent half an evening claiming that a sequence of losses proved he was statistically owed a win.

"What did you tell him?" I asked.

"That the cards hadn't signed the agreement."

I liked him more every time he finished a sentence.

The host came to ask whether I would say a few words. I asked what words were needed. He said anything inspiring about opportunity would be welcome.

"Pay people on time," I said.

He laughed, waiting for the rest.

I looked back at him.

"That was the speech."

Shawna covered her mouth with one hand. The host decided somebody else would be better at the microphone.

After dinner, Trinh found me near the windows.

"You've become difficult to introduce," she said.

"You knew that when you sold."

"I knew you could read. The public speaking problem is new."

She gave me an envelope.

It was dark, unmarked except for my name and a small embossed numeral. Inside was an invitation to a private dinner associated with a club called the Ninth Room. The language was spare. No promise of influence, no exaggerated account of the guests, no request for a contribution before I had accepted the meal.

At the bottom was a name: Đặng Minh Khải.

"Who is he?" I asked.

"One of the people who thought you should be invited. Clever. Well connected. Better company than several men you've already agreed to meet."

"That's not a difficult comparison."

"He may have mentioned that."

I turned the card over. Nothing else was printed on it.

"What does he want?"

"You could ask if you ever accept."

"Have I met him?"

"No. You would remember."

I put the card back in the envelope.

Trinh said, "You don't have to answer tonight."

"Then I won't."

She looked amused. "There may be hope for you."

In the car, Shawna examined the invitation.

"The Ninth Room," she read. "What happened to the first eight?"

"Poor occupancy."

"Are you going?"

"Not now."

"Why?"

"I have enough rooms that need me to understand what belongs in them."

She looked at me for a moment.

"That was almost sensible. Keep practicing."

The invitation went into a drawer in the office. I did not answer it that season. I did not meet Khải. His name remained a line on a card, one more possible door in a city that had begun opening them before I arrived.

The following week, a young man came to the yard carrying a folder and a fear he tried to disguise as confidence. He wanted an introduction to someone who could finance a business. He had been told that I could make things happen.

"Who told you?" I asked.

He named a man who owed Dần money.

I almost sent him away. Then he opened the folder and began explaining what he actually wanted to build. The idea was incomplete. Some figures were wrong. He knew the work better than he knew the costs, and he had brought enough of both that I could tell he had done something before asking for rescue.

I read it.

"You don't need the person who sent you," I said.

"He said he'd arrange the introduction for a share."

"Of what?"

"The company."

"How much?"

He told me.

I set down the folder.

"For knowing my telephone number?"

The young man looked frightened again.

"He said you were difficult to reach."

"Apparently not difficult enough."

I gave him the number of someone who could examine a business like his. No promise that the answer would be yes. No guarantee that wanting something made it viable. Just a place where the idea could be asked a better set of questions than the man demanding a share had asked.

After he left, I called that man.

He answered brightly until I told him why I was calling.

"People pay for access," he said.

"Not to me through you."

"I was helping him."

"Then enjoy having helped for free."

He began explaining what he had meant. I ended the call before he could make the explanation my work too.

Outside, the young man was still standing beside his motorbike, looking at the number I had given him. I could not tell whether the business would survive. I could not tell whether he would become someone I liked if it did. I had made one unnecessary payment disappear from his first attempt.

For once, that felt like enough of a day's authority.

## 25. The Men Who Stayed

The following wet season arrived while Vân was arguing with a supplier about whether a delayed delivery could be described as early for the next order.

She put the call on speaker so Ngọc could appreciate the answer.

"He's creative," Ngọc said.

"He'll need to be. I haven't paid him."

I stood in the doorway listening to them and remembered the first contract Vinh had pushed across a table. So much of the year had concerned sentences people hoped would move money in a direction the work had not earned.

The yard had changed. New machinery stood beside machines we had repaired rather than replaced. The storage room had been rebuilt with arrangements chosen by the people who actually used it. Sơn kept a board showing the shifts where anyone could read it. People still argued about who had been given the worst work, which driver returned late too often, and whether Ngọc needed every part she ordered.

She usually did.

Quang supervised the evening security work now. His leg had healed imperfectly. He walked with a stiffness that became more visible when he was tired, and he refused tasks that required him to pretend it had not happened. That made some younger men uncomfortable until they discovered how much he could notice while standing still.

Hiếu's watch had been repaired by my father after Ly decided she wanted it working again. She kept it. Quang asked about it once, then stopped. There were things friendship did not entitle him to carry away from a widow's home, and he seemed to understand that without needing anyone to explain it.

Mười retired in the middle of a week.

Sơn asked whether he wanted to wait until Friday so people could arrange something.

"I have waited for enough Fridays," he said.

We settled what he was owed. He checked the amount himself, folded the paper, and put it carefully in his shirt pocket. Ngọc brought food. Vân bought drinks. Nobody made a speech until Dần arrived, and then everybody told him not to.

Mười sat beneath the repair roof watching younger men eat food in his honor. He looked pleased and slightly suspicious, as if retirement were another company arrangement whose hidden deduction might be revealed later.

I sat beside him.

"What will you do?"

"Go home."

"After that?"

"Stay there until I want to go somewhere else."

I smiled.

He looked at me.

"You think that's a small plan."

"No."

"Good. It took a long fucking time to afford."

He took the bag of gifts Ngọc had assembled and inspected it without pretending he wasn't curious. There were practical things, food he liked, a new rain jacket he had chosen himself after refusing the first one because it had too many pockets. Nobody tried to tell him it replaced the jacket lost in the fire.

When he left, several men walked with him to the gate. I stayed near the office. He had come to the yard long before me. His last walk through it did not need to become a ceremony about the man who happened to be collecting its money now.

That afternoon, a woman came to see me about her brother.

Her name was Phương. She worked in a shop that had nothing to do with the Circle. She had brought a folded paper and a copy of a message sent to her telephone. The message said her brother's obligations would affect the family if he continued delaying payment.

I read it twice.

"Who sent this?"

She named Tú, a man who had begun handling collections for one of the lending rooms after Dần recommended him. I had met him once. He spoke carefully, dressed well, and listened with the eager attention of a man collecting phrases he hoped to use later.

"Did you sign anything?" I asked her.

"No."

"Did you take money?"

"No. My brother asked me once. I refused. That's why he borrowed elsewhere."

"And Tú knows that?"

"I told him. He said family doesn't stop because I say no."

I looked at the sentence on the page until the words became something I could hear in Vinh's voice.

I called Tú and told him to come to the yard.

Phương grew frightened when she realized I intended to speak to him directly. I asked whether she wanted to leave before he arrived. She said she wanted to know what would happen after she left.

"Nothing more is owed by you," I told her. "Your brother's account is his. You will have that in writing before you go."

"And if he calls again?"

"Then you call this number."

She looked at the card I gave her.

"He said he worked for you."

"He did."

Tú arrived smiling.

He stopped smiling when he saw Phương seated outside Vân's office. I took him into mine and closed the door. I put his message on the desk.

"Explain."

He read it as if it belonged to someone else.

"Her brother has been difficult."

"She isn't her brother."

"I was encouraging the family to—"

"Choose a less insulting verb."

He looked up.

"I didn't hurt her."

"You made her afraid to go home before speaking to me."

"That's how people pay."

There was no confusion in his answer. That was what made me stand.

He watched me come around the desk. His right hand went flat on its edge, steadying him while he tried to decide which apology would cost least.

I took that hand and drove it against the wood.

The bones broke with a small, definite sound.

He dropped to his knees, gasping.

I kept hold of his wrist until he looked at me.

"You heard the rule," I said. "You knew what I meant. You used my name because you thought it made your version safer."

"I understand."

"Now you do."

I released him and called someone to take him for treatment. His work for the Circle ended before he reached the gate. The people who had introduced him would pay what it cost to examine every account he had touched. I would pay if they could not. None of it would be added to a borrower's balance.

When I went back to Phương, she was watching my hands.

I gave her the written confirmation and told her how to reach Vân if anything else arrived.

She thanked Vân. She did not thank me.

After she left, Vân stood in my doorway.

"She heard him."

"I know."

"You frightened her too."

I looked at the mark on the desk.

"I know."

Vân remained there for a moment, then went back to work. She did not resign in a dramatic gesture. She did not tell me I had done the right thing. I paid her to keep the accounts honest, and she had included that one.

That evening I told Shawna exactly what happened.

She listened without finishing the sentences for me.

"You wanted him to feel it," she said.

"Yes."

"Would you do it again?"

I looked at her.

"Yes."

She put down the cup she had been holding.

"Then don't ask me to call it something gentle."

"I won't."

"And don't bring that voice upstairs when you ask me where I put your shirt."

I nodded.

There were costs I could calculate and costs that refused to become numbers. That did not mean I stopped choosing the thing that caused them. It meant I no longer got to claim surprise every time someone I loved looked at me and needed a moment to find the person she expected.

Hậu came to the yard the next morning carrying an envelope.

"No," I said before he sat down.

"You haven't opened it."

"I know what people look like when they bring an envelope they're proud of."

"Then let me be proud for five fucking minutes."

I took it.

Inside was a small repayment and a note listing what he believed I had spent on his hospital visit and recovery. Some figures were wrong. He had included a meal he bought himself and missed a charge I had never told him about.

"It wasn't a purchase," I said.

"I know. That's why I get to decide whether I give anything back."

I looked at him.

He had found legitimate work with a company that repaired commercial kitchens. He knew very little about the equipment and more than enough about driving between difficult customers. The owner was willing to let him learn as long as he arrived sober and did not describe lateness as a misunderstanding.

"Does he know where you worked?"

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Enough that I won't have to become a different man when someone mentions my name."

I put the envelope on the desk.

"Keep paying what you owe Linh first."

"I am."

"And Phước?"

"The amount I agreed is being handled. No visits. No family. I kept the records."

"Good."

He looked around the office. The desk was new. The fan was quiet. Through the window he could see people moving freely past the place where he would once have waited for a collector to tell him his day was finished.

"Dũng spoke to me," he said.

I waited.

"He said he got the tools. He said his wife's brother had found other work. He said he didn't want me coming to his house."

"What did you say?"

"All right."

"That's all?"

"He hadn't asked for a fucking biography."

I smiled. So did he.

Before leaving, he gave me another piece of paper. An invitation to a meal celebrating Linh finishing the course she had been taking at night. She had asked him to make clear that it was a meal, not an opportunity for me to announce a contribution.

"She knows me poorly," I said.

"She knows you well enough to ask me to say that twice."

Shawna and I went.

There were more people than chairs, so the seating changed several times before anyone began eating. Linh's friends argued about which parts of the course had been useful and which had existed to make an instructor feel necessary. Hậu carried dishes and accepted instructions from three directions without pretending he was in charge of the room.

Linh looked at the gift Shawna brought and thanked her. It was something she had actually wanted, selected after Shawna asked rather than after I guessed what could be made impressive.

Halfway through the meal, Hậu caught my eye from across the table.

For a moment I saw the man tied to the chair, the purple hand, the torn ear, the careful lie that he could walk. Then somebody asked him to pass a bowl and he did, irritated at being asked before he had finished chewing.

I liked that version of him better than any debt could have entitled me to expect.

On the way home, Shawna asked whether I was pleased.

"Yes."

"Because he repaid you?"

"Because he didn't need to ask what I wanted him to do next."

She took my hand across the space between our seats.

"Keep liking that," she said.

## 26. Come Home

The last man who came to recover Bảo's chair brought six people to help him explain why it still belonged to someone else.

They arrived on an afternoon when we were replacing a section of the office roof. The old chair stood outside under a plastic sheet, waiting for somebody to decide whether it could be repaired. One wheel had finally broken. Ngọc wanted to throw the whole thing away. Vân said there was nothing wrong with a chair that could still be repaired cheaply. I had not expressed a preference because I enjoyed watching them discover a subject on which they disagreed.

The visitor introduced himself, named two people whose importance he expected me to recognize, and said he had come to discuss continuity.

I pointed at the chair.

"You can take it."

He looked at the plastic sheet.

"I am discussing the organization."

"Then you'll need a better opening. That one has been outside since lunch."

His six men did not laugh. Two looked as though they wanted to. He saw them looking and turned toward me with an expression I knew well: a man preparing to charge somebody else for the embarrassment his own mouth had produced.

"Bảo had obligations."

"He's still alive. Discuss them with him."

"That is difficult under the circumstances."

"Being in custody has affected his availability."

"You benefited from his situation."

"I caused a considerable part of it. You're allowed to say that."

Rain began falling on the new roofing sheets. Above us, the men doing the work called to one another and moved their equipment. My visitor looked up, annoyed that an ordinary job had continued while he was explaining how important he was.

"We should speak privately."

"Sơn offered you the office. You wanted everyone to hear you arrive. Finish where you started."

He lowered his voice anyway.

The claim he described had a history and an amount. Vân brought out the papers relating to it. The amount was wrong. The history omitted a payment. The claim itself was not entirely invented, which disappointed me; complete lies were less work to untangle than an accurate fact carrying a rich man's luggage.

We went through it while his six men stood in the rain.

After a while I told them to come under the awning. Their employer began objecting, then stopped when all six moved before he had finished the sentence.

We agreed to have the remaining claim examined. He would receive what could be established, on terms appropriate to what he could actually establish. He would receive no part of the Circle. He would receive no right to visit a worker's home. I made the last point twice because his expression suggested he believed a boundary became less binding when the conversation lasted long enough.

"People used to respect an arrangement," he said.

"Bring one worth respecting."

He left with the same six men. One stayed behind long enough to ask Quang whether we were hiring. Quang gave him an application and told him the work included showing up sober and refusing instructions that turned ordinary security into somebody's private kidnapping service.

"Does that happen often?" the man asked.

"Less often when people refuse."

I heard about the exchange later. I told Quang he could use that line in the advertisement.

"Then everyone will ask what happened here," he said.

"They already do."

"Yes. I'd rather they asked before accepting the job."

By then Bảo's remaining partners had mostly stopped trying to recover the yard. Some had found other ways to make money. Some had discovered that their loyalty to him had been an estimate of what he could still provide. The estimate had changed.

The Circle kept its name.

I kept its rooms, its shares of wagers, its profitable arrangements with people who preferred my answer to a slower one they could not purchase. There were evenings when the money arrived with stories I did not enjoy hearing. I heard them. Sometimes I changed what would happen next. Sometimes I let a hard agreement remain hard because the person who had made it understood exactly what they were risking.

I had not mistaken a business for innocence merely because I answered its telephone.

Vân knew which accounts belonged to the yard and which did not. Shawna knew when I came home having made money she would never put into her work. My parents knew their son had chosen a life that would keep asking him to discover how much of himself he could excuse.

They still expected me at dinner.

That expectation could trouble me more than a threat.

Near the end of the week, Sơn called me to the gate. A stranger had arrived offering protection. He had declined to name the business issuing the offer, but he had asked several questions about the shifts and seemed especially interested in when the office emptied.

I went down without a jacket. The rain had stopped. Water dripped from the edge of the awning onto a dark patch beside the man's polished shoes.

He smiled when he saw me.

"You are younger than I expected."

"You are exactly as old as you look. We have both learned something."

He glanced at Sơn, trying to decide whether he could demand an audience without witnesses.

"I represent people who can make sure your work continues without difficulty."

"Give Sơn their names. If they need a quotation, he'll arrange it."

"A different kind of work."

"Then you'll have to become more useful at explaining yourself."

He looked through the gate. A driver sounded his horn from inside, asking the vehicle ahead to move. The stranger stepped aside automatically, then seemed irritated that his body had obeyed a request from someone he had not finished deciding was beneath him.

"A man with several interests can find it difficult to watch all of them," he said. "Your mother's printing business, for example."

I stepped closer.

That was all.

His next breath stopped halfway into his chest. He looked at my hands and found them empty. He looked at my face and decided that this was not an improvement.

"I was speaking generally."

"You used a particular person."

"No disrespect was intended."

"Then you can leave without having to explain your intention again."

He swallowed. Rainwater struck his shoe. He did not move it this time.

"The offer remains—"

"No."

He went.

Sơn watched him reach the road.

"I'll record the visit," he said.

"Please."

"Do you want someone at your mother's place?"

"I want her told what happened. She can decide what changes she wants."

"You'll ask her?"

"I'm not going to install six men in her doorway and find out what she thinks when they all call to resign."

He smiled.

We stood beside the gate until the driver who had sounded his horn returned on foot. He wanted to know whether the loading order had changed. Sơn answered him. The driver asked another question, irritated about the answer to the first. I moved aside so they could look at the board without having to look through me.

For a moment, neither of them remembered to be careful because I was there.

I had spent a year acquiring that moment. It was not the only thing I had acquired, or the most profitable. I noticed it anyway.

Mom answered on the second call. The first had gone unanswered because she was examining a delivery and refused to stop counting whenever her phone wanted attention.

I told her about the visitor.

She asked what he had actually said. I gave her the words, without improving them to justify how much I wanted to dislike him.

"Tell me if he returns," she said.

"I will."

"I'll speak to the staff. We can make sure nobody closes alone for a while."

"I can arrange—"

"You can come to dinner on time. Your father has already started taking the cupboard apart."

"Those sound like unrelated problems."

"Then you have not watched your father decide a five-minute repair deserves his full attention."

She ended the call.

I picked up Shawna from her atelier. She came out carrying a garment bag and a smaller bag containing a broken fitting that she intended to show Dad. Her assistant followed her to the door, still asking about an order. Shawna turned back and dealt with the question. I waited beside the car.

When she finally reached me, she held out the smaller bag.

"Could you carry this without improving it?"

"I improve everything I touch."

"Your shirt disagrees."

I looked down. There was a small tear near the cuff.

"Work."

"Yes. Mine, now."

She got into the car and put the garment bag across her knees. A client had decided a finished piece needed a last adjustment before the morning. Shawna had agreed because the adjustment would make it fit better, then charged for the time because the client had waited until the last possible moment to mention it.

"Aren't you pleased she trusted your opinion?" I asked.

"She trusted it three fittings ago. Today she trusted a photograph of someone with a different body."

"Can you fix it?"

"Obviously. That's why she's paying me to be annoyed."

We drove home through traffic that had never expressed any interest in my mathematical ability. At one junction, a delivery rider tapped the window to tell me that something was caught beneath the rear door. I stopped where I could and found a strip of fabric from the garment bag trailing outside.

Shawna looked at it, then at me.

"The smartest man in the world."

"I delegated the fabric."

"To the road."

"It lacked initiative."

She laughed so hard that she had to wait before thanking the rider. I gave him room to leave, and he disappeared into a gap I would not have offered to a bicycle.

When we reached home, Dad was kneeling beside the open cupboard. Mom held four screws in her palm and was telling him that the problem had started when he moved the spare towels.

"The handle was loose before that," he said.

"Because you kept opening the wrong door."

"That isn't how a screw becomes loose."

"Apparently it is how a conversation becomes long."

I put down the bags and left my shoes beside the door.

Dad looked up.

I bowed.

I was stronger than I had been the night I walked into Vinh's room. Faster. My shoulder no longer complained when I reached for something above me. I understood the jump well enough to repeat more of what had once surprised me, though I had learned to leave room in my calculations for the world doing something I had failed to ask about.

At the yard, men changed their voices when I approached. At dinners, strangers began introducing themselves before I remembered whether I wished to know them. People had started using my name to make requests seem larger than they were.

My father rested his palm on the back of my head.

I stayed there until my breathing slowed.

He did not hold me down. That had never been the point, even when I was sixteen and had needed the difference explained badly enough that he could have used the floor.

"You are home," he said.

"Yes."

"Then stop arriving at everyone."

I closed my eyes for a moment.

Behind me, Shawna set the garment bag on the back of a chair. Mom asked whether she had eaten. They began discussing the client's alteration, moving easily from food to sleeves as if neither subject required a boundary before the next could enter.

Dad took his hand away.

I straightened. He handed me the screwdriver.

"Finish that."

"You've already fixed it."

"Then it should be easy."

He stood and took the broken fitting from Shawna's bag. She described what it was supposed to do. He asked one question, turned it in his hand, and nodded.

"Tomorrow morning?" she asked.

"After dinner."

"Show-off," I said.

Dad looked down at the screwdriver in my hand.

"The handle."

I fixed the handle. It took less time than deciding how to make the act look voluntary.

At dinner, Mom asked about Hậu. Shawna told her about Linh's meal, including the seating problem and the gift. Dad listened while repairing the fitting on a folded cloth beside his plate until Mom told him that tools did not need to eat with us.

He set it aside.

We talked about Mười's retirement. About Quang's leg. About a customer who had argued with Mom over the color in a sample he had approved himself. Nobody asked me to list the people who were now afraid of me. Nobody congratulated me on the amount of money I had made that week.

When my phone rang, I looked at the name and turned it over.

"Urgent?" Dad asked.

"He wants an answer before somebody else tells him no."

"Does he need yours?"

"Not while I'm eating."

Mom went to the cabinet after we had finished the first dishes. She returned with a thin envelope. The paper had softened along its folds. I had seen it before among things she moved herself whenever furniture was being shifted.

"You asked me about my grandfather," she said.

Dad put down his cup.

Mom slid a photograph out of the envelope. A broad river lay behind a man in a worn shirt. One hand rested on a ferry railing. The other held the hand of a girl I recognized, after a moment, as my mother.

The man looked old. He looked tired. He looked as if whatever stood outside the photograph had already asked him to move and received an answer.

"Lê Văn Tín," she said. "My grandfather. Your great-grandfather."

I took the photograph by its edges.

"Your dad's father."

"Yes. Your grandfather Hòa is his son. Hòa says growing up with him made it difficult to be impressed by anyone else."

"Did you agree?"

"I married your father. I was willing to examine new evidence."

Shawna smiled. Dad did not try to hide his.

I looked more closely at the man beside the river. His free arm rested without effort. That was what caught me. Not the size of him, though he was substantial even in a photograph that had lost some of its contrast. The absence of preparation. He did not seem to be demonstrating anything for the person holding the camera.

I had spent a year discovering how much people could invent from the way I stood in a doorway. He looked as though he had forgotten that anyone might need convincing.

"I sent him photographs of you in 2004," Mom said. "You were two. We were in California. He called to give me an assessment before the envelope had finished making the journey. Your grandfather had already shown him the pictures on a screen."

"What did he say?"

"That you had a large head and I should stop worrying about your father's contribution."

Shawna made a sound into her cup.

"A medical opinion," I said.

"He was quite confident."

"I respect the methodology."

Dad leaned forward to see the photograph. I had watched him stop a car from rolling down a sloping drive with one hand while explaining to its owner that the brake needed attention. I had once seen a man my size hit him and stumble backward as though the building had objected. I remembered the effortless hand around mine on the night before I took the Circle.

He straightened now.

It was a small movement. It changed the room more than the photograph had.

"How strong is he?" I asked.

Dad studied the face by the river.

"Strong enough to have other things to think about."

"That sounds like an answer you give when you don't want to lose an argument."

"You asked for the wrong measurement."

Mom looked from him to me.

"Your father was not always so economical with his answers," she said.

"I find that difficult to believe."

"He was very impressive. He made sure people had adequate opportunities to notice."

Dad picked up his cup, considered something, and set it down again.

Shawna turned toward him with the expression she wore when a difficult client accidentally explained the real problem.

"Now I absolutely need to hear this."

"Some of it," Dad said.

"All the relevant parts," Mom said.

"Your definition will be generous."

"You married me with the evidence available."

He laughed then. An ordinary sound, deep and brief, from the man I still could not move when he decided to remain where he was.

From downstairs came another voice.

"If he's laughing, she has left something out."

Dad stood.

Mom closed her eyes for a moment, then smiled in a way I had never seen her use at work.

"You said tomorrow."

"I said we could arrive tomorrow if your father drove like a frightened postman. He took that personally."

Tín came up the stairs carrying a large covered pot in one hand and a folded umbrella in the other. Hòa followed with two bags and the patient expression of a man accustomed to being described inaccurately by a father who considered correction a form of exercise.

My great-grandfather was a hundred and five. His hair was white. His shirt had been ironed by someone who had given up arguing about the collar. He took the final three steps together, without jumping, simply lengthening his stride until the stairwell seemed to have been designed for a different size of person.

The pot held enough food for another family. When he put it down, the table groaned.

"Careful," Mom said.

"It's cooked."

"The table."

"Tell Minh to build a better one."

Dad reached for the pot. Tín laid two fingers across the handle and kept speaking to Shawna.

"He's buying your clothes now?"

"Repeatedly."

"Charge for the inconvenience before the material. You'll do better."

Dad pulled gently. The pot stayed where it was. He adjusted his grip. I watched the muscle in his forearm harden, then the other one, the quiet movement that had once stopped my entire body from making a decision.

Tín's two fingers did not move.

He looked at Dad.

"It will spill if you carry it that way."

Dad stopped.

"I was moving it off the table."

"Then ask your mother-in-law's grandson to put something under it. He looks unemployed."

"That's me," I said.

"I know. I was making the route difficult enough for you to enjoy finding it."

Shawna made a sound that she would later deny had been applause.

I fetched the thick wooden stand. Tín lifted the pot, waited for me to place it, and put it down. Nothing dramatic happened to his breathing. Dad stepped aside with an expression I had previously believed existed only on my own face after being told a lesson was finished.

"Did you try?" I asked him quietly.

"The table was old."

"That is not an answer."

"It's the one the table prefers."

Hòa put the bags away without entering the argument. One contained fruit. The other held a small metal component wrapped in a towel. Dad saw it and looked up sharply.

"Where did you find that?"

"In a drawer."

"Whose?"

"Mine, eventually."

Hòa covered it again before I could decide what part of a machine it had belonged to. There was a deep impression across one side, narrow as a thumb and far too clean to have been made by any tool I recognized.

Tín watched him fold the towel. His expression changed very slightly.

"Still keeping souvenirs," he said.

"It might be useful."

"So might an explanation."

Hòa smiled at his father.

"You first."

For three seconds nobody in the room reached for anything.

Then Tín laughed, full and enormous, and told Mom to stop looking at him as if arriving a day early required permission from the calendar.

We made room at the table. Hòa sat beside me. Tín chose the chair opposite Dad and tested its back with one hand.

"This will do."

"I repaired it," Dad said.

"I said it would do. Don't ask for a parade."

Mom served him. He looked at the photograph still beside my plate.

"That shirt cost too much."

"You wore it for years," she said.

"I wanted the money back."

Shawna looked at me. "A family policy."

Tín pointed at her with his spoon.

"She pays attention. Keep her."

"He isn't in charge of that," Shawna said.

My great-grandfather considered her for a moment.

Then he pointed the spoon at me.

"Pay attention faster."

I had spent the day discouraging men from threatening my family. It was becoming difficult to remember which side of those conversations had needed protection more urgently.

Dad asked about the drive. Hòa said there had been a delay beside a broken delivery truck. Tín had climbed into its cab to argue with the driver about what counted as unmovable. He said there had been no delay once somebody stopped explaining why the truck couldn't be moved.

"You moved it?" I asked.

"Your grandfather did," Tín said.

Hòa reached for the rice.

"A little way."

"Across two lanes," Tín said.

"It was blocking them."

"While I was still in it."

I put down my chopsticks.

Hòa looked mildly inconvenienced by the amount of attention the sentence had earned.

"He didn't want to step into a puddle."

"Was it loaded?" I asked.

"We didn't go through its paperwork."

Tín watched me try to build a useful question out of that answer. There was amusement in his eyes, but something else as well: the look of a man who knew where a measurement became an assumption and was waiting to see whether I would notice the boundary myself.

"Which of you is stronger?" I asked.

Hòa kept eating.

Tín put down his spoon.

"You're asking me?"

"You appear willing to answer questions."

"I am willing to explain why yours are poor."

Hòa reached across the table for the serving dish. As he moved it, the crack beneath the heavy pot made a small sound. One table leg folded outward.

I moved. So did Dad.

Neither of us reached the falling edge.

Hòa had put his left hand beneath it while his right continued holding the dish. The table stopped level. The tea in Tín's cup climbed one side, settled, and did not spill.

I looked beneath the table. Grandpa's chair had moved backward. One shoe remained flat on the floor. His other foot rested against the broken leg, keeping it out of everyone's way.

Tín watched him for a moment.

"You could have let Minh catch it," he said.

"He repaired it. He deserves to finish dinner before hearing the review."

Dad closed his eyes.

Hòa set down the serving dish. With his free hand he drew the broken leg back beneath the table. There was a quiet crunch. When he took his hand away, the table remained standing.

"That isn't repaired," Dad said.

"No. It's persuaded. You can make it respectable afterward."

Tín picked up his cup and looked at his son over the rim. For the first time that evening, I could not tell which man was waiting for the other to reveal something.

Then Hòa asked Shawna about her work, and the entire family accepted the change of subject as if nobody had just interrupted gravity to avoid a conversation.

I did not accept it. I had learned to recognize a problem that would take longer than the evening I first understood it existed.

Tín returned to the photograph when we had begun eating again.

"What were you telling him?"

"About his father," Mom said.

"Which version?"

Dad set down his bowl.

"He's had a difficult year."

"Then stop charging him another year to hear why you recognize all his mistakes."

The room went quiet.

Tín did not raise his voice. He did not need to.

"I am trying to keep him alive," Dad said.

"Yes. By teaching him to stop his hand while leaving him to invent what you did with yours. You think if you speak calmly enough, he'll mistake silence for wisdom. Sometimes it's only a man hoping nobody asks the next question."

Dad looked at the table he had repaired badly enough for another man to hold it together during dinner.

"Some of those questions belong to other people."

"Then leave their names out. Stop leaving yourself out."

Mom touched the envelope.

"That is why I was going to tell him."

Tín turned toward her.

"And you were going to make yourself the woman who waited at home while the men decided things."

"I have never said that."

"You let the edges of the story say it. Very economical. Your father does the same thing, but he has had more practice."

Hòa took another spoonful of rice and declined to look accused.

Mom's face changed. She was not frightened. I had seen her prepare to argue with a supplier who had arrived believing the documents would protect him from the person who could read them.

"I kept this family functioning," she said.

"You did," Tín said. "You also made decisions. Some good. Some expensive. You chose that man with your eyes open, then helped him become the man you now describe as if he arrived fully assembled with a toolbox."

Shawna had stopped eating.

So had I.

"You both love the boy," Tín continued. "Wonderful. Now stop making that an excuse for this beautiful fucking museum you've built around yourselves. Let him see where the walls were moved."

For a moment, all I heard was a motorbike changing gear outside.

Dad looked at Mom. She looked back. Neither asked the other to answer first.

Hòa set down his bowl.

"He will still think he can do it better," he said.

"Of course he will," Tín answered. "Look at his face. We aren't repairing that tonight."

I laughed before I could stop myself.

Mom began laughing too, unwillingly at first. Dad shook his head, then smiled. The room returned to us without returning to the same arrangement.

I looked at my living great-grandfather, at the photograph of his younger hand holding Mom's, at my grandfather beside me examining an orange as if the load he had carried that afternoon had been entirely ordinary. My father was still the man whose grip I could not escape. He was also somebody's student, somebody's difficult son-in-law, a man with a history he had not finished deciding how to speak.

The Circle would be there tomorrow. So would the people who wanted something from its head, and the people who had learned to ask what my promises cost. I wanted the work I had taken. I wanted the money, the rooms, the power to decide what happened when somebody brought a frightened person into one of them.

Here were people who had known me before anybody called me powerful. One had just decided that the strongest person at the table was a question he did not intend to answer for free.

Mom slid the envelope toward me so I could set the photograph down safely.

"Before I tell you about him," she said, nodding toward Tín, "you should know what your father did in 2000."

I looked at Dad.

He looked back with the same patient eyes that had watched me learn to hold a wrench.

"Eat first," he said.

I ate.

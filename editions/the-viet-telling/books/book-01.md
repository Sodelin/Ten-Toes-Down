# Book I: The Price of Standing

## Chapter 1: Money at the Curb

The first man who handed me money had mistaken me for my father.

The second knew exactly who I was and apologized for giving me less than the first.

By the time Shawna got out of the car, I had nine thousand dollars, a gold watch, and somebody's parking space for the rest of my life.

We had come to Bell Street for noodles.

“Don't put any of that in my purse,” she said.

“We could buy another purse.”

“I already like this one.”

The man with the parking space was still holding his hands together. He had white hair, a purple tracksuit and a small dog wearing the same tracksuit. The dog looked like it had objections to the arrangement.

“You remember me?” he asked.

“Mr. Flores.”

He slapped his chest. The dog barked.

“He remembers!”

People outside the barber shop applauded.

Shawna looked at them, then at me.

“You told me you were weird in high school.”

“I was very good at geometry.”

“He threw a refrigerator at my landlord,” Mr. Flores said.

“The refrigerator was empty.”

“The landlord wasn't.”

“He moved.”

“Both of us did,” Mr. Flores said happily. “I got the downstairs unit.”

Bell Street ran six blocks downhill toward the port, and every block appeared to have been informed that my wife and I were coming. A butcher stood in his doorway holding a package the size of a mattress. Two boys had put a banner between a bus stop and a palm tree. It said WELCOME BACK, GREAT CHIGGA. The word GREAT had been painted over something longer. My cousin's old nickname for me had survived six years, three changes of bar ownership and, apparently, an unsuccessful attempt at spelling it on a bedsheet.

“You didn't approve the sign?” Shawna asked.

“I would have asked for a picture.”

“Of yourself.”

“So people know which great one.”

Somebody shouted my name from upstairs. A paper bag fell from a window. I caught it against my chest.

Inside were two hot pork buns and five hundred dollars.

“Auntie Loan says don't get skinny!” the woman at the window shouted.

“That's a lot of encouragement,” Shawna said.

I put one bun in her hand. She took a bite and stopped objecting to the neighborhood for almost four seconds.

Then a man in a cream tracksuit came sprinting out of Bishop's carrying a wireless microphone.

“Ladies and gentlemen, the motherfucking prodigal nigga has arrived!”

“Saint,” I said.

He threw both arms around me. He had gained a beard, a diamond tooth and enough cologne to qualify as an indoor weather system. When we were fourteen, he had tried to book me for a paid appearance at his own birthday party. We had argued about the deposit until his mother cut the cake.

He stepped back and held the microphone beneath my mouth.

“Say something for the people.”

“Your microphone isn't on.”

Saint glanced at it. His smile remained in place while he worked the switch with his thumb.

“That's why the people love you. Technical support.”

The speaker outside Bishop's screamed. Three pigeons left a roof. A bus driver stopped his bus halfway through the intersection, opened the doors and leaned out.

“Aiden?”

I waved.

“Look at this nigga!”

He started clapping. His passengers joined him because they were on a bus and something was happening. A woman in the front seat held up a baby. I waved at the baby too. The driver closed the doors and took the whole applauding bus downhill.

Saint watched it go with professional interest.

“I should have sold tickets.”

“To public transportation?” Shawna asked.

“I'm meeting my best friend's wife for the first time. Please don't attack my business model.”

She hugged him with her free arm. He called her Mrs. Greatness. She told him she had an actual name. He immediately announced Shawna Nguyễn to the street with more ceremony than he'd used for me.

The butcher brought the meat over.

“For your wedding,” he said.

“We got married last year,” I said.

“I wasn't invited.”

“It was very small.”

He looked at the meat in his arms.

“So was the cow when I started saving it.”

Shawna thanked him and opened the trunk. He laid it beside our suitcases, bowed to her, then whispered something in Saint's ear. Saint whispered back. The butcher took a photograph of me next to his business card.

“What was that?” I asked.

“Nothing.”

“Darius.”

“He can say you eat there.”

“I haven't.”

“You got a whole animal in your trunk. Eventually the claim becomes true.”

I put an arm around his shoulders and steered him toward Bishop's. Shawna walked on my other side, checking a message from the driver carrying the dress forms and sewing machines for her new atelier. She'd spent the flight home sketching a jacket on the back of a customs form. When I asked whether the jacket was for me, she said it was for someone who knew when to stop talking.

I still had hopes.

Bishop's had the same red chairs, the same tiled counter and the same woman behind it pointing at me before I reached the door.

“Don't you bring that suitcase in here,” Denise Bishop said. “I just mopped.”

“Auntie, it's good to see you too.”

She came around the counter, grabbed my face in both hands and kissed my forehead. Then she did it to Shawna, whom she had previously met only on video calls and already preferred.

“Look what she did to you. You got a collar. Shoes match. Teeth clean.”

“I had teeth before.”

“You also had a rat tail.”

“For eleven days.”

“I counted.”

She gave us the booth by the window. Saint slid in beside me before she could tell him where to sit. She told him to get out. He got out. She put a bowl in front of him at the counter, and he accepted this change of status without argument.

There were framed photographs above the booth. A baseball team. A graduation. My mother and Denise holding a cake shaped like a car. My father standing beside an actual car, one hand under its rear bumper, while Denise changed the tire.

The picture had been taken in 2008. He was wearing the same blue work shirt he still wore on Sundays.

“He charges everybody else,” Denise said, following my eyes. “Tell your daddy that.”

“For changing tires?”

“For acting like he doesn't want his picture taken.”

I had just picked up my chopsticks when Shawna's delivery driver pulled up outside.

He parked behind a black van that hadn't been there a minute earlier. Four men got out of the van. One carried a clipboard. Another carried a baseball bat. The other two were large enough to carry most of their conversations without props.

The clipboard man spoke to the driver. The driver pointed at Bishop's. Shawna stood up.

“Sit down,” I told her. “I'll see what they want.”

She looked at me.

“Those are my sewing machines.”

She went out first.

Saint left his bowl at the counter. Denise put a lid on mine.

“You just got home,” she said.

“I know.”

“And I used the good broth.”

The clipboard man was explaining a delivery fee. He had a narrow face, a bright white shirt and the expression of a man who charged for bad news by the syllable.

“Six hundred to unload,” he told Shawna.

“I've paid for delivery.”

“To the street. Unloading is separate.”

“The building is on the street.”

“That's convenient.”

The driver held his phone toward him. “Man, I work for the moving company.”

“Then you should know the rules.”

Shawna reached for the clipboard. The man pulled it back.

“You don't touch this.”

“Then you don't get paid.”

I came down the restaurant steps.

The man with the bat recognized me. His eyes moved from my face to the banner above the street. He leaned toward the clipboard man and said something very quietly.

“Who?” the clipboard man asked.

The bat man said it again.

I stopped beside my wife.

“You've got about six seconds to make this a welcome-home joke.”

The clipboard man glanced at Saint. Saint had both hands over the microphone, which made me suspect it was on.

“We're with the Wharf Circle,” the man said.

“Four seconds.”

He looked me over. His mouth tightened when he reached the slippers on my feet. I had changed out of my plane shoes in the car. Shawna said they made me look like I was escaping a hotel fire.

“You somebody important?”

Saint lifted the microphone.

“That's a very expensive question, nigga.”

The clipboard hit my chest.

The man hadn't swung it hard. He was making a point. Behind him, one of the large men smiled and reached for the delivery truck's latch.

I took the clipboard, folded it across my knee and gave the two halves back.

“Put it on separate invoices.”

The bat man dropped his bat.

The bigger man at the truck did the opposite of what a sensible person would have done. He grabbed Shawna's wrist.

She drove the heel of her free hand into his nose. He let go and staggered against the truck. She stepped backward, out of my way, without looking to see if I was coming.

I caught his jacket at the shoulder and belt, turned him sideways and threw him through the open sliding door of the black van.

He hit the far door hard enough to push it off its track.

Both doors now opened.

The second big man charged me from the left. I stepped toward him. His fist passed over my right shoulder. I put my left hand behind his elbow and drove my right fist into the soft space beneath his ribs.

Air came out of him in a long flat note.

I held his elbow so he didn't land on Shawna's shoes. When his knees reached the pavement, I let go.

The clipboard man had made it to the driver's door. He dragged a pistol from beneath his shirt, snagged the muzzle in the fabric and pulled harder. I crossed the distance before the shirt tore.

I trapped his gun hand against the roof of the van.

He squeezed the trigger.

The shot punched through the roof. Everyone outside Bishop's ducked. Saint ducked behind a flowerpot that covered neither his head nor his body.

I pushed the gunman's wrist backward over the edge of the roof. Something clicked in his arm. He screamed and let go of the pistol. I caught it by the barrel, put it on top of the van and drove his forehead into the window.

The glass broke on the second impact.

I let him slide down the door.

Behind me, the bat man held his empty hands above his head.

“I told him,” he said.

“You did.”

“I'm from Alder Street. I know your dad.”

“Then why are you holding your hands like that?”

He lowered them halfway.

“You shot?” Shawna asked.

“No.”

She checked my chest anyway. Her hand shook once against my shirt, then steadied. I closed mine over it.

The man I'd thrown into the van crawled out through the new opening on the other side. He took one look around the vehicle at me and sat down on the curb.

The moving driver had been recording. His phone was still aimed at the sky.

“Get the machines inside,” Shawna told him. “I'll show you where.”

She took the keys out of her bag and walked him toward the empty storefront two doors below Bishop's. The driver hurried after her.

For a moment the whole block was quiet except for the clipboard man trying to breathe through his nose.

Then Mr. Flores started clapping.

The barber shop joined in. The butcher lifted both hands above his head. Somebody upstairs shouted that I'd done the window wrong and could have used the man's elbow.

“He's rusty!” Saint shouted back. “Let the nigga warm up!”

A woman handed me a tissue for my knuckles. Another put twenty dollars in my shirt pocket.

“Auntie, you don't have to—”

“Take it.”

I took it.

Denise stood at her door with the lid from my bowl in one hand.

“Your food,” she said, “is getting disrespectful.”

I looked at the bat man. He was already helping his boss into the van.

“Leave the gun,” I said.

“Absolutely.”

“And the bat.”

“Wasn't mine anyway.”

They left slowly. The displaced door rattled against the side of the van every time it hit a bump.

I picked up the bat and carried it into Bishop's. Denise pointed at the umbrella stand. I put it there.

Saint sat across from me when Shawna returned. He had found a napkin and was making a list.

“What's that?” I asked.

“Welcome-home expenses.”

“You owe me money?”

“No, I'm trying to work out how much more we could have charged.”

Shawna took his pen.

I finally got a mouthful of noodles.

Outside, a white sedan stopped beside the damaged glass on the pavement. A woman in a dark green suit got out. She spoke briefly to Mr. Flores, who pointed at the restaurant with both hands.

She entered, nodded to Denise and laid a cream envelope beside my bowl.

“Mr. Lâm would like to see you tonight.”

“I'm eating.”

“I can see that.”

“Then you're already doing better than the last guy.”

Her eyes flicked toward the bat in the umbrella stand.

“Mr. Lâm would like to apologize for the misunderstanding.”

I opened the envelope.

There was an address, a time and a check for a hundred thousand dollars.

Saint put his napkin over the check so Denise couldn't see it. She lifted the napkin, looked, then set a second bowl in front of me.

“On the house,” she said.

Shawna put down her chopsticks.

“For what?”

Denise pointed at the check.

“Whatever the hell he's doing, I want him to remember who fed him.”

## Chapter 2: Shoes Off

My mother opened the front door, looked at the meat in my arms and shut it again.

“Mom.”

The mail slot lifted.

“Did you call first?”

“You told me to come at five.”

“Then why do you have a cow?”

“Wedding present.”

“You got married last year.”

“Nobody is happy about that, apparently.”

The door opened. She wore a black shirt, gray trousers and the expression she used when someone ordered wedding invitations without knowing whom they intended to marry. Behind her, the house smelled of fried garlic and the laundry powder she'd been buying since I was little.

She hugged Shawna first. Then she took the meat from me and pointed at my feet.

“Shoes.”

“They're slippers.”

“They're outside slippers. Now they're making outside in my house.”

I took them off.

Saint arrived behind us with my suitcase. He'd insisted on helping after I told him he couldn't expense a microphone to my welcome-home account.

“Auntie Mai! Looking magnificent.”

“You look expensive, Darius. Who owes you money?”

“Nobody. That's the problem.”

She took his face in one hand and turned it toward the window.

“Is that a diamond in your tooth?”

He smiled wider.

“That's crazy, nigga. What happens when you eat corn?”

“With respect, Auntie, not everything is an engineering problem.”

“Until something gets stuck.”

She gave him a bag of recycling to take outside. He accepted it with a little bow. Shawna watched him retreat down the steps.

“Does everyone on this street work for your mother?”

“Usually only when they come inside.”

Mom kissed me hard enough to hurt my cheek, held me for a second and then slapped the back of my head.

“I saw the video.”

“Already?”

“Denise sent it before you finished. Why were you wearing hotel slippers to fight people?”

“Grip.”

“One of them came off.”

“That was the weak one.”

She made a sound that meant she loved me and didn't intend to admit I'd said anything funny.

My father was in the kitchen, holding the refrigerator.

I don't mean he was leaning against it. The refrigerator was sideways in his arms. Its doors faced the ceiling. A bowl of uncovered marinating pork sat on the upper door while my mother's rice cooker steamed on the counter beneath it.

He looked over the refrigerator at me.

“Come help.”

I went around the table and reached for the other end.

“With the mop,” he said.

There was water under his feet. A supply hose lay beside the wall.

I found the mop in the closet and cleaned under the refrigerator while he held it. Shawna came into the kitchen. My father shifted the appliance onto one arm and held out his free hand.

“Good flight?”

“Long,” she said, shaking it. “Is that comfortable?”

He glanced at the refrigerator.

“It was in the way.”

“I can see that.”

“Aiden, you missed the corner.”

I moved the mop.

Saint came back in, saw my father and the refrigerator, and quietly took a photograph.

“Darius,” Dad said.

Saint put away the phone.

“It was for the warranty, Uncle.”

“Good. You can call them.”

Dad set the refrigerator upright, caught the bowl before it slid off the door and passed it to Mom as she came through the kitchen. She took it without breaking her conversation with Shawna about commercial sewing machines.

I put away the mop. When I turned around, Dad was looking at me.

I went over, bent from the waist and bowed. We had done this since I was six and he'd caught me announcing myself as head of the household because I could reach the thermostat. He'd said the job came with bills. I'd bowed and returned the title.

He put a hand on the back of my neck and brought me into a hug.

“Welcome home, son.”

For a moment I was small enough to fit under his arm again.

Then he stepped back, took my right hand and turned my knuckles toward the light.

“Window glass.”

“A little.”

“You should have used his elbow.”

Saint slapped the kitchen counter.

“I knew that was you upstairs!”

Dad looked at him.

“I was at work.”

“Then why did—”

“Because it was stupid,” Mom said, taking plates out of the cupboard. “Several people can notice the same stupid thing.”

Shawna laughed into her hand. I pointed at her.

“You're supposed to be on my side.”

“I'm in your mother's house. I haven't established voting rights yet.”

The back door opened. A man in a straw hat came through carrying a bag of oranges and a folding chair.

“Who's stupid?” he asked.

“Your grandson,” Mom said.

Granddad Hòa looked at me.

“Which one?”

“You only have one here.”

“Then why ask me?”

He put the oranges on the counter and hugged us both. He smelled faintly of chalk, though he'd retired from teaching years before. His shirt pocket held two pens, a folded grocery list and a metal ruler with one corner missing.

He set up his folding chair at the end of the table.

“We have chairs,” Mom told him.

“This one knows me.”

Saint studied it. One leg was bent inward, and the seat had been repaired with bright blue cord.

“That's a historical chair, Uncle.”

“It will be if you stop touching it.”

Saint took his hand off the back.

Dad brought the rice to the table. Mom arranged vegetables around the pork. Shawna reached to help and was given something useful to do, which was how I knew Mom had accepted her. Everyone else got told to sit down.

I took the chair beside Shawna.

Dad put his hand on its back.

“Move it left.”

I moved it left.

“More.”

I moved it again. The table leg was now between my knees.

“Dad.”

“Your great-grandfather sits there.”

“I thought he was coming tomorrow.”

“I thought you were arriving without punching a van,” Mom said. “People surprise you.”

A horn sounded outside. It was the high, furious horn of the tiny electric cart Great-Grandpa used when he wanted strangers to think he needed assistance.

Saint was already standing.

“I'll get him.”

“Don't offer to carry his bag,” Granddad said.

Saint stopped at the door.

“Why?”

“Find out.”

We heard the gate click, Saint's bright greeting, and the prolonged grunt of a man discovering something unfortunate about luggage.

Great-Grandpa Tín came through the front door in a white linen shirt, dark trousers and sunglasses bigger than his face. Saint followed him sideways, both arms wrapped around a canvas bag. The bag dragged a little on the floor.

“Put that down, nigga,” Great-Grandpa said. “You're making it tired.”

Saint set it down with a thump that shook the spoons.

“What's in there?” he whispered.

“My things.”

“Your things weigh more than me.”

“Then get some better things.”

Great-Grandpa took off his sunglasses. His hair was white, clipped close around the ears, and his face had so many fine lines that each smile arrived with reinforcements. He inspected me from the doorway.

“You got handsome.”

“Thank you.”

He inspected Dad.

“Where did he get it?”

Mom made a delighted noise. Dad pulled out Great-Grandpa's chair.

“Please sit.”

“Don't say please like you're closing a door.”

He hugged Shawna, accepted her compliment on his shirt and told her he'd bought it to annoy his doctor. Then he sat down and reached for the nearest dish.

Mom caught his wrist with her chopsticks.

“We haven't started.”

“I've started.”

Granddad moved the dish closer to him. Mom pointed her chopsticks at her father instead. He peeled an orange with great concentration.

We made it through half the meal before the check came up.

Saint did it. He had been trying not to do it for twelve minutes, which was a personal record.

“Uncle Minh, how well do you know Bảo Lâm?”

Dad kept eating.

“Enough.”

“He gave Aiden a hundred thousand dollars.”

“For what?” Mom asked.

“Coming to a party,” I said.

Shawna looked at me. I added the part about the Wharf Circle. Dad set down his bowl.

“Are you going?”

“Yes.”

“Then don't take their car home.”

“That's it?”

“You wanted directions?”

Mom put more vegetables on my plate.

“He wanted you to tell him not to go so he could explain his independence,” she said.

“I did not.”

“You took a breath for it.”

“I breathe regularly.”

Great-Grandpa snorted. Granddad put a piece of orange on his father's plate, and the old man ate it without looking down.

“You two are very calm,” Great-Grandpa said to my parents. “Twenty-six years ago, one of you came home with blood on his wedding shoes and the other wanted to know whether he'd saved the receipt.”

“That wasn't the same,” Mom said.

“No. Your dress cost more.”

Dad reached for the serving spoon.

“He'll make his own decisions.”

“Listen to this nigga,” Great-Grandpa said to Shawna. “When he was twenty-seven, his decisions arrived at my house on a tow truck.”

Shawna rested her chin on her hand.

“I would like to hear that story.”

“After he eats,” Mom said quickly.

I grinned at Dad. He looked at me for a long moment, then slid the pork dish to my end of the table.

“Eat.”

The front doorbell rang.

Saint started to get up again. Great-Grandpa touched one finger to his sleeve.

“You already met the bag. Let somebody else have a turn.”

Dad went to the door. A man outside asked for me. His voice was loud, cheerful and completely wrong for our porch.

“Mr. Lâm sent a courtesy vehicle.”

“He has a car,” Dad said.

“This one's bulletproof.”

“He has a car.”

I stood up and went into the hall. The driver wore a black suit with a purple pocket square. He had one foot across the threshold. Dad looked down at it.

“Take that out.”

“Sir, I'm just here to—”

Dad took him by the front of the jacket, lifted him six inches off the floor and turned him around. He held him over the porch while reaching past him to open the screen door.

The man hung rigidly with both hands pressed to his trouser seams.

Dad set him on the top step, brushed a wrinkle out of his lapel and nodded toward the driveway.

“Tell Bảo thank you.”

The man walked to the car without bending either knee very much.

I watched him get in.

“You could have let me answer,” I said.

Dad closed the door.

“My house.”

I put a hand against the door before it latched. He glanced at it.

“Son.”

I pushed. He kept one finger on the brass knob. The door didn't move.

I planted my feet and pushed harder.

He looked past me toward the kitchen.

“Mai, did you put ginger in this?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

I put my shoulder against the door. The wooden frame creaked. Dad took his finger off the knob, caught my shirt as I stumbled forward and set me back where I'd started.

“Don't break the house,” he said.

From the kitchen, Great-Grandpa called, “Nigga, you broke that same frame in 2003!”

Dad shut his eyes for half a second.

I returned to dinner smiling.

Granddad had put his folding chair in my old place. Its bent leg was straight now. I looked at it, then at him.

He held up an orange segment.

“Want one?”

Under the table, Great-Grandpa nudged the chair's leg with his shoe. Granddad nudged it back. Neither looked down.

The thick oak table moved an inch toward the wall.

Mom slapped its top.

“Both of you. I have hot soup.”

The table stopped moving.

Granddad handed me the orange. Great-Grandpa winked at Shawna. Dad ate another mouthful of rice.

Saint leaned close to me.

“I need you to ask who's paying to fix my back.”

I looked at the canvas bag beside the door.

“What did he put in it?”

“I opened it while he was arguing.”

“And?”

Saint swallowed.

“A hat.”

## Chapter 3: Spring

The address on the invitation belonged to a boxing gym called Honest Work.

There was no sign explaining the joke.

Saint parked across the street and turned off the engine. He had changed into a black suit, replaced his diamond stud with a larger diamond stud and brought a second microphone in case an opportunity survived the first.

“You're staying in the car,” I said.

“Nigga, I have been waiting twenty years to say we're on the list.”

“We're on a different kind of list.”

“Every list is a different kind when you haven't been on it.”

I got out. He got out. I looked over the roof at him.

“My mother will kill you.”

“Your mother likes my initiative.”

“She gave you recycling.”

“And trusted me to complete it.”

Two men stood outside the gym beneath a yellow light. The older one recognized me and opened the door before I reached him. The younger one put a hand on Saint's chest.

“Just Mr. Trần.”

Saint pointed at me.

“I'm his—”

“Publicist,” I said.

Saint paused for half a beat.

“Publicist. Thank you.”

The younger man looked at his colleague, who shrugged. We went inside.

“You just invented my job,” Saint whispered.

“Try to keep it for ten minutes.”

The gym smelled of leather, bleach and money that had been counted with wet fingers. There was a boxing ring in the center, tables where exercise machines should have been and a bar built beneath photographs of men in trunks holding belts. Most of the tables were full. Some men stopped talking when I entered. Others leaned closer to the men who'd stopped.

A woman in the green suit from Bishop's came toward us.

“Aiden. Trinh Lê.”

“You didn't introduce yourself earlier.”

“You were enjoying lunch.”

“You could tell?”

“You had broth on the check.”

She led us between the tables. Saint walked as if somebody might be filming from either side. He greeted a man he didn't know, complimented a watch he couldn't afford and accepted a drink that hadn't been offered to him.

At the far end of the room, Bảo Lâm sat in a red barber chair. A woman was trimming the hair above his ears. He wore a charcoal suit, no tie and a white towel across his shoulders. A bowl of peeled grapes rested on the arm of the chair.

He held out one hand.

“Aiden.”

I shook it. He had rings on four fingers, each carefully arranged to avoid getting squeezed.

“My apologies for today.”

“Put them in writing next time. It keeps the van intact.”

His mouth twitched. The woman with the scissors smiled into his hair.

He pointed at a chair beside him.

I stayed standing.

“You wanted to see me.”

“I did.”

“I'm here.”

Bảo looked at Trinh. She took a slim folder from beneath her arm and put it on the table. Saint immediately found a place to set his drink.

“You know what the Circle does,” Bảo said.

“Takes money from delivery drivers.”

“A misunderstanding.”

“I'm getting a lot of those lately.”

He took a grape from the bowl.

“We run clubs. Games. Private events. We settle certain disputes before people make them public. We have enemies, naturally.”

“Naturally.”

“And occasionally we find somebody we would rather invite than inconvenience.”

Saint nodded as though he had personally developed this strategy.

“You have a remarkable family,” Bảo continued.

“They're busy tonight.”

The woman cutting his hair stopped moving her scissors. Bảo looked at me for a moment, then laughed.

“Your father said something like that to me once.”

“How much did it cost you?”

He put down the grape.

“Would you like to make money, Aiden?”

I tapped the check in my jacket pocket.

“I'm having a good day already.”

Trinh opened the folder. There was a photograph of a room with green felt tables, a narrow stage and a stained-glass window above the bar.

“The upstairs floor of the Pearl,” she said. “Games three nights a week. Fully staffed. A share of the house.”

“What share?”

“Twenty percent.”

“Forty.”

Saint took a slow drink.

Bảo smiled at him, then at me.

“You're confident.”

“You keep paying me for conversations.”

“Aiden, everyone here went through an introduction.”

I looked at the ring.

“There it is.”

Bảo lifted one shoulder beneath the towel.

“A little entertainment. People have heard stories. They'd like to meet the man.”

“The man brought a publicist,” Saint said. “So appearances are separate.”

Trinh looked at him with genuine interest for the first time.

“What does he charge?”

“Depends what he has to take off.”

I pointed at the photograph.

“Forty percent. And if I win, those four men leave my wife's deliveries alone.”

“Of course.”

“All her deliveries. All the drivers. All the little imaginary fees.”

Bảo's eyes went flat for a second. Then he nodded.

Trinh took a pen out of her pocket and amended the page. I read it. Saint read over my shoulder, moving his lips only at the numbers.

“You can read,” I told him.

“I like hearing good news twice.”

The room began to fill around the ring before I took off my jacket. A man in a sleeveless black shirt climbed between the ropes. He had a shaved head, a thick neck and scar tissue above both eyebrows. His hands were wrapped, but he wasn't wearing gloves.

“Who's that?” I asked Trinh.

“Milo Voss.”

“What does he do?”

“This.”

Voss jumped once, rolled his shoulders and hit a hanging bag beside the ring with a short right hand. The chain snapped at its top link. The bag dropped flat on the floor.

Men at the nearest table stopped pushing money toward my side of the betting sheet.

Saint watched the bag, then me.

“It's probably old equipment.”

A broad man standing at the foot of the ring snorted. He held a tray of bottled water and a hot dog wrapped in a napkin.

“They put that bag up yesterday.”

Saint leaned toward him.

“You work here, nigga?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then show a little customer service.”

The man looked at me. “Hậu Phạm.”

“Aiden.”

“I know.”

He gave me a bottle and lowered his voice.

“Watch the left elbow. He broke a man's jaw with it last month. Said he slipped.”

“Did he?”

“Afterward. There was blood everywhere.”

I handed the unopened bottle to Saint and climbed through the ropes.

## Chapter 5: Heavy Delivery

## Chapter 26: How They Spent It

## Chapter 27: Places We Could Go

“Battery?” I asked.

“Saint brought one.”

He tested the alarm. It squealed. From the kitchen, Great-Grandpa shouted that if dinner was on fire, somebody should tell the rice.

Dad smiled and climbed down.

I bowed.

He put his palm behind my neck, as he had when I was small, and pulled me close enough to feel his kiss against my temple.

“You late?” he asked.

“I'm early.”

“Good. Your mother needs chairs.”

“How many?”

“More than she said.”

The chairs were in the garage. So were a dismantled lawn mower, two engines, a shelf of tools and a wooden crate with a shipping label so old that the ink had nearly vanished. Dad moved the lawn mower with his foot and pointed at a stack beneath the window.

I took four chairs in each hand.

“Careful of the paint,” he said.

“On the chairs?”

“House. Chairs will survive you.”

Behind him, the garage door began to descend. Saint had found the wall switch and pressed it under the mistaken impression that every button in a room existed to improve the lighting.

Dad put one hand up without turning. The lower edge of the door struck his palm and stopped. The motor continued humming. The hinges along the upper panels trembled.

“Other button,” Dad said.

Saint pressed another one.

The outside sprinkler came on.

“Nigga, how many kinds of darkness does this house have?”

Dad reached back with his free hand, pressed the correct switch, and lowered his arm as the door rose.

I carried the chairs inside before Saint discovered the gas.

Grandpa Hòa sat in his familiar folding chair near the kitchen doorway, tying a fresh piece of blue cord around the bent leg. There were better chairs in the house. There were chairs in our house that cost more than the car I'd owned at seventeen. He continued to bring this one.

“You could let me fix that,” I said.

“It is fixed.”

“It has a knot.”

“Good knot.”

He tugged it once. The frame settled level beneath him.

“Where did it get bent?” I asked.

Great-Grandpa looked over from the counter.

Grandpa kept working on the cord.

“Chair had a difficult afternoon.”

“Was it near a railway bridge?”

The old man's fingers paused.

Only for a moment.

Then he tucked the loose end beneath the knot and looked up at me.

“Your father needs help with the table.”

Great-Grandpa bit into a slice of fruit. He was smiling around it.

I pointed at him.

“You wrote on that photograph.”

“I can write on several things.”

“You wrote Ask Hòa.”

“And you asked him. Excellent system.”

He handed me a piece of fruit and walked away before I could improve the question.

***

The table had acquired an extra leaf and an argument.

Mom wanted it moved closer to the kitchen. Dad said it was already close to the kitchen. Mom demonstrated that it could be closer by pushing the nearest chair into his shin.

“There,” she said. “Space.”

We moved it.

She examined the distance.

“Too close.”

Dad looked at me across the tabletop.

I kept my face still.

“You laughing?” he asked.

“No.”

“Good.”

We moved it back three inches.

Hậu arrived carrying two bags of fruit and a box containing a horn.

He set the horn on the counter.

I stared at it.

“You bought one.”

“You promised.”

“For the gym.”

“I'm demonstrating.”

He squeezed it. The blast rattled a spoon in a cup. Everyone in the kitchen stopped moving.

Great-Grandpa took the horn from him, examined it, and carried it to the back door.

He opened the door.

We heard a short, astonished honk from somewhere beyond the fence.

He came back empty-handed.

“Demonstration finished.”

Hậu looked at me.

I spread my hands.

“You want it back, you ask him.”

Hậu picked up the fruit bags.

“I brought oranges.”

Rafa arrived with his girlfriend and an immaculate shirt she had apparently selected under protest. Voss came with his sister. Loan appeared with a dish Mom claimed she didn't need and immediately made room for. Denise entered without knocking, kissed Mom's cheek, and ordered Saint away from the food with the precision of someone moving furniture she'd moved before.

The room filled. Chairs scraped. People reached around each other for plates. My grandmother Hương arrived from the backyard, where she'd been cutting herbs, and put a hand on Hòa's shoulder as she passed. He caught her fingers and held them until she bent down to hear whatever he wanted to say.

She laughed, struck his shoulder with the herbs, and continued to the kitchen.

“What did you say?” I asked.

“Private lesson.”

“You teach a lot of those.”

“Excellent students.”

At the other end of the room, Dad was attempting to carry a full tray through a gap Saint had filled with his entire body. Saint stepped left. Dad stepped left. Saint stepped right. Dad remained where he was and waited until Saint understood that only one of them needed to continue making choices.

We sat.

Hậu had the seat nearest the rice. He looked at it, then at Mom, with the tenderness of a man discovering he had been remembered in a will.

Great-Grandpa watched Dad serve Mom before filling his own plate.

“Look at this,” he said. “Manners.”

Mom lifted an eyebrow.

“He's always had manners.”

The old man laughed so hard he had to put down his chopsticks.

Dad continued serving rice.

“Careful,” Mom told her grandfather.

“He came to my house wearing a jacket that still had another man's tooth in the pocket.”

“It wasn't his jacket,” she said.

Great-Grandpa pointed at her.

“Listen to the defense.”

“It was a borrowed jacket.”

“I didn't ask who owned the tooth.”

Saint folded his hands beneath his chin.

“Please continue.”

Mom handed him a bowl.

“Please eat.”

Dad's ears had gone a little red. I had seen him bend steel. I had seen him stop machinery large enough to flatten our street. Watching my mother inspect him over the rim of her glass remained one of the few things I couldn't do to him myself.

“What happened to the jacket?” Shawna asked.

“Cleaned it,” Dad said.

“What happened to the tooth?”

Great-Grandpa leaned forward.

“Your mother-in-law made him put it in an envelope.”

Mom covered her eyes.

“It was disgusting.”

“She wrote Lost Property.”

Denise put her fork down and laughed into her napkin.

Dad looked at Mom. She looked back. For a moment their faces changed in the same way at the same time, and I could see the two people they had been before any of us had begun watching them.

Then Mom took another spoonful of rice and said, “He also forgot the soap.”

“I got the soap.”

“The next day. It was after midnight.”

“On the way home.”

“On a different day.”

“It was a large soap.”

Great-Grandpa slapped the table.

“A romantic!”

***

After dinner, we cleared the plates while the people who claimed to have cooked enough in their lives supervised us from their chairs.

Dad asked me to bring a box down from the cupboard above the hall closet. It contained old photographs, a packet of cards, and a cream envelope with his name written across the front.

The envelope slipped free when I put the box on the table.

Dad had written an address on the back, beneath a name I recognized.

“Calvin Rook?”

Dad looked over.

“Long time ago.”

“You knew him?”

“He paid me.”

Saint appeared beside my shoulder so quickly that I wondered whether he'd been hiding in the cupboard too.

“For what?”

Dad dried his hands.

“Standing by a door.”

Saint looked at the envelope, then at the doorway, then at his own shoes.

“I do that every day.”

“You talk too much.”

“How much did he pay?” I asked.

“A hundred thousand.”

I looked up again.

Mom came from the kitchen with tea.

“He wanted your father to make people feel safe.”

Great-Grandpa made a thoughtful noise from his chair.

“How did that work out?”

Dad took the envelope from me and set it beside the box.

“For some people.”

Hậu pulled a chair closer.

Dad glanced at him.

“What are you doing?”

“Getting safe.”

Shawna sat beside me, tucked one leg beneath herself, and took the cup Mom offered. Rafa closed the back door. Voss's sister asked whether this was the story about the motorcycle, and Mom told her that depended which motorcycle.

I looked at the people arranging themselves around my father.

Then at the cream envelope.

“You never told me about this.”

Dad shrugged.

“You were busy.”

“I wasn't born.”

“Busy elsewhere.”

Great-Grandpa laughed. Hòa leaned back in the chair with the blue cord, hands folded across his stomach. There was a small mark on the back of one hand that I hadn't noticed before, a pale straight line cutting across two knuckles.

He saw me looking and covered it with his other hand.

“One story at a time,” he said.

“Yours next?”

He smiled.

“Tea first.”

Mom refilled his cup. Then she sat beside Dad and took the envelope between two fingers, examining the paper as though she might still have an opinion about its price.

“This was terrible stock,” she said.

“Cost him a hundred thousand,” Saint murmured.

“Then your father could have bought better paper.”

Dad began to answer, but Great-Grandpa raised one finger.

“Before you start making yourself handsome, remember who was there.”

“I am handsome.”

“Your wife is in charge of that rumor.”

Mom straightened Dad's collar.

“And I run it very well.”

I leaned back. Shawna's hand found mine under the table. Outside, a car slowed near the house, then continued when its driver saw the rooms were full. My phone vibrated once with a message from Trinh. I checked it, saw that the Pearl was open and making money, and put it facedown.

Dad took the envelope from Mom and turned the address toward himself. A shopping list followed it in her younger handwriting.

He smiled at the last item.

“All right,” he said.

Saint reached for his phone.

Loan caught his wrist without looking.

Dad waited until we were all settled. His cup stood beside the old envelope. Mom's knee touched his. The man who had held a truck in the street, who had stopped a garage door without turning around, who had hugged me each time I came home, looked down at a list that included scallions and began to laugh.

Then he told us what happened when he agreed to stand by that door.

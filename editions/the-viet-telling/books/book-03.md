# Book III: The River’s Weight

## Chapter 1: The Refrigerator Gets a Better Offer

The refrigerator came with a free ice tray, a three-month warranty, and two men trying to steal it.

Tín had paid extra for delivery. He had lived in Eastbridge, California, for five years. In the summer of 1992, he still believed paying a man forty dollars meant the man would carry something.

He stood on the sidewalk outside Vân's grocery, holding the receipt while one of the men wrapped a chain around the axle of Bình's pickup. The other pressed a yellow notice against the windshield and smoothed it with his palm.

“Commercial loading violation,” the second man said.

The pickup occupied Vân's loading space. Vân stood beneath the sign authorizing customers to occupy it. She had owned the sign longer than either man had been shaving.

“That's my space,” she said.

“City jurisdiction.”

“Then go tell the city to buy its own refrigerator.”

The man ignored her. He wore a black polo shirt with HARBOR SECURITY sewn over the breast. Beneath the lettering, a little gold crown leaned to one side.

Tín examined the notice.

Three hundred dollars. Cash settlement available.

“You accept a check?” he asked.

“Cash.”

“Of course. Very advanced city.”

At the pickup's tailgate, Bình stood with his hands open. His right cheek had begun swelling. He was twenty-three, wore his hair high enough to require its own weather report, and had brought Tín a refrigerator strapped to a truck he was still paying for.

“I told them we were unloading,” he said.

The security man turned toward him.

“And I told you to shut up.”

Tín folded the receipt and placed it in his shirt pocket.

“Who hit him?”

The man at the chain looked up. He was broad, red-faced, and pleased with a morning in which nobody had made him work hard.

“He got mouthy.”

“I asked who.”

“Me.”

“Thank you.”

Tín walked past him to the refrigerator. He wore a white undershirt beneath an open short-sleeved shirt, brown trousers, and sandals Bích had bought because the previous pair embarrassed her in restaurants. His hair had gone almost entirely white. A paper birthday crown stuck from Vân's shopping bag beside the door.

Seventy today. Vân had insisted on the crown.

He had hoped the refrigerator would distract her.

“Where you going, Grandpa?” the chain man asked.

“Home. They're making lunch.”

Tín loosened the strap, slid his hands under the refrigerator, and lifted it out of the pickup.

The truck's springs rose.

Bình stepped backward.

Vân stopped opening the paper crown.

Tín held the refrigerator against his chest, its cardboard corners brushing his shoulders. A loose strap dangled across his sandals. He shook it away with one foot and stepped onto the sidewalk.

The security man stared at the empty truck bed.

“You can't remove seized property.”

“Then move the truck. My arms are occupied.”

He carried the refrigerator toward the corner.

From across Bell Street, Darnell Jackson came out of his tow shop holding a coffee mug. Everybody called him Boots. He had once arrived at a neighborhood basketball game wearing expensive cowboy boots and had spent twenty years attempting to make people remember anything else.

He watched Tín pass.

“You know they invented wheels.”

“I paid for them. They're being arrested.”

Boots looked at the truck, the two men, and Bình's face. His mug came down slowly.

“Oh. These motherfuckers again.”

The man with the notices reached under his shirt.

Tín stopped.

“Leave that there.”

The man's hand froze near his belt.

“I said leave it.”

Behind Tín, the refrigerator door clicked open. He leaned it against his shoulder and closed the door with his chin.

Boots set his coffee on the hood of a customer's car.

“My nigga carrying a whole kitchen appliance and you still picked the wrong moment.”

The chain man pulled a steel bar from the tow truck. He came forward with it held low beside his thigh.

Vân stepped into her shop and pulled the door mostly shut.

“Outside,” she called. “If you're doing this, do it outside.”

Tín set the refrigerator carefully on the sidewalk. He checked that its base rested flat, moved the receipt from one pocket to another, and turned.

The bar came across at his head.

He caught the attacker's wrist. The bar stopped a few inches from his ear. Tín twisted the man's arm outward and down. His knees hit the pavement. The bar struck the curb and rang.

Tín took it.

The other man drew his pistol.

Tín threw the bar through the open passenger window of the tow truck. It punched across the cab, struck the pistol man's forearm, and knocked his hand against the door. The gun dropped. He bent after it.

Boots kicked it under the refrigerator.

“Now you got a fucking situation.”

The kneeling man tried to stand. Tín pressed two fingers into the soft place above his collarbone. His knees returned to the concrete.

“You hit the boy with this hand?”

The man sucked air through his teeth.

“You can't—”

“I've been hearing that all morning. Answer something useful.”

“Yes.”

Tín bent his wrist until the man's face lost its color. He released it before the joint broke.

“Apologize.”

The man looked at Bình.

“Sorry.”

Bình touched his cheek.

“You made me swallow my gum.”

“I'm sorry about the gum.”

“And my face.”

“And your face.”

Vân opened the door a little farther.

“Tell him about the truck.”

“I don't know what you want me to say.”

“That you're a thief,” she said. “We can work on pronunciation later.”

Boots laughed so hard he had to hold the refrigerator.

The pistol man climbed into the tow truck. His partner looked over his shoulder and realized he was being left.

“Hey!”

The engine started.

Tín released the kneeling man and walked to the truck's front bumper.

The driver locked the doors.

Tín put his hands beneath the bumper and lifted the front of the truck off the street.

Inside, the driver slid backward against his seat. His cap fell into the space behind him. He tried the gas. The rear tires squealed, made two black marks, and stopped when Tín lifted higher.

The cab tilted toward the sky.

Boots picked up his coffee.

“I've been buying the wrong equipment.”

Tín carried the truck sideways until its front wheels hung over the curb. He set them down with the bumper touching a lamp post. The rear wheels remained in the street. The driver looked through the windshield at four inches of painted steel.

Tín tapped the glass.

“Unhook the pickup.”

The driver nodded.

“Leave the gun.”

Another nod.

“And the bar. It belongs to the neighborhood now.”

The man worked the door handle. The lamp post blocked it. He tried the other side. Vân's newspaper box stood there.

“I can't get out.”

Tín looked at his watch.

“I can wait.”

The window opened. The man squeezed through it, one shoe coming off inside the cab. He landed on the sidewalk in his socks and hurried to the pickup's axle.

A bus had stopped at the corner. Its driver opened the doors. Three passengers leaned out to watch the chain come away.

Somebody applauded.

The applause spread along the sidewalk. A woman outside Loretta's salon whistled through her fingers. The bus driver held out a folded transit pass.

“For your birthday, Mr. Tín.”

“How do you know it's my birthday?”

Vân pointed at the enormous banner hanging inside her window.

HAPPY 70TH, MASTER TÍN.

Beneath it, in smaller letters, Marcus Jackson had added: STILL TAKING APPLICATIONS FOR A WORTHY OPPONENT.

Tín looked at Boots.

“Your son needs employment.”

“I keep telling him. He says promotion is employment.”

The bus driver waved the pass again.

“Take it. Unlimited rides.”

Tín accepted it.

“Does it cover refrigerators?”

The driver looked at the white machine and then at the truck pressed against the lamp post.

“Yours does.”

Vân emerged carrying the crown and a bag of groceries. She put the bag on top of the refrigerator.

“Eggs, fish sauce, fruit. Birthday gift.”

“I ordered one appliance.”

“Now you have food to put in it. Stop making generosity difficult.”

She placed the paper crown on his head.

The kneeling security man rose at last. He cradled his wrist against his chest.

“You don't know whose truck that is.”

Tín looked back at the tilted vehicle.

“Currently, the lamp post's.”

“Duke Mercer.”

Boots stopped smiling.

Tín noticed.

“The man with the boat?” he asked.

“The River Queen,” the security man said. “You ever want to move anything through this neighborhood again, you come see him.”

Tín lifted the refrigerator. Vân steadied the groceries on top.

“Tell him I'm seventy. If he wants to threaten me, he should come before my nap.”

He started down Bell Street. The bus passengers applauded again. The driver closed the doors and followed at walking speed for half a block, apparently unwilling to let the afternoon become ordinary too soon.

Boots caught up beside him.

“You want help?”

Tín tilted his head toward the grocery bag.

“Take the eggs.”

Boots took them.

“Duke's not just some asshole with a boat.”

“Then he'll be pleased I remembered the boat.”

“Calvin Rook runs half the waterfront through him.”

“Which half?”

“The half that sends men to punch Bình over a parking space.”

Tín walked around a crack in the pavement so the refrigerator would stay level.

“Then we should introduce them to the other half.”

Boots looked up at the crown.

“That thing makes you worse.”

“I think it's working.”

At the house, Bích stood on the front steps holding a wooden spoon. She wore a clean blouse and an apron with a small burn near the pocket. Behind her, Hương crossed the hallway carrying a stack of plates. The smell of pork and warm bread came through the open door.

Bích looked at the refrigerator in her husband's arms.

“Delivery cost forty dollars.”

“I know.”

“Did you get the forty back?”

“There was a complication.”

“There's always a complication carrying something expensive up my steps.”

She moved aside. Tín brought the refrigerator into the house. Boots followed with the eggs held solemnly in both hands.

Hòa came from the kitchen, saw the appliance, and moved a chair out of the way without asking why his father was carrying it.

“Turn left,” he said.

“I know where our kitchen is.”

“The door frame doesn't.”

Tín turned. The refrigerator cleared the frame by less than an inch.

Mai stood beside the old refrigerator with a screwdriver in one hand. She had removed the decorative magnets and arranged them across the table in rows. One showed a smiling cartoon dog under the words HAVE A NICE DAY. Someone had drawn fangs on it.

“You wore the crown outside,” she said.

“I was conducting official business.”

She bowed deeply.

“Your Majesty. The freezer leaks.”

He bowed back without lowering the refrigerator.

“We shall investigate.”

Bích pointed with the spoon.

“Put it there, King of Back Problems.”

He did. Hòa pulled the old refrigerator away from the wall. Its rear feet scraped once, then rose clear as he shifted it. He carried it to the back door with one hand against its side and the other holding a glass of water.

Boots watched him pass.

“Does this family own a dolly?”

Hòa looked over his shoulder.

“My wife says we don't have room.”

Hương took the eggs from Boots.

“We don't.”

Nobody offered an explanation. Boots drank his cold coffee and stared at the empty space where the old refrigerator had been.

Tín plugged in the new one. A small light came on when he opened the door. Mai put the cartoon dog on the front, then moved it so the paper warranty would fit beside it.

“Happy birthday,” she said.

He kissed the top of her head.

“Thank you.”

She handed him an envelope.

Inside lay a photograph. Tín and Bích stood outside their house, both caught in the middle of laughing at something beyond the frame. He had one hand on his wife's shoulder. She had turned toward him with her mouth open and her eyes almost closed.

“When was this?” he asked.

“When Dad dropped the cake.”

From the back steps, Hòa called, “It was a difficult cake.”

Bích took the photograph. Her expression softened before she caught herself doing it.

“Good picture.”

“I developed it myself,” Mai said.

“You made me handsome,” Tín told her.

“That part was already a problem.”

He laughed and put the photograph on top of the refrigerator.

They sat down to lunch. Boots claimed he could stay only a minute, then accepted a plate, a second helping, and instructions from Bích to stop eating over his lap. Bình arrived with an ice bag against his cheek and a small wrapped box under his arm.

“A watch,” he said when Tín opened it. “I got it from a guy.”

Tín examined the face. The second hand moved in little uneven jumps.

“An honest guy?”

“He was honest about the price.”

“It's beautiful.”

Tín put it on beside the watch he already wore.

Mai leaned over.

“They disagree.”

“We'll give them time.”

The telephone rang as Bích brought out the cake. Hương answered in the hall. She listened, covered the mouthpiece, and looked toward the table.

“A Mr. Mercer. He says your birthday present is waiting at the roller rink.”

Boots set down his fork.

Bích looked at Tín's two watches.

“You have enough presents.”

Tín took a knife and cut the cake.

“Tell him I'll come after dessert.”

“He says it may not be there later.”

Tín passed the first slice to his wife.

“Then he should eat slowly.”

## Chapter 2: Everybody Bet Against the Old Man

The roller rink had a red carpet because Duke Mercer disliked entering buildings on flooring available to everybody else.

Tín found three boys standing beside it, each wearing a rented tuxedo jacket over shorts. The middle boy held a brass trumpet. The other two appeared to be waiting for him to improve.

“For Duke?” Tín asked.

The trumpet boy nodded.

“Does he pay extra when you stop?”

Boots coughed into his fist. Bình looked away.

The boy lifted the trumpet. One uncertain note came out.

Tín put five dollars in his pocket.

“For the silence after that.”

The boy lowered the instrument. His friends immediately began negotiating their share.

Inside, a painted moon hung above the skating floor. Colored lights moved across empty lanes and a row of arcade machines. The bowling alley occupied the back of the building, separated from the rink by a low rail. A snack counter faced both. Somebody had installed a chandelier over the shoe rental desk.

Boots looked up.

“Duke's mother told him to be classy once. This is what happened.”

At the rental desk, a woman offered Tín a pair of bowling shoes.

“Nine?”

“I'm here to see Mr. Mercer.”

“Everybody is. Nine?”

He accepted them.

She looked at his paper crown.

“Birthday boy?”

“Apparently.”

“Shoe rental's on the house.”

She stamped his hand with a purple star. Tín held it away from his shirt while the ink dried.

Behind him, Boots paid for his own shoes.

“I know the birthday boy.”

“Then buy him a drink.”

“Everybody getting rich off my friendship.”

A man in a green velvet tracksuit sat at the first bowling lane. He had a shaved head, a broad face, and a gold chain carrying an anchor large enough to catch a small boat. His shoes matched the tracksuit. Two women sat on either side of him with drinks in tall glasses. A third occupied her own chair several feet away, studying the score sheet.

“Mr. Tín,” Duke said. “I expected you sooner.”

“The cake was good.”

“Happy birthday.”

“Thank you. Where's my present?”

Duke spread his hands.

“We need to talk about what you did to my truck.”

“That's a disappointing present.”

The woman with the score sheet looked up. Her hair was tied back, and a pencil rested behind her ear. She wore a white shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow. When Duke shifted his glass toward the sheet, she moved the paper before it could get wet.

“Sable,” he told her, “bring our guest a chair.”

“There are six beside him.”

“A good chair.”

“They're all the same chair, Duke.”

Tín took one and sat.

Bình remained standing. Duke looked at the ice bag against his cheek.

“My men say you were uncooperative.”

“Your men asked for three hundred dollars to stand outside a grocery store.”

“Security is expensive.”

Boots leaned against the rail.

“Apparently health insurance is too. How's the wrist?”

Duke's smile narrowed.

He picked up a bowling ball with two fingers and turned it in his hand. The ball was black, with a white crown painted between the holes.

“I'm willing to call this a misunderstanding.”

Tín looked at Bình's face.

“Then understand it differently.”

Duke stood. He was taller than Tín by almost a head, and the tracksuit concealed very little of the body beneath it. People at the neighboring lanes turned to watch him walk to the line.

He swung the ball once and released it.

It struck the pins so hard that the pinsetter jumped. One pin came back onto the lane spinning on its head. Another disappeared somewhere inside the machinery.

The people behind him applauded.

Duke turned with both hands spread.

“I keep the neighborhood moving.”

From the machinery, something expensive fell over.

Sable made a mark on her sheet.

“That's the third repair this month.”

“Write down a strike.”

“I can write both.”

Tín picked up a ball from the rack. He looked at the finger holes, tried two fingers, and put it back.

“Too small.”

A young man beside the next lane snorted.

“Old man got special hands.”

Tín selected another ball and weighed it in his palm.

“I was referring to the conversation.”

Boots bent at the waist, laughing. Bình lowered the ice bag long enough to grin and regretted it immediately.

Duke came back to the table.

“You want something?”

“My friend's truck left alone. Your men off Bell Street. An apology from somebody whose apology matters.”

“That's a lot to ask over a refrigerator.”

“It has an ice maker.”

Sable covered her mouth with the score sheet.

Duke saw it.

“You know who owns the River Queen?” he asked.

“The river?”

“Me.”

“Congratulations.”

“Three bars. Two dance floors. A kitchen bigger than your house.”

Tín placed the ball on the table. The plastic tabletop bent beneath it.

“My wife would like the kitchen.”

“Your wife can buy a ticket.”

“She dislikes waiting in line.”

Duke looked around at the people watching. A few men stood now. Two had brought their drinks closer. The trumpet boy had entered through the front doors and was trying to find a place to put his instrument where Duke would not notice him abandoning it.

“Let's make this interesting,” Duke said.

Sable lowered the paper.

“Duke.”

“One throw.”

“Duke.”

He held up a finger without looking at her.

“You keep interrupting a beautiful idea.”

“I'm trying to catch it before it becomes expensive.”

He pointed toward the far lane. Its machinery had been removed. At the end stood a steel safe painted white, with a crown across its door. A thick chain ran through a ring welded to the top and down to two anchors in the concrete.

“Crown Lane,” Duke said. “Ten thousand to knock it over.”

Boots stared at the safe.

“That motherfucker got a foundation.”

“Anybody can try.”

“Anybody can try flying. Doesn't make the roof an airport.”

Duke ignored him.

“Mr. Tín knocks it over, I'll apologize to his friend. He misses, I get the three hundred and he comes down to the dock tomorrow to apologize to my men.”

“And my refrigerator's delivery fee,” Tín said.

“What?”

“Forty dollars. My wife asked.”

Duke laughed.

“Fine. Ten thousand and forty.”

Tín looked at the safe. The white paint had scuffs across the bottom where somebody had tried a very different kind of bowling ball. A small crack ran from one of the anchors to the side of the lane.

“If I knock it through the wall?”

The laughter stopped in pieces.

Duke looked toward Sable.

She shook her head once, firmly.

He looked back at Tín.

“Then I'll give you the boat.”

Sable stood.

“No.”

“It's my boat.”

“I know. I schedule repairs for it. Sit down.”

Duke's jaw set.

Tín smiled at her.

“What kind of stove?”

She stared at him.

“On the boat.”

“Six burners. Two ovens.”

“We should put that in writing.”

The crowd came closer. Someone shouted for odds. A man at the snack counter began taking money with a speed suggesting he had come prepared for several kinds of evening.

Boots took out his wallet.

Bình caught his sleeve.

“Against him?”

Boots looked offended.

“I've seen the refrigerator.”

Sable found a blank page. She wrote the terms, read them aloud, and made Duke sign beneath his own offer. Tín added his name. His handwriting was small and tidy. The pencil had a little rubber flamingo on its end.

“You can keep that,” Sable told him.

“Another birthday gift.”

“If you lose, I want it back.”

She went to the far lane and opened a side door. Two employees carried boxes away from behind the safe. She sent them outside, checked the service yard herself, and returned.

“Empty,” she said. “Nobody behind the wall.”

Duke rolled his shoulders.

“You really preparing for this?”

“I'm preparing for whatever stupid thing you say afterward.”

Tín walked to the line holding an ordinary red bowling ball. He had kept the paper crown on. A small child beside the rail waved at him with both hands.

He waved back.

“Don't encourage him,” Boots told the child. “He came like this.”

Tín set one foot at the line and looked down the lane.

Duke folded his arms.

“You want a practice throw?”

“I want the stove cleaned.”

He swung the ball.

It left his hand low, struck the lane once, and flew the remaining distance without touching the polished wood. The safe door folded inward. Both anchors ripped out of the concrete. The chain snapped taut, tore through the ring, and lashed across the empty lane.

The safe went through the wall.

Bricks burst into the service yard. Daylight opened behind the pins painted on the plaster. The safe struck a dumpster outside and shoved it across the pavement until it hit a fence.

The red ball came back through the hole.

It rolled slowly down the lane and stopped against Tín's shoe.

He picked it up.

One finger hole had become much larger.

“I'll pay for this,” he said.

Nobody answered.

At the snack counter, the man taking bets put both palms flat on the money.

Boots turned toward him.

“Don't even think about becoming religious.”

The child began applauding.

Then the whole building went up.

People shouted Tín's name. Somebody started chanting “Master! Master!” while Duke stood beneath the chandelier with his hands still folded over his chest. The trumpet boy finally found a note he could hold and put everything he had into it.

Tín raised the damaged ball.

“Thank you. Please don't stand in the yard.”

Boots approached the betting man with both hands open.

“My money has company now. Bring all of them.”

Bình laughed until his cheek hurt. Sable walked to the new hole, looked outside, and came back holding the signed paper.

She placed it in Tín's hand.

“The stove is clean.”

Duke moved at last.

He came toward Tín with his face hard and his right hand closing.

Boots stopped counting money.

Tín put the ball down on the return rack.

Duke swung.

The punch crossed the space where Tín's head had been. Tín stepped inside the arm, caught Duke by the tracksuit at the waist, and lifted him until both green shoes left the floor.

The anchor necklace swung between them.

Tín turned and sat him on top of the ball return.

The plastic housing cracked. Balls shifted beneath Duke's weight. One rolled out beside his leg and dropped into the rack with a clunk.

Tín kept one hand on his shoulder.

“We can finish the bowling first.”

Duke looked down at him. His face worked through several expressions, none of them helped by the fact that his heels no longer reached the floor.

Sable held out the pencil.

“Sign the receipt.”

“I already signed.”

“That was the wager. This is acknowledgment that you lost.”

Duke stared at the paper.

“You enjoying this?”

“I have waited six years to bill you for something personal.”

He took the pencil.

Tín removed his hand from the man's shoulder. Duke signed, climbed down from the cracked return, and faced Bình.

“My people shouldn't have touched you.”

Bình lowered the ice bag.

“Or my truck.”

“Or your truck.”

“Or charged Mr. Tín for delivery he delivered himself.”

Duke pulled forty dollars from his pocket and placed it on the table.

“Everybody has become very specific.”

“It helps,” Tín said.

As they left, the snack-counter owner pressed a box of pretzels into his hands. The woman at the shoe desk offered him the bowling shoes. A stranger tried to give him a gold watch and became briefly upset when Tín showed him that both wrists were already occupied.

“Then give it to your wife.”

“She knows what time it is.”

The stranger put it in the pretzel box anyway.

Outside, Boots carried the winnings in a paper bag. Bình held the bowling shoes. Tín had the pretzels, the watch, and ownership of a boat with six burners.

He stopped at the pay phone beside the entrance.

Bích answered on the fourth ring.

“Did you get the forty dollars?”

“Yes.”

“Good. Come home.”

He looked at the paper in his hand.

“There's one more thing.”

Boots leaned against the wall, smiling.

“How much room do we have for a nightclub?” Tín asked.

## Chapter 3: Your Grandmother Owns the Dance Floor

Bích took one look at the River Queen and asked where the grease trap was.

Duke had prepared a speech about chandeliers.

“You don't want to see the ballroom?” he asked.

“After the grease trap.”

“It's an elegant vessel.”

“Then the grease trap should be elegant.”

She went up the gangway carrying a market bag. Hương followed with a tape measure around her neck. Mai came behind them with her camera, stopping halfway to photograph the boat's name.

Tín waited on the landing while Duke stood beside a brass post with a rope looped through it. A square of dark water separated the barge's hull from the timber pier. Beyond the pier, the river opened east toward the harbor bridge. Cranes rose along the far bank. A tug passed carrying three men and a dog who appeared to outrank them.

The River Queen had once carried freight. Now a two-story white structure sat on its steel deck. Red trim ran beneath the windows. The lower doors opened onto a dance floor and three bars. Above them, a balcony wrapped around a smaller lounge beneath a striped awning.

“She doesn't have engines,” Duke said.

“My house doesn't either.”

“You need a tug to move her.”

“Then I won't lend her to the children.”

Duke looked at the signed papers in Sable's hands. She had brought a folder, a ring of keys, and the expression of a woman determined to finish one bad transaction before lunch produced another.

“Title and registration,” she said. “The operating business is separate. You get the vessel and what Duke listed on the attached inventory.”

“The stove?”

“Yes.”

“The chandelier?” Duke asked.

Sable turned a page.

“You wrote all fixtures.”

“I was under emotional pressure.”

“You were holding a pencil.”

Tín accepted the keys.

“Does the trumpet boy come with it?”

“Absolutely not,” Duke said.

“Good. I was going to release him.”

They went aboard.

Inside, Loretta Jackson was already standing in the middle of the dance floor. She wore a yellow dress, carried a handbag the size of a small overnight case, and had brought two women from the salon to help decide whether the room deserved them.

“We can put the band there,” she said, pointing at the stage. “Food along the windows. People can sit outside if they want to pretend they came for the view.”

“Who invited you?” Duke asked.

Loretta looked him up and down.

“The new administration.”

Boots entered carrying a box of records. Marcus followed with a speaker on one shoulder and an extension cable trailing from his hand.

“Grandmaster's birthday cruise,” Marcus announced. “I got the flyer designed in my head.”

Tín looked at the speaker.

“My birthday was yesterday.”

“We're extending the season.”

“Without asking?”

“You bought a boat without asking. We're communicating at your level.”

Hương laughed from the kitchen doorway.

Duke turned toward Sable.

“They've invaded.”

“You're standing on their dance floor.”

Mai took a photograph of him beneath the chandelier. He straightened automatically, then realized what she had done.

“Give me that one.”

“Five dollars when it's printed.”

“I used to own this place.”

“Then you can afford six.”

Tín watched his granddaughter put the camera away and open a notebook. She and Marcus began arguing over the name of the event. Marcus wanted THE GRANDMASTER'S SUMMER REIGN. Mai wanted people to know there would be food.

“People understand reign,” he said.

“They understand lunch better.”

“You can't put lunch in gold letters.”

“Watch me.”

Bích emerged from the kitchen.

“The grease trap is filthy.”

Duke closed his eyes.

“Sable.”

“You fired the last cleaner for leaving during your speech.”

“He had a bad attitude.”

“He had another job.”

Bích put down the market bag.

“We need volunteers.”

Everyone looked at Duke.

“No,” he said.

Tín smiled.

“An elegant vessel.”

Ten minutes later, Duke knelt in the galley wearing rubber gloves while Bích supervised from a stool. Loretta's friends carried tablecloths upstairs. Boots checked a socket with a little tester he distrusted. Marcus set a record on the turntable and immediately received three different instructions about volume.

Tín stood at the window facing the pier.

Two black cars had stopped beside the landing.

A young woman stepped out of the first. She wore a gray suit and carried no handbag. Her dark hair was cut just below the jaw. Two men got out behind her. The second car remained running, its windows closed.

Sable came beside Tín.

“Iris Rook.”

“Calvin's wife?”

“Daughter.”

“He named her after a flower and sent her to collect his boat?”

“He sends her when he'd like people to keep their teeth.”

Tín looked at the men following Iris.

“Whose teeth?”

Sable did not answer.

Iris stepped onto the gangway. Its boards barely moved beneath her. She entered the ballroom, glanced at the speakers and tablecloths, and stopped beneath the chandelier.

Marcus took the record off the turntable.

The sudden silence annoyed Loretta more than the arrival.

“Put that back,” she told him. “She didn't rent the air.”

He lowered the needle. Music resumed quietly.

Iris faced Tín.

“Mr. Lê.”

“Tín is fine.”

“My father would like to resolve an error.”

“He should speak to his doctor.”

One of the men behind her laughed before he could stop himself. Iris turned her head a fraction. He looked down at the floor.

She produced an envelope from inside her jacket.

“Twenty-five thousand dollars. You return the vessel, keep the money, and enjoy your birthday.”

Tín looked into the kitchen. Duke had stopped cleaning.

Bích tapped the grease trap with her spoon.

Tín tapped the hood.

“You can walk.”

“My father didn't authorize that.”

Bích took the pan away.

“Sit down.”

## Chapter 4: The Man with Two Tow Trucks

Calvin Rook brought a wheelchair to Boots's garage.

It was red, had a padded seat, and carried a ribbon across the back. Two men unloaded it from a van while Rook watched from beneath the shop's awning. His suit was cream. His shoes had never been near anything Boots would call honest work.

Tín sat on a folding chair beside the open bay while Bình changed the oil in his truck. His bandaged left arm rested across his lap. On the floor beside him stood a paper bag containing the forty dollars he had retrieved for Bích and a pair of peaches Vân had insisted he take on the way over.

Rook looked at the chair, the bandage, and the paper crown hanging from the truck's mirror.

“Something more appropriate for your age.”

Tín leaned forward.

“Electric?”

Rook's smile paused.

“Yes.”

“Excellent.”

He stood, walked to the wheelchair, and examined the controls. Boots came out of the bay wiping his hands on a rag.

“You bring the charger?” Boots asked.

Rook looked at him.

“What?”

“These things need a charger. You can't give a man half a present.”

One of Rook's men returned to the van and fetched a cardboard box.

Tín tested the seat with his palm.

“Mr. Benítez will love this.”

Across the street, the cobbler looked up from a shoe clamped between his knees. He was sitting outside his shop because Loretta had told him to stop spending the whole day beneath a fluorescent tube.

“What's that?” he called.

“A birthday present.”

“For who?”

“You're about to have one.”

Boots wheeled the chair across the street. Rook watched the ribbon bob over the uneven pavement.

“It was for you.”

“I'm sharing.”

Mr. Benítez transferred from his stool into the padded seat with Boots holding the frame steady. He tried the control. The chair moved forward. He released it, turned, and came back with a grin spreading beneath his gray mustache.

“How fast?” he asked.

“Don't start,” Boots said.

“I'm asking academically.”

Tín took a peach from the bag and offered it to Rook.

“Thank you. You've made a friend.”

Rook left the peach in his hand.

“We need to discuss my river.”

Tín bit into it.

“I thought you owned half.”

“Who told you half?”

“A friend. He was trying to frighten me economically.”

Rook looked around the garage. A blue tow truck occupied the second bay. An older white one stood outside with its boom raised and a sign beneath the windshield: DO NOT TOUCH THE RADIO.

Boots returned before Rook could read the smaller handwriting beneath it.

“Don't touch the radio,” he said.

Rook looked at him once, then back at Tín.

“You won a foolish wager. Duke will regret it for years. I'll pay you fifty thousand dollars, and we can all enjoy watching him learn.”

“My wife has begun cleaning the kitchen.”

“I'll pay her as well.”

“That's between you and her.”

“You don't seem to understand what you've taken.”

Tín ate another piece of the peach.

“A stove, mostly.”

Rook's eyes hardened. He had pale eyes, almost the color of the sky reflected in the van's windshield. A small scar crossed the bridge of his nose. When he moved his head, the scar stayed white against his tan.

He stepped closer.

“People use that berth because I permit it.”

“You can come on Saturday next week. We haven't printed invitations.”

“I am offering you a profitable ending.”

“I'm offering you lunch.”

The van's side door slid open.

A large man stepped down onto the pavement. The van rose a little behind him. He had short dark hair, a thick neck, and a narrow silver plate visible along the side of his jaw when he turned. His black shirt was buttoned to the throat despite the heat.

Boots glanced toward Bình. The younger man put down his wrench and moved away from the open truck hood.

“Tom,” Rook said.

The man came forward.

Tín looked at the silver plate.

“Dental work?”

“Nail,” the man said.

“Unfortunate dentist.”

Boots made a strangled sound and pretended to cough.

Nail put one hand on the blue tow truck's fender. His fingers pressed into the metal. The fender crumpled beneath his palm with a soft series of clicks.

Boots stared.

“That was painted Tuesday.”

Nail looked at him.

“Now it's Wednesday.”

Tín set the peach on a clean piece of paper beside the chair.

He took Nail's wrist.

“Take your hand off.”

Nail tried to keep it there.

The fender bent farther. Tín lifted the wrist. Nail's fingers came free one at a time. His shoulder rose until he had to straighten to follow it.

For several seconds they stood facing each other beside the truck. Nail's forearm trembled. Tín's expression remained mildly disappointed, the look he gave Bình when the young man claimed a car's strange noise was probably temporary.

Nail brought his other fist up.

Tín pushed his captured arm across his chest. The fist struck Nail's own elbow. Bone hit bone with a crack. Nail staggered back into the van and dented the sliding door.

Rook caught his arm before he could come forward again.

“Enough.”

Nail looked at him, breathing through his nose.

Tín picked up the peach.

“The fender,” Boots said.

Rook removed a roll of cash from his pocket. He peeled off several bills and placed them on the hood.

Boots counted them without touching them.

“Labor.”

Another bill.

“Paint matching.”

Another.

“Pain and suffering.”

Rook looked at him.

Boots pointed at the fender.

“I've named that truck. We're close.”

Rook added one more bill and turned toward the van.

“Enjoy your party, Mr. Lê.”

“Tín is fine.”

“For now.”

He climbed inside. Nail followed, forcing the dented door closed with his shoulder. The van pulled away from the curb.

Across the street, Mr. Benítez drove his new chair in a careful circle.

“Tell your rich friend thank you!” he called.

Tín raised the peach.

“He heard you.”

Boots gathered the money from the hood.

“That nigga came here to threaten us and paid for my whole week.”

“Keep some for the fender.”

“I'm emotionally attached to the money too.”

Bình came out from behind the pickup.

“Nail's the man from the dock fights.”

Tín looked at him.

“What dock fights?”

“Under the warehouse. They charge admission.”

“Of course they do.”

“He put a man through a shipping crate.”

Boots examined the van's disappearing shape.

“Was it painted Tuesday?”

Bình did not laugh.

Tín saw it and put a hand on his shoulder.

“You boxed there?”

“Once.”

“Against him?”

“No. I'm ambitious, not suicidal.”

“Did you get paid?”

Bình hesitated.

“They said the purse was being settled.”

Boots shut his eyes.

“Baby, that's rich-man language for theft.”

A horn sounded at the corner.

The white tow truck began rolling.

Tín turned.

Someone sat behind its wheel. The driver's head was low, his cap pulled down. Behind him, another man climbed onto the rear platform carrying a crowbar.

Boots ran toward it.

“Hey! Hey!”

The truck accelerated.

Its raised boom swung toward the pickup. The hook caught the pickup's front bumper and tore it sideways. Bình jumped away as the truck lurched out of the bay.

The man on the platform swung the crowbar at Boots.

Tín caught Boots by the back of his shirt and pulled him clear. The bar struck the metal door frame, showering old paint across the pavement.

The tow truck dragged the pickup toward Bell Street.

Bình ran after it.

“My truck!”

“Stay back,” Tín said.

He caught the rear corner of the pickup and lifted. The hooked bumper tore away. The pickup stopped across the garage entrance. The tow truck shot forward with the loose bumper swinging beneath its boom.

Tín jumped onto the pickup's hood, then to the roof, then across the gap to the departing truck.

He landed beside the man with the crowbar.

The man swung again. Tín ducked beneath it, drove a shoulder into his stomach, and pushed him against the towing boom. The crowbar fell into the street. Tín pulled the man's jacket over his face and tucked both sleeves through the hook's retaining loop.

The man hung bent forward, swearing into his own lining.

Tín climbed along the boom to the cab.

The driver saw him in the side mirror and swerved.

The truck mounted the curb beside Loretta's salon. Women scattered away from the doorway. A rolling rack of wigs toppled. One landed on the swinging bumper and rode with it, glossy black hair blowing behind the chrome.

Loretta came outside.

“You motherfuckers better bring that back!”

Tín reached the cab roof.

The driver fired upward through it.

Tín moved his foot. The bullet punched a hole beside his sandal. He drove his hand through the thin roof panel, caught the gunman's wrist, and pulled the arm up through the torn metal.

The pistol fell onto the roof.

Tín kicked it away from the crowd and into the open bed of an empty parked truck.

“Stop,” he said.

The driver pressed harder on the accelerator.

Ahead, Mr. Benítez sat in his new chair at the curb, unable to reverse around two pedestrians who had frozen in front of him.

Tín dropped from the roof to the driver's window. He put both feet against the door and pulled.

The steering wheel came off the column with the driver's hands still around it.

For an instant, the man stared at the wheel in his lap.

“That complicates it,” Tín said.

He jumped to the street, caught the front bumper with his good arm, and lifted the entire front of the truck. The rear wheels squealed. He turned the truck away from the curb and shoved it sideways across the road.

Its rear tires struck the gutter. The vehicle stopped facing the empty wall of a closed furniture store.

The loose bumper swung forward and hit the brickwork.

The wig remained attached.

Tín set the truck down.

Inside, the driver held the detached wheel against his chest.

“Get out.”

The man opened the door.

“Bring that,” Tín said, pointing at the wheel. “Boots will be angry.”

Boots arrived in the blue tow truck, its crumpled fender shaking above the front wheel. Bình stood on the rear platform, holding a length of rope.

They tied the driver and his companion beside the furniture store. Loretta came to retrieve the wig, inspected it, and slapped the driver with it twice before Boots persuaded her to stop adding hair to the evidence.

Tín leaned against the white truck. His bandage had loosened. A line of blood ran down toward his elbow.

Boots looked at it.

“Your wife is going to kill me.”

“She knows whose arm it is.”

“That makes it worse.”

The furniture-store owner emerged from a side door carrying a key ring. He stared at the truck, the tied men, and the steering wheel on the sidewalk.

“This your doing?” he asked Tín.

“Partly.”

The owner looked toward Mr. Benítez, who had finally found a clear route back to his shop.

“He would've been right in front of that thing.”

Tín nodded.

The man took a brass key from the ring.

“You need chairs for that party? Take twenty. Tables too.”

Boots put a hand over his face.

“I need to start having birthdays like this.”

Tín accepted the key.

“Thank you.”

The owner opened his doors. Inside, stacks of folding chairs lined the wall.

Loretta picked one up, tested its weight, and looked at the tied driver.

Boots gently took it from her.

“Party first.”

She let him have it.

Down the block, a siren began approaching. Tín sat on the curb while Bình brought water and a clean rag from the garage.

He looked at the white truck's torn roof.

“Tell Duke,” he said, “I'd like to see the downstairs room.”

Bình understood which downstairs room he meant.

“Tonight?”

Tín checked both watches. Neither agreed with the clock above the furniture store.

“After supper. These people keep interrupting meals.”

## Chapter 5: Nobody Ordered This Much Shrimp

## Chapter 6: The House Asked for Another Table

“With which arm?”

Bình looked at the sling.

“The optimistic one.”

Tín laughed and handed him a cup of tea.

“Heal first. There's a long supply of fools.”

“You never wait.”

Tín lifted his bandaged arm.

“Your auntie has mentioned it.”

They drank while the two boys Bình had been coaching argued over which wrap looked more professional. Bình corrected one of them with his good hand, his attention returning to the work.

Across the street, Hương measured a woman for a jacket while Hòa held the order book. Mai brought the finished flyers from the printer and divided them among the stalls. She had earned her first afternoon's wages helping Mr. Chen finish another job. She kept the folded bills in a separate envelope and refused when Marcus tried to borrow two dollars for a soda.

“Business expense,” he said.

“Your business.”

Tín watched her pass the flyers to Bích. His wife held one at arm's length, checked the date, and smiled.

“Good,” she said.

Mai looked more pleased by that single word than she had by all of Marcus's complaints.

Near sunset, the man from the furniture store brought another stack of chairs. He had painted a small crown beneath each seat.

“For the boat,” he told Tín.

“You already gave us twenty.”

“People keep asking where you got them. I'm having a good week.”

Tín shook his hand.

Behind them, Bích counted the day's stall money with Loretta and Rosa. They argued briefly over who had paid for ice, settled it with three bills and a promise, then began discussing how much food a floating party could reasonably hold.

Tín took the last tray home.

At the door, Bích caught his sleeve.

“Seven tomorrow?”

He nodded.

“I'll have dinner ready at six.”

“You don't have to organize around him.”

“I'm organizing around you. Eat first. Then do your stupid thing.”

She went inside before he could answer.

He followed her carrying the empty tray.

## Chapter 12: Bishop Brings a Choir

Bishop Price arrived at seven with eleven men, two singers, and a portable organ nobody had asked him to explain.

Marcus stood beside the stage with the microphone hanging from one hand.

“You booked entertainment?” he asked Tín.

“He said he wanted an audience.”

“This nigga brought witnesses with harmonies.”

Bishop wore a white suit with a purple handkerchief folded above his breast pocket. The gold-tipped cane was gone. In its place, he carried a small black bag. Behind him, his men spread across the street in matching dark jackets while the singers argued quietly over where the extension cord went.

Loretta pointed toward an outlet beneath the salon window.

“You trip anything, you pay for the dryers.”

One singer nodded.

“We professional.”

“Then you'll understand the rate.”

Tín stood at the center of the cleared section of Bell Street. Boots had moved the trucks away, and the market tables had been stacked along the curb. Neighbors watched from doorways, windows, and the folding chairs Bích had decided were too useful to leave empty.

At six, she had served dinner exactly as promised. Tín had eaten two bowls while she explained which shirt he would wear and where she expected him to put his shoes if they came home wet.

Now she sat beside Hương outside the house, holding a cup of tea.

Hòa stood behind them with Mai. He wore his teaching shirt, sleeves rolled neatly above his wrists. His expression suggested he had expected a quieter evening and remained open to being compensated for the difference.

Bishop placed the black bag on a chair.

“You said bring everyone whose opinion matters.”

“They look expensive.”

“Respect costs.”

“The singers too?”

Bishop glanced back.

“They set the mood.”

One singer tapped the microphone.

A loud crack came through the speaker. Everybody flinched except Tín and Hòa.

Marcus took the microphone away from him.

“The mood is feedback. Stop.”

Bishop opened the bag and removed two bundles of cash.

“Twenty thousand,” he said. “You beat me, it's yours. I beat you, the River Queen moves by morning.”

Tín looked at the money.

“No.”

Bishop smiled.

“Afraid?”

“My wife has a kitchen in it. Your twenty thousand has no stove.”

Laughter moved through the chairs.

Bishop's smile tightened.

“Then what do you want?”

“You pay Mateo's hospital bill. You replace everything your men broke. You stop sending people to Bell Street.”

“And if I win?”

Tín took a bundle from his pocket and put it on the chair.

“Fifty thousand.”

Bishop looked at the thick stack.

“Where'd you get that?”

“Rook. He enjoyed the last show.”

Behind Bishop, two of his men looked at each other.

Tín saw it.

“He didn't mention that?”

Bishop closed the bag.

“Let's begin.”

He removed his jacket and handed it to Wade, the man Tín had assigned to carry chairs. Beneath it, his shirt pulled tight across a heavy chest and strong shoulders. He rolled his neck once, unbuttoned the cuffs, and stepped into the open street.

One of the singers began a low sustained note.

Bishop lifted a hand.

The note stopped.

“After,” he said.

Tín took off his own shirt and handed it to Mai.

She looked at the bandage on his arm and the red skin above it.

“Don't make me sew this one.”

“I'll try to remain inside it later.”

She folded it over her arm.

Bishop came at him without another word.

His first punch struck Tín's forearm and drove him half a step sideways. The second came low toward his ribs. Tín caught it on his elbow, turned his hips, and struck Bishop across the mouth.

The man staggered back.

Blood appeared on his lower lip.

He wiped it with his thumb, looked at it, and grinned.

“There he is.”

Tín motioned him forward.

Bishop lowered his head and drove in with his shoulder. Tín moved aside. Bishop struck the folding chair behind him, crushing it against the bumper of his own car.

Vân raised a hand.

“That's one.”

Bishop turned.

Tín kicked him in the side of the knee. His leg bent. Before he could recover, Tín caught his wrist and pulled him across the open space into a short elbow to the jaw.

Bishop went down on one hand.

He swept his leg toward Tín's ankles.

Tín jumped over it and landed behind him.

Bishop rose with a backward elbow. It struck Tín's cheek. His head turned with the blow. Blood filled the edge of his mouth.

The crowd went quiet.

Tín touched his lip with his tongue, then looked at the man behind him.

Bishop's grin returned.

“Old doesn't mean slow,” he said. “But it means something.”

Tín caught him by the back of the shirt and the waistband.

He lifted him over his head.

Bishop's feet kicked once above the streetlights.

Tín turned and placed him on the roof of the white car.

The roof folded beneath his back. Both rear windows burst outward. The trunk popped open and released a spare hat, a jacket, and a packet of parking notices into the street.

Tín stepped back.

“It means I dislike bending.”

One singer whispered, “Jesus.”

The other nodded without taking her eyes off the car.

Bishop rolled onto his side and pushed himself upright. His shirt had split along one seam. He climbed down from the roof, breathing hard.

Wade moved forward.

Bishop held up a hand.

“Stay.”

Wade stayed.

Bishop reached into the open trunk and pulled out a short steel bar.

Boots leaned toward Marcus.

“He's adding a lesson.”

Tín waited.

The bar came down at his head. He caught Bishop's wrist with both hands and turned the blow across his body. The bar struck the pavement, breaking a pale chip from the concrete.

Tín stamped on it.

Bishop tried to pull it free.

Tín struck him in the chest with his palm.

The air left him in a harsh burst. His hands opened. Tín took the bar, bent it around the nearest parking sign, and let the ends point downward like an expensive handle.

Mr. Benítez examined it from his chair.

“That's useful.”

Bishop dropped to one knee.

Tín stood in front of him.

“Are you finished?”

Bishop looked up, sweat running along his temples.

“Not yet.”

He lunged.

Tín stepped into him, put one hand beneath his chin, and drove him backward until his shoulders hit the hood of the car. He held him there with the hood buckling beneath his weight.

“Try a different answer.”

Bishop's hands closed around Tín's wrist.

He pulled.

Tín waited.

The hands loosened.

“Finished.”

Tín released him.

The singers began applauding before Bishop's own men did. Marcus turned the microphone toward them. They caught the moment, found a bright harmony, and filled the street with a triumphant chord that had clearly been rehearsed for the other man.

Bishop looked toward them.

“Really?”

The woman nearest the keyboard spread her hands.

“You paid for a winner.”

The street erupted.

Boots bent double, laughing. Vân hit the side of her chair with a folded program. The furniture-store owner held up three fingers toward Bishop and pointed at the ruined chair, then at the car's broken glass as if considering whether he could reasonably include something he had not sold.

Tín picked up the fifty thousand he had brought and returned it to his pocket. He handed Bishop's twenty thousand to Rosa.

“For Mateo.”

Rosa took it without smiling.

“He'll need more than this.”

Bishop leaned against the dented hood.

“I'll pay.”

“You will,” she said.

A sharp crack came from the roof above the closed pharmacy.

Tín moved before the second one.

He caught Bishop by the collar and threw him behind the car. The bullet struck the hood where the man's head had been. Paint and metal burst into the air.

“Inside!” Tín shouted.

Hòa pulled the two women nearest him through the house doorway. Hương took Bích by the arm. Mai ducked behind the porch rail and crawled toward them with Tín's shirt beneath her.

Boots overturned a table in front of the singers.

Tín jumped onto the hood, then onto the roof of Bishop's car. He crossed from there to the pharmacy awning in one long leap.

The shooter tried to turn toward him.

Tín tore the metal awning free of its brackets and drove it upward.

It struck the roof edge and folded across the shooter's legs. The rifle fired into the sky.

Tín caught the gutter, pulled himself onto the roof, and kicked the weapon away before the man could free it.

The shooter reached for a knife.

Tín drove his fist into the man's face.

The head struck the brick vent behind him. He fell forward and did not move.

Tín knelt and checked him. There was no pulse. He stood.

Below, the street had emptied into doorways and cover. Bishop crouched behind his car with blood running down his chin. He looked up at Tín, then at the bullet hole in the hood.

Tín carried the rifle to the roof edge and broke it across his knee.

He dropped both pieces into the empty street.

Wade stared at them.

“That wasn't ours,” he called.

“I know.”

Tín came down using the awning frame and a drainpipe. He landed beside the car and went to the house.

Mai reached him first, holding out his shirt.

He took it and looked past her at Bích.

His wife nodded. Hương stood beside her with one arm around Loretta. Everyone in the doorway was breathing.

Tín put on the shirt.

Bishop came from behind the car.

“Rook,” he said.

Tín looked at him.

“You have enemies with good timing.”

“He told me you were half dead. Said the burns had finished you.”

“He should ask for a better doctor.”

Bishop looked at the roof.

“That shot was for me.”

Tín nodded.

The man stood silent for several seconds, then pulled a ring from his finger and offered it.

It held a dark red stone in a heavy gold setting.

Tín looked at it.

“I don't want to marry you.”

“It's my word.”

“Then speak it.”

Bishop closed his hand around the ring.

“My people leave Bell Street alone. Rook asks me to send a man, I send him the bill for tonight.”

“And Mateo?”

“Everything.”

Rosa stepped beside them.

“I'll bring numbers.”

Bishop nodded.

The singers emerged from behind the table. One had lost a shoe. Boots found it beside the keyboard and offered it to her with a small bow.

She looked at the broken rifle in the street.

“Do we still get paid?”

Bishop took out his wallet.

“Yes.”

“Hazard pay?”

He looked at Tín.

Tín spread his hands.

“You brought professionals.”

Bishop paid them.

The police sirens came while Marcus helped collect the cables. The market chairs returned to the sidewalk one at a time. Bích brought tea for the people who had stayed inside her house and handed the last cup to her husband.

He sat beside her on the steps.

“Dinner was good,” he said.

She looked at the split in his lip.

“Tomorrow we're having soup.”

He drank carefully.

## Chapter 13: A Night Off Costs Extra

Bích took him to the movies because she had heard a film was somewhere he could sit quietly for two hours.

The manager met them outside with flowers.

She stopped beneath the marquee.

“Who told him?”

Tín looked at the bouquet.

“I don't know this man.”

“Everybody who brings trouble says that.”

The manager hurried down the steps. He was thin, wore a blue jacket, and had combed his hair into a shape that required faith from everyone involved.

“Mr. Tín. Mrs. Lê. My niece saw the bowling.”

Bích closed her eyes.

“Of course she did.”

“Your seats are ready.”

He offered the flowers. Tín accepted them and gave them to his wife.

She took the bouquet, smelled it once, and allowed herself to enjoy that part.

“Thank you.”

The manager opened the doors.

Inside, the concession counter had been cleared. A young woman waited beside two drinks, a large tub of popcorn, and a box of candy wrapped with a red bow.

“Complimentary,” the manager said.

Tín reached for his wallet.

The manager looked wounded.

“Please.”

Bích took her husband's hand away from the pocket.

“Let the man finish.”

The young woman behind the counter leaned forward.

“My brother was at the wedding landing. He said you carried the groom.”

“He was inconveniently heavy,” Tín said.

She laughed and slid the candy closer.

“For the road.”

They entered the auditorium.

At the center of the room, someone had removed two ordinary seats and replaced them with padded dining chairs. A small table stood between them. On it sat a battery candle and a handwritten card: RESERVED FOR THE GRANDMASTER AND HIS QUEEN.

People turned as they came down the aisle.

Applause began near the front.

Bích kept walking.

“Sit quickly,” she told him. “They'll start a parade.”

Tín sat.

The chair creaked under him.

He shifted his weight carefully.

It creaked again.

Bích put the flowers on the table.

“Your throne needs oil.”

He looked toward the manager, who was still standing near the door with both hands folded over his stomach.

“Don't,” Bích said.

“I wasn't going to.”

“Your face was preparing.”

The lights went down.

For twenty minutes, nobody shot at them.

Tín considered this promising.

The film concerned a man who had lost a bag of money and was trying to convince his wife he had merely misplaced their future. Bích watched him lie through two scenes, then leaned toward Tín.

“He should tell her.”

“He would have a shorter film.”

“I'd pay more.”

Onscreen, the man hid beneath a hotel bed while his wife questioned someone in the next room. Tín laughed. His split lip stung. He touched it with his tongue and found Bích watching him.

“You used to come home like this,” she whispered.

He looked back at the screen.

“Less often.”

“You had fewer witnesses.”

He smiled slightly.

She rested her hand on his arm, below the burn.

They watched the next scene without speaking.

When the lights rose for an unexpected break in the projector, the manager came down the aisle carrying another box of candy. Bích intercepted it.

“We have enough.”

“I'm sorry about the delay.”

“The delay is fine. It gives my husband time to chew.”

Tín held up a piece of candy in protest.

The manager bowed and retreated.

Bích watched him go.

“This country has been very strange to you.”

“I think I've been fair in return.”

She laughed, and he turned toward her fully, glad to have caused it.

They had been married forty-five years. He had watched her laugh in borrowed rooms, kitchens too hot to stand in, a crowded airport where one of their suitcases had split open, and the first rented house on Bell Street when Hòa discovered the bathtub had been painted blue instead of cleaned. Each place had offered her something to dislike. She had rarely run short of material.

“Do you remember the man at the airport?” she asked.

“Which one?”

“The one who said we couldn't bring the cooking pot.”

“He thought it was commercial equipment.”

“It was a pot.”

“It had ambitions.”

She shook her head.

“You offered to cook for him.”

“He looked hungry.”

“You offered to cook him.”

Tín considered this.

“My English was developing.”

She hit his good shoulder with the back of her hand.

“Your English was perfect when you wanted the pot.”

“The pot reached the house before the bed,” she said.

Tín looked at her.

“The bed was delayed.”

“You went to buy one.”

“I found something on the way.”

“A piano.”

He remembered it standing halfway down an outside stair, one end against the rail, three exhausted men above it and a woman below announcing that she would not spend another month paying rent on a room occupied by an instrument nobody could move. He had been carrying the address of a used-furniture store in his pocket. Hòa waited at home with the borrowed truck they had arranged for the bed.

“It was in the way,” he said.

“Everything important to you begins in the way.”

He smiled.

The woman had asked whether he knew someone who could help. Tín had looked at the piano, the sagging stair, and the three men sweating against its polished side.

He had told them to step back.

One man refused. Said he had been moving pianos for twenty years and understood what would happen if anybody let go.

Tín had put one hand beneath the lower end and lifted it clear of the stair.

The man stopped explaining.

With the instrument balanced against his shoulder, Tín had carried it down the remaining steps, crossed the little yard, and set it on the sidewalk beside the waiting truck. One of the piano movers followed him, still holding the end of a strap that no longer appeared necessary.

The woman came down last.

She offered him money. He refused because he was late for the furniture store. She offered him the television from her front room. He asked whether it worked.

“That question cost us the whole afternoon,” Bích said.

“We needed a television.”

“We needed somewhere to sleep.”

“The children enjoyed it.”

“Mai sat on the floor watching a man sell knives while her father went looking for you.”

Tín laughed quietly.

“Hòa found me at the furniture store.”

“Holding the television.”

“The woman didn't have a box.”

“And the man at the store said he recognized you from the sidewalk.”

Tín remembered that too. The furniture seller had watched the piano come down. When Tín arrived carrying the television, he offered him a mattress at half price if he would help move a wardrobe away from the office door.

Tín moved it.

Then there was a sofa.

Then somebody asked about a bathtub behind the building.

By the time Hòa found him, six people were standing beside their chosen heavy objects, holding money and explaining why their particular request would take only a minute.

His son looked at the line, took the television, and put it in the borrowed truck.

“My father is closed,” Hòa announced.

The furniture seller tried to argue.

Hòa picked up the mattress with one hand, put it in the truck beside the television, and held out the agreed price with the other.

The seller accepted it very quietly.

Bích watched Tín remember.

“You came home with two mattresses, a television, and a lamp shaped like a fish.”

“The fish was free.”

“I know. You told me four times.”

“Do we still have it?”

“Hương uses it in her sewing room.”

Tín looked pleased.

Bích shook her head and squeezed his hand.

“You see? A good afternoon can end with furniture inside the house and everybody still having dinner.”

The projector started again. The room darkened. Bích settled against her chair, still smiling.

Tín took her hand beneath the little table.

They stayed until the film ended.

Outside, the manager offered a free pass good for a year. Bích accepted it before her husband could ask whether it covered refrigerators. She had heard that joke enough times to recognize the beginning in his face.

They walked toward the night market two blocks away.

The air smelled of fried onions and warm pavement. Cars moved slowly along the curb. A man played saxophone beside a shuttered jewelry store, his open case holding coins and several folded bills. Tín stopped long enough to listen, then put twenty dollars in the case.

The musician saw his face and changed the tune into a bright little flourish.

Bích pulled her husband onward.

“No encores. I want noodles.”

At the corner, a black car rolled past them twice.

Tín saw the same crack in its left tail light on the second pass.

He kept walking.

Bích felt his hand tighten slightly around hers.

“Where?”

“Behind us.”

She looked at the shops ahead.

“Laundromat's open.”

He nodded.

They crossed the street and entered beneath a flickering sign. Inside, washing machines lined both walls. Three people sat near the front reading magazines. An older man folded shirts at a long table. At the rear, a woman carried a basket of towels toward a dryer.

Tín went to the attendant's little booth.

“There's trouble outside,” he said quietly. “Take people to the back.”

The attendant looked through the window, saw the black car stop, and put down his newspaper.

Bích helped the woman with the towels. Together they moved toward the rear door. The others followed when the attendant called them over.

Tín remained beside the folding table.

Two men entered.

The first carried a short metal baton. The second kept one hand beneath his jacket.

“Come outside,” the baton man said.

“I'm waiting for a cycle.”

“Now.”

Tín looked at the rows of machines.

“You seem confused about laundries.”

The baton came down on the table.

It broke a corner from the wood.

Tín caught the man's wrist, pulled him forward, and drove his face into the pile of folded shirts. The second man drew his pistol. Tín lifted the first attacker between them.

The gun fired.

The baton man jerked against his arms.

Tín pushed him toward the shooter and moved to the side as they collided. The pistol came up again. He seized its slide, forced the muzzle toward the floor, and struck the gunman's elbow with his other hand.

The joint bent backward.

The man screamed.

Tín took the weapon and hit him behind the ear with its butt. He fell beside the nearest washer.

Outside, the driver climbed from the car with another gun.

Tín tore a dryer door from its hinges and threw it through the open entrance.

The round door hit the driver across the shoulder and spun him into the car. His gun discharged through the roof. Tín crossed the room, went out after him, and caught him by the back of the neck.

He put the man's head through the car's open window and closed the door against his hips.

The driver made a high, choked sound.

“Stay there.”

Tín took the gun from the ground and returned inside.

The first attacker lay beside the folding table. Blood spread beneath his jacket. Tín knelt, pressed his fingers against the man's neck, and waited.

There was no pulse.

He closed the man's eyes and stood.

At the back door, Bích waited with the attendant and customers. The woman with the towels held her basket against her stomach. One young man had both hands over his ears.

“It's over,” Tín said.

Bích came forward and looked at the body.

Then she looked at her husband.

“You?”

He shook his head.

She touched his sleeve, checking for blood anyway.

The attendant reached for the telephone in his booth. Tín placed both guns on top of a washer, away from everyone else, and went outside to watch the driver until the police arrived.

The saxophone player stood at the corner holding his instrument against his chest. The music had stopped.

Tín raised a hand toward him.

The man nodded but did not begin again.

After the officers took over, Bích and Tín sat on a bench outside the laundromat. The manager had offered them free washing for the rest of the year. Bích accepted the card, then made Tín pay for the broken dryer door from the cash he carried.

“It belonged to him,” she said.

Tín gave the attendant enough for the door and the table. The man tried to refuse half of it. Bích made him take it.

Now she held the flowers from the theater across her lap. One stem had broken.

Tín looked at it.

“I'm sorry about the evening.”

“The film was good.”

“After the film.”

She turned the broken flower between her fingers.

“We came here with four suitcases and your terrible pot.”

“It was your pot.”

“You carried it. Ownership changed.”

He smiled faintly.

“I wanted a house where people came because we invited them,” she said. “We got one. We have friends. Mai has school. Hương has work she chose. Your son comes home tired from arguing with boys who don't want to learn fractions.”

Tín listened.

She looked toward the black car.

“That man doesn't get to put a price on all of it.”

He took her hand.

“He won't.”

“And you don't get to disappear into him until there's nothing left for us.”

He looked at her.

She held his gaze.

“I married you before anyone on this street knew your name. Don't make me take a number.”

He bent and kissed her hand.

“First in line.”

“I don't stand in line.”

“I remember.”

She leaned against him.

The saxophone began again, softly, from the corner.

After a while, Bích stood and took his hand.

“Noodles,” she said.

“It's late.”

“Then they'll have room.”

They walked together to the night market. At the first stall, the cook recognized Tín and opened his mouth.

Bích put the money down before he could offer anything.

“Two bowls. We are ordinary customers for the next twenty minutes.”

The cook looked at the bills, then at her face.

“Yes, ma'am.”

Tín sat on the stool beside her and waited for his supper.

## Chapter 14: The Wrong Man in the Trunk

The ransom note had three spelling mistakes and a recommendation from Hòa to use a cleaner sheet of paper.

Tín read it at the kitchen table while Hương stood beside him with the telephone receiver pressed between her ear and shoulder.

“They say they have him,” she said.

“I can see that.”

At the bottom of the note, beneath the demand that Tín come alone to the old motor court, Hòa had written: Please bring my brown shoes. The left one came off in the car.

Bích looked over her husband's shoulder.

“He was wearing the brown shoes.”

“Apparently one stayed behind.”

Hương held out the receiver.

Tín took it.

“Where is my son?”

The voice at the other end sounded younger than he expected and much less pleased with itself.

“Motor court. Room seven. You come alone.”

“Put him on.”

“He's not near the phone.”

“Bring him nearer.”

A muffled conversation followed. Tín heard someone say, “I'm not opening that again.”

Then the first voice returned.

“Just come.”

The line went dead.

Tín put the receiver down.

Mai stood in the hallway with her schoolbag on one shoulder. She had heard enough to stop pretending she was leaving.

“Dad?”

“I'm going to get him.”

Hương picked up her car keys.

Tín looked at her.

“They said alone.”

“They also spelled alone with two Ls. I'm not trusting their judgment.”

She went to the door.

Bích took Mai's bag and placed it on the table.

“You stay with me.”

Mai opened her mouth.

Bích pointed at the telephone.

“If they call again, I want somebody who can hear the whole sentence without deciding to break the furniture.”

Mai looked at her grandfather, then at her mother.

“Call us.”

Hương nodded.

They drove west in the family sedan. Tín sat beside her holding the ransom note and the spare pair of shoes Hương had taken from the cupboard.

“He corrected their spelling,” Tín said.

“He does that when he's irritated.”

“I know.”

“You do it when you're trying to look patient.”

Tín folded the note.

Hương kept her eyes on the road. Her hands held the steering wheel firmly enough that the knuckles had gone pale.

“He called me from school this morning,” she said. “He was bringing work home. He asked if we needed anything.”

“What did you say?”

“Laundry soap.”

Tín looked down at the shoes in his lap.

“We'll get it.”

The motor court stood beyond the old cannery road, a row of low rooms around a cracked courtyard. Half the doors had boards over their windows. A drained swimming pool occupied the center, its blue paint peeling in long strips. Two cars stood beside room seven. A third sat crookedly near the office with its trunk open.

Hương stopped behind the office wall.

Tín got out.

“Stay here.”

She pointed toward the courtyard.

“Bring him to the car.”

He nodded and walked toward room seven carrying the shoes.

A man came out of the doorway holding a pistol. His shirt was damp down the front. One side of his face had begun swelling.

“I said alone.”

“My daughter-in-law drives better.”

“Where's the money?”

Tín lifted the shoes.

“You asked for these.”

The man looked at them, then back at Tín.

“Do you think this is funny?”

“That depends where my son is.”

The trunk of the nearest car made a soft knocking sound.

The gunman flinched.

Tín turned toward it.

“Hòa?”

“Here.”

His son's voice came clearly through the metal.

“Are you hurt?”

“The upholstery is unpleasant.”

The gunman's pistol trembled.

“Shut up.”

Tín looked back at him.

The trembling stopped because the man's whole body had become still.

“Put it down.”

“Rook said—”

“You should have asked him to come.”

The man fired.

Tín stepped inside the muzzle's line and caught his wrist. The shot broke a window behind him. He pulled the man forward, drove a knee into his stomach, and struck him across the jaw with the pair of shoes.

The gunman fell against the wall.

Tín took the pistol and laid it on the roof of the car.

From room seven, a second man rushed out with a shotgun.

The trunk lid opened.

Hòa sat up inside, holding a stack of exercise books on his knees.

“Behind you,” he said.

Tín turned.

He threw the shoes at the second man's face. The man ducked, bringing the shotgun down. Tín crossed the distance and caught the barrel beneath his arm. He hit the gunman in the chest with his palm and drove him backward through the doorway.

The bedside table broke beneath them.

Her hand rested on the polished wood.

For a moment she said nothing.

Then she turned toward Tín.

“My husband played this when the theater opened.”

“Marcus's teacher told him about it.”

“Bastard.”

“Which one?” Duke asked from the doorway.

Ruth turned her head.

“I'm capable of including several.”

Duke stepped out of the way.

She ran her hand along the console once more and replaced the blanket.

“Keep it here for tomorrow. My technician can fit enough of it together for a performance.”

Tín shook his head.

“The boat's damaged.”

“So is everyone worth inviting. We'll use the pavilion if we have to.”

“You'll need carpenters.”

“We have friends,” Tín said.

“I'll send professionals too.”

She took a key from the ring at her waist and handed it to Bích.

“Pier kitchen. Use it.”

Bích accepted the key.

“How much?”

“Feed me.”

“That can become expensive.”

Ruth smiled.

“I'm seventy-four. I know what I'm risking.”

Boots returned with the correct speaker part after making the shop owner listen to Marcus's distress through the telephone. The owner came with him, carrying a second part and a bill he wrote at a discount so dramatic Boots asked whether something was wrong with it.

“The festival flyer has the wrong pier,” she said.

“Can you change it?”

“Mr. Chen will open tonight.”

“I'll pay.”

“I know.”

She looked up at him.

“I want to do the work.”

He nodded.

“Then do it well.”

She tied the clean cloth and put the bowl aside.

Hòa came down the pier carrying a box of freshly printed directions. He had already been to the shop with Hương's car. Mai saw the box and stood so quickly her chair scraped the boards.

“Dad.”

“He said you should approve them.”

She opened the box.

Tín watched her compare the new directions with the old flyer. Hòa stood beside her, hands in his pockets, saying nothing until she asked about the map.

On the black tug, Duke had found a can of white paint.

He covered the word CROWN on its side and stood back to examine the result.

Rosa leaned over the rail.

“You can't just leave a blank.”

“I'm considering names.”

“How about Student?”

Duke pointed the brush at her.

“You're making me want to improve out of spite.”

Tín called across from the pier.

“That's how most good work begins.”

Rosa stared.

Then she laughed hard enough to sit on the rail.

“Mateo's going to hate that.”

“Then he's healing.”

Tín leaned back in his chair and watched the lights come on beneath the pavilion roof.

For the first time since the barge left its berth, he could hear music without waiting for a gunshot inside it.

## Chapter 18: The Band Refuses to Stop

The rehearsal acquired an audience because Marcus tested the speakers with the doors open.

By nine, the pier held enough people for Bích to open the second tray of food.

By nine-thirty, Ruth Hollis had sent someone home for better shoes.

“It's rehearsal,” Mai told Marcus.

He looked at the people dancing beneath the pavilion lights.

“They're practicing attendance.”

“You told the radio station.”

“Public service.”

“You said there was a surprise performance.”

“Look at them. Surprised.”

Tín stood beside the organ console while an elderly technician named Ellis worked beneath it with a lamp clipped to his collar. A small section of the recovered pipes had been set on a temporary frame behind the stage. The rest would take weeks to restore properly. Ellis had decided that a few good notes were preferable to an entire instrument remaining silent out of pride.

He pressed a key.

A thin, mournful sound came from one pipe.

Duke looked toward it from the tug.

“That one heard about the wedding.”

Ellis kept his hand on the key and glared at him.

Tín leaned over the console, pressed two other keys, and listened. He walked behind the frame, touched one loose fitting, and moved it a fraction.

The note steadied.

Ellis looked up.

“You work on these?”

“No.”

“Then what made you touch that?”

Tín pressed the fitting again. The pitch bent slightly, then returned when he let go.

Ellis stared at his hand.

“Do that once more.”

He did.

The technician climbed out from beneath the console and handed him a small wrench.

“Stay.”

Bích passed carrying a tray.

“Good word,” she told Ellis.

They worked for twenty minutes while the band played around them. Tín held one pipe upright, then two, then an entire section of the frame while Ellis secured its base. The old technician stopped commenting after the first few impossible adjustments and began giving instructions as if he had spent his whole life waiting for someone to hold things properly.

When he pressed the keys again, a broad warm chord rose beneath the pavilion roof.

The band stopped.

Ruth put down her glass.

Ellis played four slow chords, then a little ascending run. The sound was smaller than the theater organ had once been, rough at the edges, and unmistakably alive.

Ruth sat in the nearest chair.

Tín stepped away from the pipes.

“You should play,” he told her.

She shook her head.

“My husband played.”

“You listened.”

“I complained. He practiced at unreasonable hours.”

Ellis moved aside from the bench.

Ruth looked at the keys for a long moment, then went to them. She sat, placed both hands carefully, and played a simple melody with two fingers.

The band found it.

Bass joined first. Then the drummer brushed the edge of a cymbal. A saxophone followed the melody a step behind her until she smiled and let it carry the difficult parts.

People gathered beneath the lights.

Tín found Bích beside the food table. He held out his hand.

She looked at the serving spoon in hers.

“I'm working.”

Hương took the spoon.

“You have staff.”

Bích looked toward her daughter-in-law, then at her husband's hand.

She took it.

They went to the open space beside the stage. Tín placed one hand at her back. She settled her other hand against his shoulder, careful of the fresh cut.

“No lifting,” she said.

“I hadn't begun.”

“I know your ideas.”

He drew her closer and moved with the music.

Around them, people made room. Marcus raised the microphone, thought better of it when Mai looked at him, and lowered it again.

Tín watched his wife's face beneath the warm bulbs. She had taken off the apron. A loose strand of hair had escaped near her ear. He brushed it back with his thumb.

“Better than the cinema?” he asked.

“The chair was quieter.”

He laughed.

She leaned toward him.

“Don't turn this into a performance.”

“We have witnesses.”

“They can learn to wait.”

He turned her once, slowly, and brought her back against him.

The applause began anyway.

Bích looked past his shoulder at the crowd.

“These people are impossible.”

“I've heard that about their host.”

At the edge of the dance floor, Bishop Price arrived carrying a large square box. Wade followed with two smaller ones and the careful posture of a man who had learned to keep his hands visible on Bell Street.

Bishop wore a dark jacket over a clean shirt. His lower lip remained swollen from the night before. He waited until the song ended, then approached Tín.

“For the festival.”

Tín looked at the box.

“If it sings, return it.”

Bishop opened it.

Inside lay a gold-colored crown lined with red fabric. It was far too ornate for any sensible head. Little red stones ran around its lower edge. At the front, a small plate carried the words GRANDMASTER TÍN.

Boots came closer.

“Oh, this is a terrible idea.”

Bích folded her arms.

Tín lifted the crown.

It was heavier than the paper one.

“Real?” he asked.

“Gold plate,” Bishop said. “The good kind.”

“You bought me imitation royalty.”

“Real royalty costs more than twenty-four hours.”

Tín put it on.

The crowd cheered.

Bích covered her face with one hand.

Boots pointed at Bishop.

“You apologized by making him worse.”

Bishop almost smiled.

Wade opened the smaller boxes. Inside were new microphone stands and several lengths of sound cable.

Marcus took one stand as if accepting a newborn.

“Now that's respect.”

Tín removed the crown and gave it to Mai.

“Put it somewhere safe.”

“A locked room?”

“Somewhere accessible.”

She shook her head, smiling, and carried it toward the office.

Bishop lowered his voice.

“Rook called me.”

“You should change numbers.”

“He offered two hundred thousand to bring you to the eastern terminal.”

“When?”

“Tonight.”

Tín looked toward the river. The bridge crane showed as a dark shape beyond the lights. Work lamps glowed around the new span. Boats moved through the lower passage in a slow procession.

“What did you tell him?”

“That I'd need half first.”

Tín looked back at him.

Bishop held out an envelope.

“He paid.”

Boots stared at it.

“This man is funding the whole neighborhood.”

Bishop kept his eyes on Tín.

“He thinks I'm bringing you through the service gate at eleven.”

“What does he have there?”

“A warehouse. A launch. Men who still believe him.”

“How many?”

“Enough that I asked for the money first.”

Tín took the envelope and weighed it in his hand.

“Keep it.”

Bishop hesitated.

“You sure?”

“You'll need it if you betray me.”

The man swallowed, then put the envelope away.

“I won't.”

Tín looked toward Bích. She stood beside Ruth, listening as the older woman described the first night at the theater. Hương had brought them both food. Mai came from the office carrying a small stack of fresh flyers and the crown balanced on top.

He went to his wife.

She looked at his face and put down her plate.

“How long?”

“I don't know.”

“Then say that when you leave. Don't say ten minutes.”

He nodded.

She took his hand.

“Do you need someone with you?”

“Duke. Bishop.”

“Take Boots too. He needs to stop advising the musicians.”

Tín smiled.

“Stay here with Hòa.”

“He already said that. You both think repetition improves instructions.”

She stood and straightened his collar around the fresh bandage.

“Come back while there's still food.”

He kissed her cheek.

As he crossed the pier, the band struck up another song. Marcus saw him heading toward the tug and raised the microphone.

“The Grandmaster has a brief engagement. Everybody keep dancing.”

Tín looked back.

“Brief?”

Marcus lowered the microphone.

“Marketing.”

On Mercy's Cousin, Duke checked the engine while Rosa stood beside him with her arms folded. She had decided he could be trusted with a short journey if someone experienced remained close enough to insult him through the radio.

“Slow at the turn,” she said.

“I remember.”

“You remembered the opposite last time.”

“That was practice.”

She handed him a dry jacket.

He looked at it, then at her.

“For me?”

“You keep getting wet.”

He put it on without a joke.

Tín boarded with Boots and Bishop. Wade remained on the pier beside Hòa, apparently relieved to have been assigned a place where carrying chairs might once again become his greatest problem.

The tug moved away from Hollis Pier.

Behind them, the pavilion lights reflected in long broken lines across the water. The band kept playing until the distance folded its sound into the engine's steady beat.

Tín stood at the bow with his good hand around the rail.

Ahead, the eastern terminal waited beneath three white lamps.

## Chapter 19: Rook Cuts the Lights

Bishop tied Tín's wrists with a silk scarf so loosely that Tín had to keep his hands together to stop it falling off.

“Convincing,” Duke said.

“I'm working with difficult material.”

Boots looked at the scarf.

“That's yours?”

“Yes.”

“You want it back?”

Bishop glanced at Tín.

“Preferably.”

Mercy's Cousin came alongside the terminal's service landing. Two men waited above the ladder with guns held across their chests. Neither moved to help with the line.

Duke brought the tug close enough that its rubber fender touched the concrete.

“Somebody catch this,” he called.

One guard looked toward the other.

Boots threw the line at his feet.

“We can't intimidate you and tie up at the same time.”

The guard caught it.

Tín climbed the ladder behind Bishop, holding the scarf in place with his thumbs. Boots followed carrying the black bag Bishop had used for his stake on Bell Street. It now contained a wrench, two flashlights, and a sandwich Bích had insisted somebody take.

Duke remained at the tug's controls.

At the top of the ladder, the nearest guard pointed toward Tín's wrists.

“That's all you used?”

Bishop looked at him.

“You want to improve it?”

The guard stepped aside.

They entered the warehouse through a wide service door. Inside, floodlights hung above rows of freight pallets. A central aisle led to a raised loading platform at the far end. Rook stood there beside a black car with its trunk open.

He wore a dark suit now. The cream one had apparently belonged to a more optimistic part of the week.

Nail stood beside him with fresh clothes and wet hair. His wrist brace had been replaced by a tight bandage. A dark bruise spread above the silver plate along his jaw.

Tín looked at him.

“You found a towel.”

Nail's hands closed.

Rook stepped forward.

“Bishop.”

“Calvin.”

“You brought him.”

“He was available.”

Rook examined the scarf.

“Untie him.”

Tín separated his hands.

The scarf fell.

Boots caught it before it reached the floor and handed it back to Bishop.

“Still clean.”

Rook looked at the three of them, then toward the guards at the door.

The guards shifted their weapons.

“You took my money,” Rook told Bishop.

“You offered it.”

“To bring him here.”

Bishop spread his hands toward Tín.

“Here he is.”

For a moment Rook's expression became so empty that Tín almost laughed.

Then the man recovered.

“Everyone has become clever this week.”

“It started with the refrigerator,” Boots said.

Rook ignored him.

Tín walked down the central aisle.

“Your men went after my son.”

“Your son hurt two of them.”

“You should have sent them to school first.”

“I gave you chances.”

“You gave me a wheelchair. Arturo loves it.”

Nail moved off the platform.

Tín stopped beside a freight pallet.

“You want another turn?” he asked.

Nail came at him.

He swung a heavy length of cable with a steel hook at its end. Tín stepped behind the pallet. The hook tore through the wooden slats and caught in the crate beneath them. Nail pulled. The crate slid across the floor toward him.

Tín kicked it forward.

The crate struck Nail across the thighs. He stumbled. Tín jumped onto it, drove his heel into the man's shoulder, and sent him backward against a steel upright.

The upright rang like a bell.

The guards at the door raised their guns.

Boots threw the black bag at the nearest man's face. The wrench hit through the canvas. Bishop caught the second guard's rifle with both hands and drove him against the door frame.

The first shot went into the roof.

Dust fell over the aisle.

Tín pulled Nail away from the upright and hit him across the jaw. The silver plate cut into his own knuckles. Blood slicked his hand.

Nail drove his knee into Tín's ribs.

Tín grunted, took the next blow on his elbow, and shoved Nail through the side of an empty packing crate. Wood broke around his shoulders. He rolled clear and came up holding a splintered plank.

Rook stepped down from the platform.

He moved fast.

Tín saw the dark shape in the polished side of the car and turned as Rook's cane struck his forearm. The cane had a narrow steel core. It bent slightly against the impact and sent pain up Tín's burned arm.

Rook reversed it toward his throat.

Tín caught the shaft beneath his chin and pulled Rook forward into a short punch to the mouth.

The man's head snapped back.

He stayed on his feet.

Blood showed against his teeth when he smiled.

“There,” Rook said. “Now we can speak honestly.”

Tín hit him again.

Rook went over the hood of the car and landed against the windshield. Glass cracked behind his shoulders.

“You speak slowly,” Tín said.

Nail struck him from behind with the plank.

The wood broke across Tín's back.

He staggered one step, turned, and caught Nail's throat with his bleeding hand. He drove him backward across the aisle until his heels struck a low cart. Nail fell over it, pulling Tín with him.

They hit the floor together.

Nail wrapped his good arm around Tín's neck.

Tín planted one palm beside the man's head and rose, lifting Nail with him. The enforcer's feet dragged, then left the concrete. Tín turned and slammed him onto the cart.

All four wheels burst from their mounts.

Nail let go.

Tín stepped away, breathing hard.

At the door, Boots had pinned the first guard beneath a fallen sign. Bishop stood over the second with the rifle in his hands. He had broken its stock during the fight and looked personally dissatisfied with the manufacturer's decision.

“You good?” Tín called.

Boots lifted a thumb.

Behind him, a man appeared on the overhead catwalk.

Tín shouted.

The shot struck Boots high in the arm.

He fell against the door frame.

Bishop fired upward with the damaged rifle. The gunman ducked behind the rail.

Tín seized the fallen cart and threw it at the catwalk.

It hit beneath the gunman, tore two supports from the wall, and folded the walkway downward.

The man fell through the broken rail.

He struck the concrete beside the pallets and did not get up.

Tín ran to Boots.

Blood darkened the sleeve of his work shirt. He was conscious, teeth clenched, one hand gripping the wound.

“Loretta,” he said.

“We'll get you to her.”

“She's gonna make this about the shirt.”

Tín pressed a clean cloth from the black bag against the wound. The sandwich rolled out beside them.

Boots looked at it.

“Don't leave that.”

“I won't.”

At the far end of the warehouse, Rook reached a control box beside the platform.

The floodlights went out.

Darkness swallowed the aisle.

Tín heard an engine start behind the platform, then the clatter of a rolling door opening onto the water.

“He's leaving,” Bishop said.

“Take Boots.”

Bishop put an arm around the wounded man's waist and lifted him. Boots swore once, then found his feet.

Tín picked up the sandwich and shoved it back into the bag.

The first fire appeared near the pallets at the rear.

Light flickered over the car, showing Rook climbing through the open door onto a launch. Nail staggered after him with one arm held against his side.

Tín ran toward them.

A row of shelving came down across the aisle.

He caught its upper beam on his shoulder. Boxes broke against the floor around his legs. The far end struck the car and crushed its roof. Fire climbed along the spilled packing material beneath it.

Rook's launch pulled away from the dock.

Tín looked through the open rear door.

For a moment he and Rook faced each other across the widening water.

Then Tín turned back toward the warehouse entrance.

He lifted the shelving higher, stepped beneath it, and shoved it into the empty aisle beside the car. The steel struck concrete with a roar that drowned the launch's engine.

He ran after his friends.

At the service landing, Duke had brought the tug as close as its fenders allowed. Bishop helped Boots down the ladder while the boat rocked beneath them. The wounded man's boots struck the deck. Duke caught his belt and pulled him inside.

Tín climbed down last.

Behind them, fire brightened the warehouse windows.

“Hospital,” Tín said.

Duke turned the tug toward the patrol lights farther up the channel.

Bishop knelt beside Boots and kept pressure on the wound. Tín took the radio from its hook and called Vega. His hand left a dark print across the microphone.

She answered at once.

“Where are you?”

He told her.

“Stay in the channel. A patrol boat's coming.”

“Rook went east.”

“We'll look.”

Boots opened one eye.

“Tell Loretta I was useful.”

Tín looked down at him.

“You were.”

“Lead with that.”

The patrol boat reached them before the bend. Its crew took Boots aboard and brought the other wounded men from the service landing. Bishop went with Boots, still holding the cloth against his arm. Tín watched until the boat turned toward the hospital dock.

Then he looked back at the burning terminal.

The warehouse roof had begun glowing along its edge. Emergency lights moved on the road behind it. Rook's launch was gone.

Duke stood beside the wheel with both hands resting on it.

“Back to Hollis?”

Tín nodded.

The radio crackled before Duke could turn.

Rosa's voice came through, sharp and breathless.

“Tín. The pier's on fire.”

## Chapter 20: Nobody Gets to Sink My Wife

Fire climbed the yellow warehouse at Hollis Pier in three separate places.

The pavilion lights were still on.

Tín could see people beneath them, moving toward the River Queen while smoke rolled across the roof. Hòa stood at the gangway helping an older man across. Hương held a wet cloth over a child's mouth. Marcus had the microphone and was shouting instructions that sounded almost like promotion until the words reached Tín through the engine noise.

“Keep moving. Leave the boxes. If it's not a person, stop carrying it.”

Duke brought the tug toward the barge's outer side.

“How close?”

“Closer.”

The fenders touched.

Tín jumped aboard.

He landed beside a stack of empty food trays and went through the ballroom. Smoke hung beneath the repaired skylight. People sat against the far wall, coughing. Two men helped a woman with a burned hand. Ruth stood beside the organ console, refusing to move until Ellis left it alone.

“It survived one fire,” she told him. “It can manage another. Walk.”

Ellis looked at Tín.

Tín pointed toward the outer deck.

The technician walked.

Bích came through the galley door carrying a large cooking pot.

Tín stopped in front of her.

She stopped too.

They looked at each other.

“People first,” he said.

“Everyone from the kitchen is out.”

“You brought soup.”

“They'll be hungry.”

“Stay where the air is clear.”

She went, carrying the ladle.

Tín crossed to the gangway.

“Rosa's checking the far end. Mai's with her.”

Tín looked toward the pavilion.

His granddaughter emerged through the smoke carrying a little girl against her hip. Rosa followed with the child's father, whose leg was bleeding through his trousers. They moved quickly until a section of the overhead awning dropped in front of them.

Mai stopped short.

Tín ran down the gangway.

He caught the burning beam before it reached her. Heat struck his face. The sleeve on his left arm began smoking. He lifted the beam above his head and turned it toward the water.

Mai passed beneath his arms with the child.

Rosa dragged the father after her.

Tín threw the beam over the rail.

It hit the river and vanished beneath a cloud of steam.

He went toward the pavilion.

“Nobody back there,” Rosa called.

He looked through the smoke anyway, searching the spaces beneath the tables and along the far wall. A white shape moved behind the organ pipes.

Tín crossed the open boards.

The saxophone player from the night market crouched beneath the temporary pipe frame. His jacket had caught on a protruding bolt. He was pulling at it with both hands, unable to reach the fastening behind his shoulder.

The man staggered up with the instrument case against his chest.

“I came for the music,” he said.

“It's on the boat.”

Tín guided him toward the gangway.

Behind them, a sharp burst came from the warehouse. Windows shattered along the upper wall. Burning fragments fell onto the pavilion roof.

He felt the movement run toward the gangway.

“Go!”

The saxophone player ran.

“Clear the gangway,” he called.

“Almost.”

The weight shifted again as the pavilion roof came down onto the supported beam.

“Move them!”

Hòa called down.

“Clear.”

He pulled again.

“Tín. Stay west of the bridge. Rook's men have taken the crane.”

## Chapter 21: Three Boats and a Bad Idea

Duke cut the tug's speed when he heard the warning about the bridge.

The River Queen continued moving behind him, its heavy hull carrying its own opinion about stopping.

Rosa brought her boat against the barge's rear quarter and pushed it back toward the center of the channel. The patrol boat stayed on the opposite side, transferring injured people one at a time while its crew watched the dark water ahead.

“Can we turn?”

“Hold straight. I need room to get the stern around.”

Mr. Benítez sent a pair of soft shoes with a note promising that the soles would survive ordinary walking. Vân sent groceries because, as she explained to the nurse, the refrigerator should not become lonely during a family emergency.

One envelope held five hundred dollars and a card from the father whose daughter Mai had carried through the fire.

Tín read it twice.

He asked Hương to return the money and keep the card.

“He'll argue,” she said.

“Tell him to spend it on her.”

Hương nodded and put the envelope in her bag.

Ruth arrived with a small group from the Orpheum. Ellis carried a photograph of the organ before it had been dismantled. The saxophone player came with them, his instrument case held in both hands.

“We're playing tonight,” Ruth announced.

Tín looked at Bích.

His wife had been expecting it.

“At the eastern basin,” she said. “The old excursion landing. There's room beside the barge.”

“Who decided?”

Ruth, Bích, Hương, and Loretta looked at him.

He lifted one bandaged hand.

“A strong committee.”

The doctor returned while they were discussing food. She listened for a moment, then looked at the patient.

“You're planning a party.”

“They are.”

“Good. You can sit at it.”

Bích nodded.

“We've already discussed the chair.”

The doctor gave them her instructions, checked the fresh dressings, and told Tín she would see him again before he left. He listened this time. Bích noticed and did not make a joke of it.

When the room finally emptied, Hòa remained beside the window.

He held the board from the bridge crew beneath one arm. FORTY TONNES faced the wall, as if he had decided the number had enjoyed enough public attention for one morning.

Tín looked at his son's hands.

There was a dark bruise across one palm.

“The bollard?” he asked.

Hòa glanced down.

“It moved.”

“I saw.”

His son said nothing.

Tín leaned back against the pillow.

“You've been keeping busy.”

“I have a family.”

The answer settled between them without challenge.

Hòa put the board beside the gift cart, straightened the blanket over his father's feet, and moved the water cup within reach.

“Sleep,” he said.

Tín looked at him for a long moment.

Then he closed his eyes.

In the corridor, someone began arguing with Marcus about whether the hospital permitted a rehearsal. Hương's voice joined the discussion. A moment later the argument ended abruptly.

Bích sat beside the bed and took Tín's uninjured fingers in hers.

He slept while she held them.

## Chapter 25: Rook Signs the Receipt

Iris brought the check in a white envelope and placed it on the table as if it might bite her.

Tín sat opposite her in a quiet room off the hospital corridor. Bích had permitted the meeting because the doctor had not yet permitted him to leave, and therefore someone else would have to do any climbing involved.

Sable stood beside the window. Vega waited near the door. Rook's lawyer occupied the remaining chair and had arranged his briefcase on his knees with the care of a man hoping it would count as a barrier.

“An initial payment,” the lawyer said.

Tín looked at the envelope.

“You can say the number.”

“Two million dollars.”

From the corridor, Boots said, “Goddamn.”

Loretta's voice followed.

“Stop listening at the door.”

“He said the number loudly.”

Vega stepped outside long enough to move them farther down the hall. When she returned, the lawyer had begun adjusting his tie.

Tín opened the envelope.

The check lay inside beside a short letter listing the damaged properties and medical expenses Rook had agreed to address. Tín read it once, then passed it to Sable.

She read it more slowly.

“The tug?” she asked.

The lawyer opened his briefcase.

“Separate document.”

“Duke will be thrilled you found another page.”

Tín looked toward Iris.

Her father had remained at the hospital under guard through the morning. His injuries had been treated. His accounts and businesses were being pulled into questions that neither this envelope nor the money inside it would settle. Iris had spent several hours telling Vega which deliveries she had arranged and which orders had come directly from him. She looked tired enough to be angry with furniture.

“He wants to see you,” she said.

“I saw plenty of him last night.”

“He said five minutes.”

Tín looked at Bích, who had come to stand in the doorway.

She folded her arms.

“He said ten minutes at the cinema.”

“That was me,” Tín said.

“I'm establishing a standard.”

Vega considered them, then nodded toward the corridor.

“Five. With us present.”

They took him down the hall in a wheelchair because the nurse had decided walking remained a privilege he had abused. Tín looked at the chair's plain gray frame and thought of Arturo's red machine back on Bell Street.

“Calvin bought a nicer one.”

Bích pushed him a little faster.

“Then next time ask him before you spend the night fighting.”

Rook sat upright in a private room with one hand cuffed to the bed rail. A dressing covered the cut above his eyebrow. His lip had swollen. Someone had brought him a clean shirt, but he wore it open at the collar because the bruising around his throat made anything else uncomfortable.

He looked at the wheelchair.

For a moment, he almost smiled.

Tín saw it.

“Don't become hopeful.”

The smile disappeared.

Bích stopped the chair beside the bed. Vega remained at the foot, and the lawyer took a position near the wall where he could see both men without being within either one's reach.

Rook looked toward the envelope in Tín's hand.

“You received it.”

“Yes.”

“My daughter will handle the rest.”

Iris stood at the door.

“The legitimate accounts,” she said. “You can explain the others.”

Rook looked away from her.

Tín took the check from the envelope and examined it again.

“You signed this sitting down.”

“Naturally.”

“Much neater than upside down.”

The lawyer stared at the wall.

Rook closed his eyes briefly.

“Must you?”

“I've paid for entertainment. I like to receive it.”

Bích touched the back of his chair.

“Five minutes.”

Tín put the check away.

Rook opened his eyes.

“You think you've won the waterfront.”

“I won a boat.”

“You took a route worth more than the boat.”

“Then you should have offered Duke a better bowling ball.”

“There will be other men.”

Tín looked at him without smiling.

“Send them your photographs from last night.”

Rook's jaw worked.

Vega shifted slightly at the foot of the bed.

Tín continued before anyone needed to speak.

“You have businesses away from Bell Street. Keep your people there. Duke runs the tug. My wife runs the kitchen. The people whose homes you tried to burn get to decide when they're finished being angry.”

Rook glanced toward Bích.

“Mrs. Lê—”

“No,” she said.

He stopped.

“You can pay for what you broke. You don't get a conversation with me because the check is large.”

She turned the wheelchair toward the door.

Tín let her.

As they passed Iris, the young woman stepped aside. She watched Bích push her husband down the corridor, then followed them to the quiet room.

The lawyer remained with Rook.

Sable sat on the window ledge with the tug document in her hands.

“Mercy's Cousin,” she read. “They've put the new name on it.”

Tín looked at her.

“Duke works quickly when encouraged.”

“Rosa encourages him loudly.”

Iris came through the door and closed it behind her.

For a moment she stood with both hands at her sides.

“I'm selling the eastern office,” she said.

Vega looked up.

“You'll tell us before moving anything.”

“I know. I mean after.”

She looked toward the window, where the harbor cranes stood against the pale morning sky.

“I don't want to sit behind his desk and explain why everything smells like smoke.”

Sable folded the tug paper.

“There are desks without his name on them.”

Iris gave her a tired look.

“You found one quickly.”

“I spent years learning where the bad ones were.”

Tín watched them without interrupting.

Iris turned back to him.

“When the captain fired at the lighter, you pulled me down.”

“You were in front of me.”

“You knew who I was.”

“You introduced yourself very formally.”

She smiled despite herself.

Then the smile went.

“Thank you.”

He nodded.

“Tell Vega everything you saw.”

“I am.”

She left with the detective a few minutes later, carrying none of her father's papers. Tín watched the door close and sat back in the wheelchair.

Bích took the envelope from him and put it in her bag.

“You don't need to carry this around.”

“It's my first two-million-dollar receipt.”

“Then we'll keep it flat.”

Sable stood.

“There's something else.”

She placed a smaller envelope on the table.

Tín looked at it.

“You've been busy.”

“My wages. The part Rook kept promising and moving into another month.”

“How much?”

She told him.

He nodded toward the envelope.

“Keep it.”

“I took it from the chest before I gave you the rest.”

Bích looked at her directly.

Sable held the look.

“I'm telling you now because I want to work somewhere I don't have to keep doing that.”

Tín considered her. She had brought him to Rook's ring, shown him the passage beneath the pier, called the police before the cannery raid, and kept finding the one door that a room full of men had forgotten to watch. She had also worked for Rook while his crates passed beneath Duke's dance floor.

He pushed the envelope back across the table.

“We discuss pay before the month begins.”

Sable looked at Bích.

“With you?”

“With me,” Bích said.

Sable let out a breath.

“Good.”

Bích stood and moved behind Tín's chair.

“Now he needs to rest.”

“I slept.”

“You slept once. Don't treat it as a completed project.”

She wheeled him back toward his room.

In the corridor, Bishop stood beside Boots with a large paper bag. Boots had been allowed into a chair and was eating the rescued sandwich while Loretta watched every bite as if personally responsible for its medical suitability.

Bishop held out the bag.

“Fresh clothes.”

Tín looked inside.

A plain dark shirt, loose trousers, and soft shoes lay folded together. Beneath them was a gold-colored sash.

Tín lifted one corner.

Bích took it from him.

“No.”

Bishop looked disappointed.

“It came with the crown.”

“Then return half the kingdom.”

Boots began laughing and immediately regretted moving his wounded arm.

Loretta steadied his cup.

“Laugh on the other side.”

Tín looked around at them: the nightclub owner dressed like a pilot, the street boss holding a rejected royal sash, his oldest Bell Street friend guarding a sandwich, and his wife directing traffic through a hospital corridor with an envelope worth two million dollars in her bag.

He smiled.

Bích saw it.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That's never true with you.”

He took her hand where it rested on the chair handle.

“We should buy the good refrigerator next time.”

She bent until her mouth was near his ear.

“There is no next time.”

Then she kissed his cheek and pushed him toward the clean bed waiting at the end of the hall.

## Chapter 26: Nobody Bought Enough Chairs

The festival opened without him.

Tín learned this from a radio in the hospital hallway, where Marcus was giving an interview with the confidence of a man whose entire business plan had survived being set on fire.

“We have food, music, the recovered Orpheum organ, and a special appearance by the Grandmaster himself.”

The interviewer asked when Tín would arrive.

“When he's finished being medically admired.”

Tín looked at Bích.

She kept folding his clean clothes.

“I told you people would come.”

“He's charging admission to my stitches.”

“No admission. We agreed.”

From the radio, Marcus added that photographs would be available at a reasonable price.

Bích paused.

“I'll discuss reasonable with him later.”

They left in Hương's sedan after the doctor checked the dressings one last time. Tín sat in the back beside his wife, wearing the loose dark shirt Bishop had brought. His soft shoes looked almost painfully ordinary beneath the new trousers. He appreciated them every time he moved his feet.

Hòa drove. Hương sat beside him with a basket of food on her knees, because somehow leaving the hospital for a food festival still required carrying something to eat.

On Bell Street, people came out as the car passed.

Vân waved a kitchen towel from her doorway. Mr. Benítez sounded a little bicycle bell he had attached to the arm of his red chair. The bus driver from Monday leaned out of his window and held up Tín's folded transit pass, which had been found beneath a chair after the market and returned to him for safekeeping.

Hòa stopped long enough to take it.

The driver looked into the back seat.

“Still covers refrigerators.”

Tín lifted his bandaged hand.

“I'm between appliances.”

Bích leaned across him.

“He's retired from delivery.”

The driver nodded solemnly.

“I'll tell the city.”

They continued toward the eastern basin.

The old excursion landing had become a small fairground. Banners hung between lamp posts. Food stalls lined the broad quay, leaving a clear path to the River Queen. The barge's repaired windows reflected the afternoon light, and a temporary cover lay over the broken skylight. Mercy's Cousin rested beside it with Duke's uneven lettering now outlined in gold.

Someone had tied a tiny family tree beneath the tug's new name.

Rosa stood on the deck staring at it.

“You're making the joke official.”

Duke came from the wheelhouse with two cups.

“The paperwork agreed.”

“The paperwork needs supervision.”

He offered her a cup.

She took it.

When Tín's car stopped, Marcus called his name through the loudspeaker.

The crowd turned.

Tín closed his eyes for a moment.

Bích touched his knee.

“You can still get out.”

“I'm considering the bus.”

Hòa opened the door.

Applause rose along the quay as Tín stood. People moved aside to make a broad path toward the boat. Several tried to shake his hand, saw the bandages, and changed the gesture into a careful touch on his shoulder or a little bow from the waist.

Tín returned each greeting as well as his ribs allowed.

A boy of about eight stood near the gangway holding a toy crane.

He looked up at the old man.

“Mine broke too.”

Tín stopped.

One wheel had come off the toy's base. The boy held it in his other hand.

Tín looked toward Hòa.

His son took the wheel, crouched beside the boy, and pressed it back onto the axle. He rolled the crane across his palm, tested the wheel once, and handed it back.

The boy stared at him.

“You can do it too?”

Hòa smiled.

“Small jobs.”

Tín watched his son's hand close around the little toy, then open again. He said nothing as they continued aboard.

Inside, the ballroom looked better than it had when Duke owned it.

Hương had hung clean fabric along the damaged wall. The new glass was clear. Tables stood with enough space between them for people to move without climbing over a stranger's knees. Flowers from the wedding had been mixed with fresh ones in jars along the bar. Near the stage, the organ console shone beneath its polished cover while Ellis made an adjustment to the little pipe frame behind it.

Ruth met Tín at the door.

“You look less alarming.”

“They threw away my shirt.”

“Good hospital.”

She took his arm and led him to a broad chair near the stage. A cushion had been placed on it, and a small table held water, tea, and a bowl of fruit.

Above the chair hung the board from the bridge crew.

FORTY TONNES.

Tín looked up at it.

“I said refrigerator.”

Mai stood beside the chair with her camera.

“We considered the traffic.”

He sat.

Marcus approached carrying the gold-plated crown.

Bích stepped into his path.

“One photograph.”

Marcus looked toward Mai.

She lifted one finger.

He placed the crown on Tín's head and stepped away.

The flash went off.

Bích removed the crown.

“Finished.”

The crowd laughed. Marcus opened his mouth, saw her expression, and decided the microphone needed adjusting somewhere else.

People came to greet Tín in small groups. The printer brought a stack of good paper for Mai and shook Tín's uninjured fingers carefully. The theater manager delivered the annual pass in a little frame. The furniture-store owner counted the chairs in the ballroom and looked almost tearful at how many people were sitting in them correctly.

“I sold eight sets this morning,” he told Tín.

“Then you'll have to buy food.”

“Already did.”

He held up three tickets for the stalls outside.

“My wife thinks I'm investigating the competition.”

“Are you?”

“Thoroughly.”

The father of the little girl from the fire came aboard with his daughter. He had received his envelope back from Hương and carried a paper bag instead.

“She chose it,” he said.

The girl took a small framed drawing from the bag.

It showed a man holding a bridge over his head. The bridge had six windows and a chimney. Beneath it, tiny boats floated among blue lines. The man wore a crown almost as large as his body.

Tín accepted it with both hands.

“You gave me a house.”

She nodded.

“Bridges don't have enough rooms.”

He laughed and put the drawing beside the fruit bowl.

When they left, Bích leaned toward him.

“That one can go over the refrigerator.”

He nodded.

On the outer deck, Duke had begun offering short rides around the basin in Mercy's Cousin. Rosa stayed with him for the first two, then stepped ashore and let him take the third alone with an experienced deckhand.

Tín watched from the ballroom window.

Duke stood straight at the wheel, concentrating so hard that he forgot to look impressive. He brought the tug back to the landing without scraping anything. Rosa caught the line, secured it, and waited while he came down from the cabin.

“Well?” he asked.

“You arrived where you intended.”

“High praise.”

“Don't get greedy.”

He leaned against the rail beside her.

“Dinner?”

She looked toward the food stalls.

“We're surrounded.”

“With me.”

She considered him for a moment.

“You paying?”

“I still have money.”

“After the boat?”

“I lost a barge. I acquired education.”

Rosa smiled and took his arm.

They walked toward Bích's stall together.

Tín turned back from the window to find his wife watching him.

“You look pleased.”

“Duke may survive.”

“Rosa will decide.”

At the bar, Sable had already begun running the room. She moved between staff with a pencil behind her ear and a little pad in her hand. When somebody tried to open a tab under Tín's name, she pointed toward the chair where he sat.

“Ask him.”

The man looked at the bandaged hands and the board above his head.

“Cash is fine.”

Bích heard and gave Sable a small approving nod.

Outside, Bishop's men carried tables, cleared plates, and discovered that helping a festival run involved more bending than guarding a doorway. Wade had been assigned to supervise the chairs. Vân corrected him twice and then promoted him to carrying the heavier ones.

Tín watched Bishop stand at the end of the quay with his hands in his pockets.

The street boss came aboard a few minutes later and stopped beside the chair.

“My people are working.”

“I can see.”

“They're good at it.”

“You should tell them.”

Bishop looked toward Wade, who was holding a chair open for the woman who had taken it from him at the market.

“They're going to ask for more money.”

Tín smiled.

“You have a hundred thousand from Rook.”

Bishop sighed.

“Nothing stays private on this street.”

The first full song began at four.

The band had rehearsed beneath smoke, through power cuts, and around a half-assembled organ. Now it had a whole afternoon, a room full of people, and every piece of equipment Marcus had managed to persuade someone to donate.

The sound filled the ballroom and spilled across the basin.

Tín leaned back in his chair.

Bích sat beside him at last.

For one song, nobody brought either of them a problem.

They held hands beneath the table and listened.

## Chapter 27: The Bride Comes Back

Denise arrived at the festival pushing Mateo in a wheelchair he had decorated with a small green pennant from the Little Mercy.

“Temporary,” he told everybody who looked at it.

“Marriage too, if you keep announcing that,” she said.

He looked up at her.

She bent and kissed the top of his head.

The hospital had released him that afternoon with instructions Denise could recite more accurately than the staff. His leg remained wrapped and supported. He would use crutches at home, the chair for the crowded quay, and his wife's patience only where absolutely necessary.

Rosa came down the gangway to meet them.

She put both hands on her brother's shoulders and looked into his face.

“You look better.”

“They gave me trousers.”

“That helps everyone.”

Denise wore a new dress Hương had finished while supposedly resting. It had a simple cream skirt and sleeves that let her move freely. The damaged wedding dress was folded at home. She had decided to keep it, bloodstains and all, because she refused to let anyone tell her later that she had been dramatic about the day.

Hương met her at the ballroom door and inspected the hem.

Denise turned slowly so she could see it all.

“Good?”

“Good.”

“Can I eat now?”

“Carefully.”

They laughed and embraced.

Tín rose from his chair when the couple approached.

Bích put one hand against his chest.

He sat again.

Mateo watched this with satisfaction.

“Even the bridge man gets parked.”

Tín pointed at his wheelchair.

“We have a club now.”

“Do we charge?”

From the stage, Marcus said, “I can help.”

Three women answered him at once.

“No.”

Mateo rolled his chair beside Tín's. Denise sat on the other side, and Bích brought them plates of food before anyone could begin another public speech.

For a while, they ate.

Mateo closed his eyes over the first bite.

“We should have started with this.”

“You wanted a lighthouse,” Denise said.

“I made several ambitious choices.”

“One of them married you.”

He took her hand beneath the table and kissed her fingers.

Tín looked away long enough to let them believe nobody had noticed.

The wedding cake had survived in Rosa's shop refrigerator through every emergency. Now she brought it aboard with two men walking beside her as if escorting a valuable prisoner. Its icing leaned slightly to one side. The little bride on top had lost an arm during transport, and the groom's painted face had acquired a smear of frosting that made him look suspiciously like Mateo after surgery.

Denise studied the figures.

“Accurate enough.”

Mateo nodded.

“Leave them.”

Frank appeared with the blue folder from the ceremony.

Denise saw it and raised a finger.

“We are already married.”

“I know.”

“No reading.”

He looked disappointed.

“Your aunt has been asking.”

“Then read it to her.”

Frank considered this, turned toward the older woman seated near the window, and went to join her. A few minutes later, Tín heard the reading begin quietly behind him. The aunt listened with both hands folded over her handbag. Frank looked relieved to have finally used the page.

Marcus brought the microphone to Denise.

“Anything you want to say?”

She looked at the room, then at Mateo.

“Thank you for coming back.”

She handed the microphone away before the sentence could become a speech.

The applause came gently this time.

Mateo wiped his eyes with a napkin and pretended the food had been too hot.

Bích let him.

The band began the song that should have played on the tug. Denise stood beside Mateo's chair and took both his hands. He pushed himself upright on his good leg with her help, leaning against the table while she held him close.

They moved only a little.

It was enough.

People made room around them. Rosa stood beside Duke near the door. He offered his arm. She looked at it, looked at the crowded floor, and accepted.

“No showing off,” she told him.

“I lost my best opportunity at the bowling alley.”

“You keep finding smaller ones.”

He smiled and led her into the music.

Tín watched them pass the couple, then turned toward Bích.

She shook her head before he asked.

“You can dance later.”

“When?”

“When standing stops being an argument.”

He leaned back, defeated by terms he could not improve.

Mai moved through the room with her camera. She took the photograph of Mateo and Denise first, then Rosa and Duke, then Frank finally reading his page to the aunt. When she reached Tín and Bích, she paused.

“Together,” she said.

Bích leaned against her husband. He put his good arm behind her shoulders and looked at the camera.

“Don't do the burial smile,” Mai warned.

He laughed.

The shutter clicked.

She lowered the camera, checked the remaining film, and looked pleased.

“That one.”

“How do you know?” Tín asked.

“I was looking.”

She went to find Marcus, who had begun explaining to an impressed visitor how much effort it took to make an event look spontaneous. Mai stood beside him until he noticed her.

“You missed the best part,” she said.

“Which?”

She took his hand and pulled him onto the dance floor.

For once, he followed without negotiating a title.

Late in the afternoon, Loretta arrived with a message from Boots. He remained at the hospital for another night and had begun organizing the other patients into an audience for a story in which his role grew every time the nurse changed shifts.

Loretta handed Tín a folded note.

It read: TELL THEM I SAVED THE SANDWICH.

Below it, in smaller writing: Glad you home, brother.

Tín read the second line again.

He folded the note and put it in his pocket.

“We'll take him cake.”

“The nurse already said no,” Loretta replied.

“Then we'll take it to you.”

She smiled.

“That's legal.”

Ruth came by with Ellis and the saxophone player. They had decided which parts of the organ needed a workshop and which could stay aboard for future performances. Ruth wanted music at the pier again once it was rebuilt. Bích wanted enough notice to plan food. Sable wanted somebody to tell her the dates before posters appeared in the windows.

Marcus heard the word posters from across the room and lifted one hand.

Mai pulled it back down.

Tín watched the small group argue over Saturday nights, Sunday lunches, and whether Duke's new tug could provide rides without him calling them royal excursions.

“It was one flyer,” Duke said when accused.

Sable held up three.

He took them from her.

“Early drafts.”

Rosa looked over his shoulder.

“You spelled cousin wrong.”

Hòa, passing with a tray of empty cups, stopped long enough to point at the missing letter.

Duke looked at him.

“Your family enjoys corrections.”

Hòa smiled.

“You provide opportunities.”

Tín laughed until his ribs asked him to reconsider.

Bích handed him water and waited for the pain to pass. Then she took the empty cup and sat close again, her shoulder warm against his.

Outside, the river moved past the excursion landing. Children watched it through the rail while adults stood close enough to catch a sleeve if anyone forgot the water was real. The repaired tug came and went, carrying short trips around the basin. People returned windblown, happy, and newly willing to believe Duke had always intended to become a captain.

Near sunset, Mateo and Denise prepared to leave.

Rosa packed enough food for them to avoid cooking for several days. Hương folded a blanket over Mateo's lap. Frank gave Denise the blue folder, and she accepted it at last, laughing as she tucked it beneath her arm.

At the door, Mateo turned his chair toward Tín.

“Next anniversary, land.”

“A sensible start.”

“Somewhere quiet.”

Tín looked at the ballroom, the band, and Marcus already approaching with another idea.

“Don't tell anyone here.”

Denise laughed and pushed her husband down the gangway into the warm evening.

Tín stayed in his chair and watched them go.

This time, they reached the car without anybody reaching for a gun.

## Chapter 28: Hòa Keeps His Own Hours

On Monday, Duke delivered the safe from the bowling alley to Hòa's school.

He had painted it blue.

The red bowling ball remained lodged inside its ruined door.

“A teaching aid,” Duke explained.

Hòa stood beside the loading bay with a cup of tea in his hand.

“For what subject?”

“Consequences.”

Tín sat in the truck's passenger seat with Bích's permission to leave the house for one hour. She had made Duke repeat the limit before handing over the soft shoes. The bandages beneath Tín's sleeves itched, and the stitches at his side pulled whenever he turned too quickly. He was learning to turn slowly. He disliked being good at it.

Duke climbed onto the truck bed and looked at the safe.

“We need equipment.”

Hòa set his cup on the loading-bay ledge.

“Where does it go?”

“I thought the courtyard.”

“The principal will want to see it first.”

Duke looked toward Tín through the open window.

Tín lifted his bandaged hands.

“I'm on holiday.”

Hòa stepped onto the truck's rear platform, put one hand beneath the safe's lower edge, and drew it toward him.

Duke moved aside.

The safe slid across the bed. Hòa lifted it clear, stepped down to the pavement, and carried it toward the loading bay with the red ball pointing away from his shirt.

The truck rose on its springs.

Duke watched him go.

Then he looked at Tín.

Tín watched his son set the safe beside the wall and pick up the cup of tea.

“Should I ask?” Duke said.

“He has classes.”

“After classes?”

“He has a family.”

Duke closed his mouth.

Hòa returned to the truck.

“Come in. The office has chairs.”

Tín sighed.

“Everyone keeps offering me furniture.”

“You've been hard on theirs.”

They went inside.

The principal was a tall woman with gray hair and a stack of papers that appeared to grow whenever she looked away. She came out to inspect the safe, heard Duke's explanation, and decided it could remain in the courtyard if someone made sure no child could close the damaged door on a hand.

Duke promised to have it fixed open.

“And remove the ball?” she asked.

He looked at the red curve inside the steel.

“I'd prefer to preserve the artistic integrity.”

She looked at Hòa.

Hòa smiled.

“I'll handle it.”

The principal returned to her papers.

Tín sat outside Hòa's classroom while his son finished the last part of a lesson. The door stood open. Students worked at their desks, some bent intently over the page, others wearing the expression of people hoping arithmetic might become embarrassed and leave them alone.

Hòa moved between them without raising his voice.

At one desk, a boy pointed toward the corridor.

“That's your father?”

“Yes.”

“He threw the bridge.”

“He moved part of it. Finish the problem.”

“Can you do that?”

Hòa looked at the boy's page.

“I can do this. So can you.”

He tapped the unfinished line and moved on.

Tín smiled to himself.

When the bell rang, several students came into the corridor slowly enough to make their intentions obvious. One carried a notebook open to a clean page.

“Could you sign?” he asked.

Tín took the pencil.

“For school?”

“For my brother. He doesn't believe you came here.”

Tín signed his name and handed the notebook back.

The boy looked at it, then at Hòa.

“Your dad's famous.”

Hòa began collecting the exercise books.

“His teacher was very strict,” Tín said.

The boy looked from father to son, laughed, and hurried after his friends.

Duke went to arrange the safe's mounting with the caretaker. Tín remained in the classroom while Hòa stacked books into a canvas bag.

“You waited in the trunk,” Tín said.

His son looked up.

“Yes.”

“After the latch was broken.”

Hòa folded the bag's flap over the books.

“The men outside kept shouting. I was curious what they wanted.”

“They wanted me.”

“They chose an indirect approach.”

Tín studied his son's face.

Hòa held his gaze without challenge.

“You could have left.”

“I could have.”

The classroom was quiet now. Outside, Duke could be heard telling the caretaker that the ball had traveled faster than anyone in the building had expected. The caretaker asked whether the bowling alley was still open.

Tín rested his hands on his knees.

“When did you start keeping so much to yourself?”

Hòa considered the question before answering.

“When I found work I wanted to come home from.”

Tín nodded slowly.

His son took the bag from the desk.

“You know where I live.”

“Your mother makes that difficult to forget.”

“Then come for tea without a damaged vehicle.”

Tín looked toward the loading bay.

“Duke brought this one.”

“You're improving.”

They walked back outside together.

The safe stood beside the courtyard wall, its blue paint still glossy in the sun. Duke had found a sheet of cardboard and written a sign for it.

DO NOT CHALLENGE THE TEACHER'S FATHER.

Hòa read it.

He took the marker and added a second line.

PLEASE FINISH YOUR HOMEWORK.

Tín laughed until he had to hold his ribs.

Duke looked between them.

“This is why nobody on Bell Street knows how to act normal.”

Hòa handed back the marker.

“You're welcome to practice.”

They drove home before the hour ended.

At the house, Bích checked the clock, inspected Tín's shirt for fresh damage, and allowed him inside. Duke received a container of food for bringing the patient back in the same general condition.

“Rosa gets half,” she told him.

He looked at the container.

“She'll know?”

“I called her.”

He accepted his fate and went down the steps.

Tín found Mai at the kitchen table sorting photographs. The new prints lay in careful rows: the repaired ballroom, Denise beside Mateo's chair, Ruth at the organ, Duke trying to look casual beside Rosa, and Hòa carrying cups through the crowd.

Mai held up the photograph of Tín and Bích.

“This one's for home.”

He took it.

His wife leaned against him in the picture, looking directly at the camera. His own face had turned toward her. The bandages were visible. So was the ridiculous board above the chair, cropped at the edge so only part of the number remained.

“You didn't get the whole sign,” he said.

“I got the important people.”

He looked at her.

She met his eyes and smiled.

“Mr. Chen said I can learn the presses properly. Not just carry paper.”

“What did you say?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She returned to the photographs. He stood beside the table for a moment, then placed the picture gently on top of the new refrigerator.

Bích came to look at it.

“Better than the crown.”

Tín put his arm around her shoulders.

“You can say both are good.”

“I can say many things. I choose carefully.”

She leaned against him just as she had in the photograph.

Behind them, the refrigerator hummed steadily, pleased at last to be the least troublesome large object in the house.

## Chapter 29: The Long Way Home

Three weeks later, Bích allowed him to walk to the river without an escort.

She came anyway.

“You said I could go alone,” Tín reminded her.

“I wanted air.”

“In the same direction?”

“It's a small street.”

She took his arm as they went down the steps. The wounds along his side had closed. The stitches were out. His palms remained tender where the deeper cuts had healed, and a pale line crossed the bridge of his nose. He wore the soft shoes Arturo had made and carried no crown, weapon, or object requiring two men to explain why he was holding it.

At the gate, Mai called after them.

“Be home before dinner.”

Tín turned.

“Who is making it?”

“Dad.”

“We'll be punctual.”

Hòa appeared behind her holding a kitchen towel.

“I heard that.”

“Then improve accordingly.”

His son smiled and went back inside.

Bell Street had repaired its windows. The new glass looked a little too clean beside the older panes. Boots's garage had a fresh section of roofing, and the white tow truck's cab no longer admitted daylight from above. He had insisted on keeping the torn steering wheel, mounted on the wall beneath a small plaque reading ADVANCED DRIVER TRAINING.

Boots sat outside the shop with his recovering arm supported against his chest.

Loretta stood beside him holding a cup.

“You're walking,” Boots said when he saw Tín.

“I've done it before.”

“Without a vehicle in your hands.”

Tín looked toward the white truck.

Boots lifted one finger.

“Don't admire anything mechanically.”

Loretta handed Bích the cup she had been carrying. The two women stepped into the salon doorway and began discussing somebody's daughter, a dress, and a decision that had apparently been made without enough consultation.

Tín sat beside Boots.

For a while they watched the street.

A customer came out of the garage and stopped beneath the mounted steering wheel. He read the plaque, looked at Tín, and opened his mouth.

Boots pointed at the cash register inside.

“Pay for your repair first. Story afterward.”

The man laughed and went back in.

Tín looked at his friend.

“You charging now?”

“Only people with warranties.”

He shifted his arm carefully and let out a breath through his nose.

“Still hurts,” Tín said.

“Some days.”

“You sleeping?”

“Loretta says I am. Apparently the whole house knows.”

They smiled.

Boots looked down the hill toward the river.

“You ever think about that first year?”

“Here?”

“You came in asking if I could fix the little fan.”

Tín remembered the fan. It had rattled so loudly that Hương said the neighbors would think they were keeping a motorcycle in the bedroom.

“You said you worked on cars.”

“You said a fan was a car with limited ambition.”

“You fixed it.”

“You sat there for two hours telling me which screw sounded loose.”

Tín laughed.

Boots looked at him, then at the steering wheel on the wall.

“Should've known.”

“You did know. You kept inviting me back.”

“You brought food.”

From the salon doorway, Bích said, “I brought food.”

Boots raised his good hand.

“And we have been grateful under the correct management ever since.”

She nodded, satisfied, and returned to Loretta's story.

When they left, Boots stood long enough to embrace Tín with one arm. Neither squeezed hard. Both pretended this was a matter of style.

“Come by tomorrow,” Boots said.

“Something broken?”

“Probably. Come anyway.”

Tín promised.

At Vân's grocery, the birthday banner had finally come down. In its place hung a framed photograph of Tín beneath the words PLEASE REFRIGERATE AFTER OPENING. Vân had enlarged the writing rather than crop it out.

Bích stopped to look.

“You're encouraging him.”

Vân came to the doorway.

“It sells cold drinks.”

Tín examined the photograph.

“I should receive a percentage.”

Vân handed him a bag of fruit.

“Your quarterly payment.”

“Generous.”

“There's a bruised peach. That's tax.”

Bích took the bag before he could start negotiating.

They continued downhill.

The old berth at Pilgrim Slip had new pilings and a half-finished landing. Rook's damage payments were turning into wood, glass, medical bills paid, and men arriving on time with tools instead of guns. The remaining claims would take longer. Tín had left Sable to argue with the people who enjoyed numbers written on several different pages. He preferred seeing the boards become a place where someone could stand.

Rosa and Duke waited beside the repaired gangway.

Mercy's Cousin sat beyond them with a clean coat of paint. The gold outline around its name had been straightened. The little family tree remained, now protected beneath clear varnish despite Rosa's objections.

“We're taking her around the bend,” Duke said. “You coming?”

Bích looked at Tín.

He kept his face innocent.

“I didn't arrange it.”

“That's usually when it's worse.”

Rosa held out a hand.

“I'll supervise.”

Bích accepted that.

They boarded. Duke helped her down the short step, then waited while Tín followed. He wore a plain captain's cap with no crown on it. Tín considered this a sign of either maturity or Rosa's influence and decided there was no need to distinguish them aloud.

The tug left the berth smoothly.

Duke looked toward Rosa.

She nodded once.

He smiled at the water ahead as if it had complimented him personally.

They passed the River Queen at its temporary landing. The vessel was open for an afternoon meal, its tables occupied by families, workers, and two men in suits who looked uncertain whether the food justified telling their colleagues where they had been. Sable stood at the bar, listening to a supplier's explanation with a pencil poised above her pad.

She saw the tug and raised a hand.

Tín waved back.

Marcus leaned out from the upper balcony.

“Grandmaster! We need a name for the Sunday show!”

Bích cupped one hand around her mouth.

“Lunch!”

Rosa laughed.

Marcus stood with the defeated expression of a promoter outvoted by food, then turned back inside to tell somebody he had a new concept.

Beyond the barge, the eastern bridge work continued.

The forty-tonne span rested on proper supports again. Inspectors and engineers had spent days examining what the fall, catch, and landing had done to it. Some parts would be replaced before it could go into service. Tín could see workers fitting a new brace along one side while another crew prepared the lifting gear with considerable attention to the cables.

The young crane operator stood beside the workboat.

He recognized Tín and took off his cap.

Tín raised his hand.

The operator held both palms up, showing an exaggerated amount of distance between them.

Tín laughed.

“What did he say?” Bích asked.

“He'd prefer to use the crane next time.”

“A sensible man.”

They passed through the lower passage, clear now beneath the morning-blue sky. The water widened beyond it. For several minutes, the shoreline receded enough that Bell Street's noise became a low murmur behind the engine.

Bích stood beside Tín at the rail.

He offered her the unbruised peach from Vân's bag.

She took it, rubbed it clean with the corner of a cloth, and cut it with the little knife she carried for fruit. She gave him half.

They ate while the tug moved across the basin.

“You could have bought an ordinary birthday present,” she said.

“I did. The refrigerator was ordinary.”

“Then you became involved.”

He smiled.

She looked toward the distant barge.

“I like the kitchen.”

“I noticed.”

“I like that Hương can use the upstairs room for fittings. I like that Mai has somewhere to show her photographs. I even like Duke when Rosa is nearby.”

From the wheelhouse, Duke called, “Thank you, I think.”

Bích ignored him.

“I don't want every week to be this week.”

Tín held the rail with one hand and looked at the scars across the other.

“Neither do I.”

She studied his face.

“You mean it today.”

“Today is available.”

She leaned her shoulder against his.

He put his arm around her.

For a while they watched gulls follow a fishing boat across the open water. One bird dropped toward the wake, missed whatever it had seen, and rose again with an indignant cry.

“That one's you,” Bích said.

Tín looked at the bird.

“Magnificent?”

“Loud when hungry.”

He laughed and kissed her temple.

They returned to Bell Street before dinner.

Duke brought the tug alongside without touching the fenders hard enough to make Rosa comment. She caught the line, secured it, and kissed him on the cheek as he stepped down from the wheelhouse.

He stopped moving.

Tín looked at Bích.

“We've lost the captain.”

Rosa took Duke's hand and pulled him out of their way.

“Temporary mechanical fault.”

They left the two of them aboard and walked home slowly.

At the top of the hill, the house windows were open. The smell of cooking reached the gate before they did. Mai sat on the steps reading a printshop manual, making little marks in the margin with a pencil. She looked up when they approached.

“You made it.”

“Your father's reputation encouraged speed,” Tín said.

From inside, Hòa called, “I heard that too.”

Mai closed the manual and stood. She took the fruit bag from Bích, then looked at her grandfather's soft shoes.

“No damage?”

Tín lifted one foot for inspection.

“A successful expedition.”

She bowed toward him with exaggerated gravity.

“Welcome home, Grandmaster.”

He bowed back, a little shallower because his ribs still had opinions.

“You may rise.”

“I already did.”

“Advanced student.”

She kissed his cheek and went inside laughing.

Hòa met them in the hallway with the kitchen towel over his shoulder. He looked at his father, then down at the clean shoes, then at his mother carrying nothing heavier than her purse.

“Good trip?”

“Very quiet.”

“Did you cause that?”

“I'm learning.”

Hòa smiled.

They stood facing each other for a moment. Tín remembered the damaged trunk, the bollard beneath his son's hand, the safe carried off the truck without spilling a cup of tea. Hòa looked back as though there was plenty of time for whatever question his father eventually decided to ask.

Tín put one hand on his son's shoulder.

“Dinner smells good.”

“Then sit before it gets ideas.”

They went to the table.

Hương brought the last dish from the kitchen. Mai poured tea. Bích inspected the rice, approved it without surprise, and handed the serving spoon to Hòa. He began filling bowls while Tín took the photograph from the top of the refrigerator and moved it to the shelf where they could all see it.

The gold-plated crown sat beside the picture.

Someone had put a wooden spoon through its center.

Tín looked at his wife.

She met his eyes calmly.

“Useful,” she said.

He smiled and took his seat.

Outside, a boat sounded its horn on the river. Another answered farther away. Boots's shop door rolled down with its familiar rattle. Somewhere along Bell Street, Marcus was explaining Sunday lunch as if he had invented the afternoon.

Tín reached for the bowl his son offered.

Bích touched his wrist before he lifted it.

“Hot.”

He waited.

Then he blew gently across the surface, took the first mouthful, and began telling his family about Duke's new problem with kissing.

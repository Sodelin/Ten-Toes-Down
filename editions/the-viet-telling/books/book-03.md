# Book III: The River's Weight

## 1. The Chair Beneath the House

In 1992, when Lê Văn Tín was seventy, a woman brought him a debt bearing the thumbprint of a dead man.

He was repairing a chair beneath his house. The morning heat had brought the smell of wet earth through the floorboards. Beside him a kettle rested on a charcoal stove. He set the chair leg down before taking the paper.

The woman was Thảo, a cashier from the ferry landing at Bến Sậy. Mud had dried halfway up her trousers. A shallow cut crossed her eyebrow, and she held a second sheet inside a plastic sleeve against her stomach.

Tín read the amount twice.

“Your father died in February,” he said.

“They know.”

The first paper said her father had renewed his obligation in July. Beneath the thumbprint stood an old seal: two boats, their bows facing one another.

Tín had chosen that design himself.

“The other paper?”

She handed him the sleeve. It contained a copy of the death record. Someone had written a number in the corner, crossed it out, and written a different number beside it.

“Who wrote those?”

“The men at the office. They said I was late bringing proof.”

“You were late proving a man was dead?”

“They said my father was late telling them.”

Tín looked up.

Thảo's face remained quite still.

He invited her to sit. There were two chairs beneath the house. One lacked the leg in his hand; the other held his wife's basket of mending. He put the basket on the table, wiped the seat with a clean cloth, and moved the chair out of the sunlight.

“Tea?”

“I have to be back at the landing.”

“The kettle's already hot.”

She sat as if agreeing to a small delay that would be held against her later. He poured tea into a cup and set it beside her. She did not drink.

Forty years earlier, boat owners had used his money to replace engines, repair hulls, buy fuel. He had settled disputes at a table on the landing. Later he had employed men to collect payments. Later still he had stopped asking how they collected them. Along the connected rivers and canals, people had called the arrangement the Compact.

He had left it seventeen years ago. Until that morning he had permitted himself to call leaving an achievement.

“Did your father borrow the original amount?”

“Some of it. After his engine caught fire.”

“How much did he repay?”

She named a figure without looking at the papers.

“You kept the receipts?”

“I collect fares for a living, Mr. Tín. I can count.”

“Then count for me.”

She did. Principal, interest, engine hire while the boat was being repaired. The penalty for missing a payment during her father's illness. A fee for sending someone to collect the penalty. A fee for recording the fact that the penalty had been collected. Each item arrived in a level voice. By the time she finished, he had the entire account in his head.

The sum on the paper was wrong even by the collectors' own rules.

“Who holds this now?”

“Phúc.”

Tín set his cup down without drinking.

“He came himself?”

“His men. They took my brother to work off the balance.”

“Where?”

“First the rice mill. Then the repair yard. They say he's been transferred. It's been three days.”

“Your brother's name?”

“Lâm.”

He remembered a boy running along the landing with a stripped engine cover held over his head against the rain. That had been years ago. The boy in his memory was perhaps twelve; the man Thảo described was twenty-nine.

“Does he owe them money?”

“Only what they say our father owes.”

She touched the cut above her eye.

“I asked to speak to Phúc. They told me to go home and bring the money. I asked again.”

“Which man?”

“There are four in the ticket office. The oldest wears a gold watch.”

Tín knew the watch before she described its face.

His wife came down the stairs carrying a plate and a small knife. Bích was sixty-seven. Her hair had turned white before his, which she considered proof that being married to him was more demanding than being him.

She looked at Thảo's eyebrow, then at the papers.

“Have you eaten?” she asked.

“I'm going back to work.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Bích set the plate on the table and went upstairs again. Tín heard a cupboard open. A moment later she returned with another bowl and a jar of pickled vegetables.

Thảo took the bowl when it was put into her hands.

Tín watched her eat. The first few mouthfuls were careful. Then she stopped pretending she was not hungry.

His granddaughter came down with her schoolbooks held against her chest. Mai was seventeen, and every object she possessed appeared to have acquired a new urgency during the previous year. Books, a comb, a pair of shoes: all were now parts of a future which required him to stop putting them wherever he found convenient.

She saw the guest and greeted her. Then she inspected the chair on its side.

“You said it would be finished yesterday.”

“It was finished yesterday.”

“Then why is its leg in your lap?”

“Your father sat on it.”

From the stairs, Hòa said, “My mistake. I assumed it was a chair.”

He came down carrying a basin. At forty-three, Hòa had his mother's narrow face and none of Tín's taste for command. He taught arithmetic. He could quiet a room full of children without raising his voice, a power Tín had never learned to imitate.

Mai held out her satchel. The catch hung at an angle.

“This too.”

“When you return,” Tín said.

She looked at Thảo again. Whatever she saw in the woman's face changed her expression. She lowered the satchel.

“All right.”

Hòa waited until she had gone along the path before taking the debt paper from his father.

He read it standing beside the table. Bích carried the empty plate inside. Nobody spoke while he read.

“You said the business was finished,” Hòa said.

“I said I had finished with it.”

Hòa set the basin down carefully.

“That's a fine distinction for people who are still paying.”

Tín rose. The leg of the broken chair remained in his hand. He placed it on the table, took a clean shirt from the peg beside the stairs, and began doing up its buttons.

His wife came back to the doorway.

“Are you going to shout at someone?” she asked.

“Probably.”

“You never used to bother shouting.”

“I'm older.”

She stepped down and adjusted the collar he had folded under itself. Her fingers were cool against his throat.

“Then remember that before someone makes you prove otherwise.”

He met her eyes. She had seen him brought home with wounds that should have killed him. She had seen men touch their foreheads to his hand and other men refuse to enter a room where he was sitting. She had never confused those things with being a husband.

“I'll come back,” he said.

“Come back having thought.”

Hòa followed him to the gate.

“What should I tell Mai?”

“Tell her I'll mend the catch when I return.”

“I'm asking where you're going.”

“The old landing.”

His son waited. Tín took a small key from the ring in his pocket.

“The books in my cupboard. Look at them.”

Hòa looked toward the house. He had never been allowed to open that cupboard.

“All of them?”

Tín put the key in his hand. “All.”

Thảo walked quickly. He matched her pace without appearing to hurry. They passed a woman turning sheets on a line and a bicycle repairer sitting beneath a patched umbrella. The repairer recognized Tín, stood, and immediately pretended he had stood to reach a wheel hanging above him.

At a stall near the road, two men stopped arguing as he approached. One looked down into his bowl. The other raised a hand and let it fall before completing the greeting.

Thảo noticed.

“They still know you.”

“Most of them owe me nothing.”

“That isn't what I said.”

He looked at her. She continued walking.

The old landing had lost most of its trade when the new crossing opened downstream. A corrugated warehouse stood between the road and the water. Behind it, the abandoned loading gantry rose above the trees. Its upper beam carried a line of birds. Below, a man sat on a low wall repairing a basket while his grandson searched the mud for a missing marble.

Six motorcycles stood outside the old ticket office.

Thảo stopped across the road. Tín gave her both papers.

“Stay here.”

“You don't get to lose those.”

“I won't.”

“Or tell them the money is mine to pay.”

He nodded once.

Four collectors came outside when they saw him. The youngest held a length of iron pipe. The oldest wore the watch Tín had given Phúc's father after a profitable season.

“Uncle,” the older man said. “Nobody told us you were visiting.”

“Where is Lâm?”

“Which Lâm?”

Tín looked at the watch.

The man shifted his wrist behind his thigh.

“Thảo's brother,” Tín said.

“These are private accounts.”

“Then speak quietly.”

The collector smiled. The other three moved apart by small amounts. Tín watched the reflections in the office window. The youngest was left-handed. One of the men behind him carried a knife inside his trouser leg. The older collector's right hand rested too close to his shirt opening.

“Lâm accepted work,” the older man said. “He'll come home when it's completed.”

“What work?”

“Repairs.”

“Where?”

“I don't have to answer that.”

“You're answering it badly. That isn't the same thing.”

The pipe struck the side of Tín's head.

He had heard the young man move. He had let it happen because the older collector's hand was going inside his shirt, and Tín needed to see what it held before anyone started running.

The pipe bent.

A thin line of blood ran into Tín's collar.

For a moment the young collector continued holding the pipe against his head, unable to understand why the blow had stopped there. Tín turned his face toward him.

“Was that meant to help?”

The older man drew a pistol.

Tín stepped between them. His left hand closed around the gun and the fingers holding it. Metal flattened against bone. He drove his right palm through the younger man's guard and into his chest. The young man went backward through the ticket-office door and took it off its hinges.

The collector with the knife bent to reach it.

Tín kicked him under the chin before his fingers touched the handle. The man's head struck the wall. His legs folded.

The fourth man ran.

Tín released the broken gun. The older collector dropped to his knees, making a small, continuous sound. Across the road the basket repairer had taken his grandson inside.

“Where?” Tín asked.

“They move them.”

“Who moves them?”

“Different boats.”

Tín crouched. The man tried to retreat and found the wall behind his shoulders.

“You have until the pain stops surprising you.”

“I don't know where he is now.”

“Then tell me where he was.”

He learned about the mill and the repair yard. He learned that debtors were taken from several landings, kept at different sites, and sent where labour was needed. Some came home after a week. Some were transferred again. Their wages were entered against accounts which acquired fresh charges while they worked.

The man did not know where Lâm had gone. Tín believed him. His fear had become too immediate to leave room for invention.

“Who does know?”

“Phúc. The woman who keeps his schedules. Lan.”

Tín stood.

The collector looked up at him, sweat and tears shining together on his face.

“He'll say you attacked us.”

“He'll be right.”

“He'll come here.”

“Tell him I don't intend to make it difficult to find me.”

Thảo crossed the road after Tín stepped away. She went into the office, found a basin, and filled it at the tap. The youngest collector had begun to stir beneath the broken door. She moved the door enough for him to breathe properly.

Tín stood beside the river. The water carried small pieces of vegetation past the landing, each turning when it reached the disturbed current beside a piling.

Behind him, Thảo asked the youngest man whether he could feel his hands.

Tín looked toward the old gantry. Seventeen years of weather had passed over it since he last stood beneath its beam. Someone had painted his old seal over with a rectangle of white. The paint had peeled away in places, leaving the two boats visible again.

He had expected the world to keep moving after he left.

He had not asked where.


## 2. Men at the Table

Tín borrowed the upstairs room of a restaurant near the new crossing and sent for the men who had once waited to be invited into it.

The owner asked how many places to lay.

“Six.”

“Will they all come?”

“Put out six bowls.”

The room overlooked the ferry queue. Below the window, bicycles stood between sacks of produce. A woman shaded a sleeping child with a newspaper. Across the water, a truck driver worked on something beneath his bonnet while passengers watched him with the patience of people who had already used up their complaints.

Tín washed the blood from his neck in the restaurant kitchen. The owner gave him a towel without asking how it had got there. He pressed the cloth against the cut and inspected the old scar above his left eyebrow in a small mirror nailed to the wall.

For seventeen years he had been a man who could be left alone with a kettle and a broken chair.

Now a boy downstairs was telling another boy about a bent pipe.

He climbed the stairs.

Chấn arrived first. He had grown broad around the stomach and careful around the eyes. He brought a basket of fruit, which he set on the table with both hands.

“For your wife.”

“She can buy fruit.”

“Of course.”

“Sit down, Chấn.”

Chấn sat. He looked at the six bowls and chose the chair farthest from the stairs.

Lan came next, carrying a small handbag and no visible gift. She was in her forties, with a firm mouth and hair pinned away from her face. Tín remembered her as a clerk who had corrected a total in front of a room full of angry boat owners and refused to apologize for being right.

“You sent a schoolboy to fetch me,” she said.

“He knew the way.”

“He announced it in front of everyone.”

“That saved you explaining where you were going.”

She pulled out a chair.

Sơn arrived behind her. Tín had employed him as a collector, then as a driver, then as the man sent to persuade other collectors to behave. He was sixty now. His right foot dragged slightly, the result of a fall from a motorcycle rather than any of the incidents he preferred to tell stories about.

He looked at Tín's collar.

“I heard you found something to do.”

“You could have told me.”

“Which part?”

Tín let him see that the answer had landed badly.

Sơn took a chair beside the window.

The last man came without hurrying. Dưỡng wore a pale shirt with sleeves rolled neatly to the elbow. His hair remained thick and dark except at the temples. He paused at the top of the stairs to hold the door for a waitress carrying a pot of soup.

“Careful,” he told her. “The hinge catches.”

He eased the door back with two fingers. The metal hinge moved inside the wood, pulling screws out far enough to straighten itself. He released it. The waitress passed through without noticing what he had done.

Tín noticed.

“You still like fixing things where nobody can thank you,” he said.

Dưỡng smiled. “You still like arranging rooms so everybody must.”

They shook hands.

There had been a time when people crossed a street rather than walk between them. Tín could remember Dưỡng beside him on a half-built landing, both of them young enough to believe that being able to force a structure upright meant they understood how to make it last.

At fifty-seven, Dưỡng had acquired the manners of a man accustomed to speaking with officials and engineers. He owned dredging equipment, warehouses, a company that repaired banks and another that supplied materials to the first. Most of the work was real. Tín had seen the channels his crews kept open.

That had always made him more useful than a man who only knew how to hurt people.

“Where is Phúc?” Tín asked.

Lan set her handbag beneath her chair.

“Busy.”

“Doing what?”

“He didn't give me a list.”

The waitress began serving. Tín waited until she had left and the door had closed.

“A dead man borrowed money in July,” he said.

Nobody reached for the food.

He put Thảo's copy of the demand on the table. He had asked her permission to bring it. She had made him promise to return it before evening and had watched him fold it until she was satisfied he had not damaged the thumbprint.

Lan read the paper. Chấn looked over her shoulder and immediately sat back.

Dưỡng did not touch it.

“There are mistakes,” Lan said.

“Someone supplied a thumb.”

“I can have this corrected.”

“Where is her brother?”

She looked at the name again.

“Lâm was sent to a repair crew.”

“Where?”

“I'll find out.”

“You make the schedules.”

“I make payment schedules. The men who move workers do not ask me where to put them.”

Tín watched her eyes. She was irritated rather than frightened. That meant she knew enough to resent the question and not enough to answer it safely.

“Who asks them?”

“Phúc.”

“Then someone can interrupt him.”

Chấn cleared his throat.

“There is a larger difficulty. Some of those accounts have been guaranteed elsewhere.”

Tín turned toward him.

“Elsewhere.”

“Other lenders. Contractors. Men who paid to have the work completed.”

“Paid whom?”

“The office.”

“And the workers?”

Chấn moved his bowl a finger's width farther from the table edge.

“Their earnings reduce their balances.”

Tín took a pencil from the ledge beside the window. The owner kept it there for writing orders. He turned over the paper placemat in front of him.

“Give me an example.”

Chấn looked at Lan.

“You raised it,” she said.

Chấn named an amount, then the daily rate offered for labour. Tín asked about food. Lodging. Transport. The fee for transferring a worker from one site to another. Lan corrected two figures. Sơn swore quietly at the third.

Tín wrote the figures down.

“At this rate, a man who works every day owes more at the end of the month.”

“Only on the older accounts,” Chấn said.

“How old is an older account?”

“It depends.”

Tín put down the pencil.

“Don't make me spend the rest of lunch finding out how stupid you think I am.”

Chấn looked toward Dưỡng.

Dưỡng finally reached for the paper. He studied it, then put it beside his bowl.

“The arrangement has become untidy.”

“Men are being held.”

“You held boats.”

“I remember.”

“A family without its boat cannot leave either.”

“I remember that too.”

Dưỡng considered him. Outside, a ferry sounded its horn. The noise came through the window and made the glasses tremble very slightly against the table.

“Then say what you want,” he said.

Tín placed both hands flat beside his bowl.

“Everyone working against an inherited balance comes home. Anyone whose payments already exceed what was agreed comes home. Anyone who did not choose the job comes home. Their wages are paid directly to them. If you believe there is a real debt afterward, you bring the claim where somebody can examine it.”

Chấn gave a short, involuntary laugh.

Nobody joined him.

“And the contractors?” Lan asked.

“I will speak to them.”

“You intend to pay?”

“For work actually done and materials actually supplied. I don't intend to buy people back from men who had no right to take them.”

“They will call that a distinction,” Dưỡng said.

“They can call it dinner. They're still bringing the men home.”

Sơn picked up his bowl and began to eat. It was an old signal, one he had used when arguments at Tín's table were finished. Chấn saw him do it and looked down at his own untouched food.

Lan remained still.

“You can't settle this by paying one sum,” she said. “Every man at this table has promised someone else a share of something he doesn't yet possess. The labour, the landing fees, next season's repair work. You pull one promise out and the others start moving.”

Tín looked at the spare bowl set for Phúc.

“How many promises did he make on my name?”

Lan's mouth tightened.

“There it is,” he said.

Dưỡng leaned back.

“Your name made the first arrangements possible. It did not disappear when you went home.”

“You could have stopped using it.”

“You could have stopped owning the buildings.”

For the first time, Tín looked away.

He owned the old engine yard. A warehouse. A section of land beside the upper channel. Men paid rent through people he no longer saw. Hòa handled the household accounts, and Bích had occasionally asked why a payment had increased. Tín had answered that trade must be improving.

He could hear that answer now with his own voice removed from it.

Lan watched him closely.

“If I bring you six men today,” she said, “will you let me untangle the rest?”

“Which six?”

“The ones I can locate before evening.”

“Bring them.”

“Without another visit like the one this morning.”

“That depends on the welcome.”

She exhaled through her nose.

“You broke Khang's hand in three places.”

“Four. He was holding a pistol.”

Sơn kept eating, but Tín saw the corner of his mouth move.

Lan rose to use the telephone downstairs. Chấn followed, claiming he needed to speak to one of his own men. When the door closed behind them, Dưỡng began serving himself soup.

“You should have invited me to your house,” he said.

“You know the road.”

“Your wife doesn't like me.”

“She knows the road too.”

Dưỡng laughed once. It was the first unguarded sound he had made.

“How is Mai?”

Tín's eyes returned to him.

“Preparing for examinations.”

“Hòa must be pleased.”

“He worries. It gives him something to do when the pupils are behaving.”

“And you?”

“I am repairing furniture.”

Dưỡng glanced at the blood dried beneath his collar.

“Not today.”

The waitress knocked. Tín called her in. She replaced the empty soup pot and gathered plates while the three men spoke about ordinary things: the rise in fuel costs, a bad repair to a road, a mutual acquaintance whose second marriage was proving more expensive than his first.

When she had gone, Dưỡng put his spoon down.

“Phúc is not his father.”

“I noticed.”

“He grew up hearing what you could do.”

“So did my son.”

“Your son didn't have to collect money from men who used your name to excuse not paying.”

Sơn stopped eating.

“Don't put that on the boy,” he said.

Dưỡng looked at him mildly.

“Which boy?”

“Either of them.”

Tín stood and went to the window. The truck across the river had started. Its driver closed the bonnet while several passengers applauded with deliberate cruelty. Ordinary irritation carried clearly across the water.

“If Phúc wants to speak to me,” Tín said, “he knows where I live.”

“He may want more than a conversation.”

“Then he should bring more than a pipe.”

Dưỡng rose. He took the fruit basket Chấn had brought and moved it out of the strip of sunlight crossing the table.

“I have a bank repair to inspect.”

“Which bank?”

“Above the upper cut.”

Tín looked at him. The upper cut served small boats that could not safely use the main branch during rough weather. Land beside it had once belonged to three farming families. He remembered refusing to buy it because the families did not want to sell.

“Whose work?”

“Mine.”

“Who requested it?”

Dưỡng buttoned his cuff.

“Come and see. You're allowed to leave the house for reasons other than breaking someone's hands.”

He shook Tín's hand again and left.

Sơn waited until the stairs stopped creaking.

“You still trust him?”

“I trust him to know what he wants.”

“That wasn't the question.”

Tín returned to the table and picked up Thảo's paper. He folded it along the creases she had made.

“Find out which boats move the repair crews,” he said.

“Quietly?”

“Accurately.”

Sơn nodded.

Before evening, Lan brought six men to the restaurant courtyard.

They arrived in the back of a small truck with a collector sitting beside the driver. One man had a bandage around his wrist. Another carried his clothes in a fertiliser sack. They climbed down uncertainly and waited in a line until Tín told them to come under the awning.

Thảo was there. She had finished her shift early and lost the pay for the remaining hours. She looked at each face before looking at Lan.

“My brother?”

“Not with this crew.”

“Where is he?”

“I'm still finding out.”

Thảo took a step toward her. Tín did not move between them.

“You can find six men when he asks,” she said. “I've been asking for three days.”

Lan received that with a face so controlled it had become a kind of anger.

“These six are here.”

One of the men raised his hand, then lowered it as if embarrassed by the habit.

“Can we go?” he asked.

Tín looked at him.

“Yes.”

“Home?”

“Yes.”

The man turned to the others. None moved immediately. Then the one with the sack stepped out into the road. He looked back once and began to walk faster.

The others followed.

Thảo watched them leave. The anger did not leave her face, but something else entered it. She went after the man with the bandaged wrist and asked where he had worked.

Tín saw Lan watching her.

“I want the rest,” he said.

“I heard you the first time.”

“Good. Then you can start before I say it again.”

She opened her handbag to take out a cigarette. Her hands were steady. Only when she struck the match did he see her thumb fail once against the box.

Across the river, the last ferry of the afternoon sounded its horn.


## 3. The People on Shore

Hòa had opened the cupboard and found that his father numbered everything.

The books were wrapped by year. Envelopes held receipts for roof repairs, purchases of timber, deliveries of fuel. There were lists of names, lists of boats, lists of sums divided between men who had since died. Hòa brought a selection to the school because carrying the whole cupboard would have required a cart.

He placed the books on a teacher's desk while Tín stood beside him.

“You never kept the household accounts this carefully,” Hòa said.

“Your mother kept those.”

“She'll be delighted to hear she should have charged a collection fee.”

Tín opened the oldest volume. The paper had yellowed at the edges. His handwriting remained clear: a little narrow, each line beginning the same distance from the margin.

He had written down the cost of every rope.

Three pages later, Hòa stopped him turning the leaf.

“This.”

No boat released until the account is satisfied.

Next of kin to assume the balance.

Tín read the sentences. He remembered writing them after a dispute in which a borrower had died and his sons had taken the boat to another crossing. At the time he had been furious about the inconvenience. He remembered the room, the weather, the ink stain on his finger. The dead man had become part of the circumstances surrounding the inconvenience.

“Yes,” he said.

“You told me the rule was only about property.”

“That was how I described it.”

Hòa waited for something more. Tín did not supply it. An explanation would be easy. He had employed explanations almost as carefully as he had employed men.

In the corridor outside, several people were arriving together. Thảo had asked the six released workers whether they would speak about where they had been. Four agreed. One went home and sent his wife. The sixth said he wanted nothing more to do with any of them.

Tín stood when they entered. Most stopped just inside the door.

The man with the bandaged wrist looked at the books on the desk and then at Tín.

“Is this another agreement?”

“No,” Hòa said.

“Do we have to sign?”

“Only if you want what you say written down and attributed to you.”

The man looked toward the open door.

Tín moved his chair away from it.

Thảo arrived with a pot of tea borrowed from the caretaker. She saw the arrangement of the room and began moving chairs before anyone could object. She put them in a loose circle, carried the teacher's chair out from behind the desk, and placed it beside the others.

“Nobody needs to stand in a line,” she said.

Tín sat where she pointed.

The first man spoke about a mill upriver. He had gone willingly to repair a machine and stayed because the manager kept adding charges for the tools he used. The second had been taken from his boat while his wife was buying fuel. The third could not say how much he owed because the collector refused to show him an account.

Hòa asked questions and wrote slowly. He repeated each important point before putting it down. Thảo made tea. Tín listened.

When the wife of the absent worker began to speak, she looked at him and stopped.

“Would you prefer me outside?” he asked.

Nobody answered.

He stood and went into the corridor.

The school caretaker was repairing a window catch at the far end. He glanced at Tín, considered saying something, and returned to the catch. Rain had begun in short bursts, each darkening the courtyard and then leaving steam rising from the bricks.

Tín sat on a low bench outside the classroom. Through the wall he heard voices become less careful.

He stayed there for nearly an hour.

Mai came along the corridor carrying exercise books. She stopped when she saw him.

“Dad said you might be here.”

“Did he send you?”

“No. I go to school here. It's a remarkable coincidence.”

He looked at the books in her arms. She had written her name on the uppermost cover in a hand less regular than her father's but more assertive.

“How were the examinations?”

“They haven't happened yet.”

“How were the preparations?”

“An excellent recovery.”

She sat beside him. Through the classroom wall, a man raised his voice and immediately apologized for raising it. Hòa told him he did not need to apologize.

Mai looked at the closed door.

“People are saying you broke someone's face.”

“I struck his chest.”

“That's the correction you want made?”

“He hit me with a pipe.”

She inspected the cut at his hairline.

“And that was a bad idea.”

“For him.”

For a moment she smiled. Then she touched the edge of her satchel, where the damaged catch had been tied shut with string.

“Did he know who you were?”

“I don't think so.”

“Everyone at school seems to.”

Tín looked at the courtyard. Two small children were trying to move a puddle with their feet while an older sister shouted that they were making it worse.

“What are they saying?”

“That you used to own the river.”

“Nobody owns a river.”

“Then you should tell the people saying it. They sound very certain.”

He turned toward her. She was not accusing him. She was trying to find out which parts of the man beside her she had been allowed to know.

“I owned boats,” he said. “Buildings. Loans people could not repay. Men worked for me.”

“And now?”

“Some of those things still belong to me.”

“Dad says we need to know which.”

“Your father is right.”

She nodded, then leaned back against the wall.

“He'll enjoy hearing that.”

Hòa opened the classroom door. He saw Mai beside his father and stopped.

Behind him, a crowded metal cabinet tipped as someone moved a chair against it. Hòa caught its upper corner without looking back. The cabinet stopped so completely that the loose cups on top barely moved. He set it against the wall and took his hand away.

Tín noticed the shallow impressions left in the metal beneath his son's fingers.

Hòa saw him notice. Neither spoke about it.

“Your mother is waiting at home.”

“She knows where I am.”

“Does she know you're still here?”

“She knows school is here.”

Tín lowered his eyes to hide his expression.

Hòa pointed at him without looking. “Don't encourage her.”

Mai stood. Before leaving she took the cup from beside her grandfather's foot and carried it to the tea tray.

Hòa watched her go, then sat on the bench.

“Three people spoke after you left,” he said.

“What did they say?”

“That they were afraid you would decide which debts were fair and send them back.”

“I'm not sending anyone back.”

“They don't know that.”

Tín rubbed the heel of his hand over his jaw. The skin there had begun to dry after the blood was washed away.

“I could make an announcement.”

“They've heard announcements.”

“Then what would you suggest?”

Hòa looked into the room. “Keep sitting here.”

Tín did.

By dusk the school had acquired names from seven landings. Some people came with papers. Others came because they had heard someone else's husband had been released. A woman arrived carrying her sleeping grandson and asked only whether anyone had seen the child's father.

Nobody had.

Thảo began a list of the missing, then stopped when one man asked who would be allowed to read it. She tore the page out of the book and started again on separate sheets.

“You decide whether your name is read aloud,” she told each person. “You decide whether we send it to the other crossings. We can still write down what happened without telling everyone who said it.”

Tín saw the old system beginning to show its shape through the gaps between their accounts. A collector visited one house at a time. He named a sum. He mentioned a child. People sold something quietly, then hid the sale from their neighbours. The private embarrassment did half the work.

He had once admired how few men collection required.

Thảo came outside with a stack of sheets and sat at the other end of the bench.

“I want to read some of the names at the crossing,” she said.

“Do it.”

“I'm telling you, not asking.”

“Then do it without my permission.”

She gave him a tired, hostile look.

“You find that funny.”

“Only the part where you think I can stop you.”

“You can stop anyone here.”

He did not answer immediately.

The last light had reached the upper branches of the trees. Beyond the school wall, a motorcycle slowed. The engine remained running while someone looked through the gate.

Tín saw a young man in a dark shirt, one foot on the ground. He recognized the fourth collector from the ticket office, the one who had run.

He rose and crossed the courtyard.

The collector tried to turn the motorcycle. Tín reached its rear wheel before he could accelerate and lifted it off the ground. The engine howled. Mud spat from the turning tyre without moving the machine an inch.

The young man looked back over his shoulder.

“Shut it off,” Tín said.

He did.

Tín lowered the wheel.

“What do you want?”

“A message.”

“From Phúc?”

The young man nodded.

“Then get off and deliver it.”

He climbed down. Without the engine and the motorcycle beneath him, he looked younger than he had that morning. Twenty, perhaps. Tín noticed a patch on his sleeve where the cloth had been mended with thread of the wrong colour.

“He says people who interfere with contracted work will be responsible for the losses.”

“Tell him to write down the losses.”

The young man blinked.

“And tell him I'm responsible for what I do. He can try it himself.”

“He said you'd understand.”

“I understood the first time. You're free to go.”

The collector looked through the gate at the people inside.

Tín stepped into his line of sight.

“Deliver messages here,” he said. “Don't collect names.”

The young man put on his helmet and rode away. Tín watched until he turned onto the main road.

When he returned to the bench, Thảo had not moved.

“That's what I mean,” she said.

“About what?”

“You can lift a man and his motorcycle. My brother can't. Neither can the people in that room.”

“I know.”

“Then stop talking as if telling Phúc to behave settles anything.”

He sat down slowly. Inside, Hòa was asking someone to spell a place name.

“What do you need?” Tín asked.

Thảo stared at him for a moment, as if considering whether the question was genuine.

“People at the other crossings who'll tell us when the repair crews arrive. Boats we can use without asking the men who hold the debts. Somewhere the families can wait if the collectors come at night.”

“The side rooms are empty,” Hòa said from the doorway.

“The collectors know that too.”

“Then the families shouldn't come alone.”

Thảo considered the school wall and the open gate.

“There are women at the new landing who know everyone. If they agree, they can carry messages. Nobody notices someone buying fish until she starts asking the wrong questions.”

“Ask them what questions are safe,” Tín said.

She looked at him again. This time the anger eased slightly.

“I will.”

He went home after dark.

Bích had kept food warm. Hương sat at the table marking a tear in one of Mai's blouses with pins. She looked up when Tín entered, then looked at the fresh cut on his head and returned to the cloth.

“Hòa is still at the school?” she asked.

“He'll be back soon.”

“He said that an hour ago.”

“People came.”

“People usually do.”

Bích put a bowl in front of him. He took the spoon. His hands, steady under the weight of engines and men, hesitated over the small ordinary meal.

His wife sat opposite him.

“Don't make a ceremony of eating,” she said. “I have to wash that bowl.”

He began.

Mai came downstairs with a notebook and placed it beside him.

“For the satchel catch,” she said. “If you're going to keep forgetting.”

He opened the notebook. On the first page she had written three tasks: repair chair, repair catch, come home.

Tín looked at the list for a long time.

“Your handwriting is poor,” he said.

“You'll survive it.”

Bích reached across the table and turned the page toward herself. She read it, closed the notebook, and placed it back beside his bowl.

“Keep that somewhere you can find it,” she said.


## 4. Rope of Sand

The woman who agreed to take them upriver made Tín remove his shoes before entering her boat.

“Mud,” she said, pointing at the soles.

He looked at the river, the muddy bank, and the boat resting between them.

“You're floating in it.”

“You're walking it onto my floor. Different business.”

Her name was Ngân. She carried vegetables, schoolchildren, sacks of feed, and passengers whose destinations did not justify a larger vessel. The boat was narrow, with a patched canopy and an engine she appeared to dislike personally. She had known Thảo for years and Tín for much longer, though knowing him had once meant paying a man who spoke on his behalf.

He took off his shoes.

“Thank you,” she said, and pushed away from the bank.

Thảo sat near the bow with the names they had permission to ask about. Tín settled behind her. Ngân steered from the stern, lifting the engine slightly whenever they crossed a shallow patch. She did it without looking at the water ahead, guided by things Tín could see only after she had acted: a change in the colour of the current, a line of drifting leaves, the angle of a branch over the bank.

He had once known this stretch as thoroughly. He found that the river had not preserved the exact arrangement for his convenience.

They passed the short channel controlled by Chấn. Two iron lighters lay across its mouth. A man on a raised platform watched them without waving.

“How long has that been there?” Tín asked.

“The boats or the charge?” Ngân said.

“Both.”

“Boats, four months. Charge, long enough that my youngest thinks it comes with the weather.”

“Can you go around?”

“In this?” She tapped the gunwale with her heel. “On a fine day. With no cargo. And a passenger who doesn't shit himself when a larger boat passes.”

Thảo smiled for the first time that morning.

Ngân glanced at Tín.

“You'll be all right. You're probably too stubborn.”

The first landing stood beside a small rice mill. Its wheelhouse windows were open, and grain dust covered the leaves of a tree that grew close to the wall. A woman met them before they tied up. She had been waiting for Thảo rather than for Tín, and her expression changed when she saw who had come with her.

“He's listening,” Thảo said. “You don't have to tell him anything you don't want to.”

The woman considered that and then considered the boat. Finally she led them up the path.

Her husband had been taken to repair a conveyor. He came home every third evening, bringing no money and an explanation that changed according to whichever collector accompanied him. She had stopped asking in front of their children.

“Does he know Lâm?” Thảo asked.

“He thinks so. A man with a scar on one thumb.”

Thảo looked down at her own hand. Her brother had cut himself opening a tin when they were children. Tín remembered the description she had given him at the school and saw the same detail connect inside her.

“Where did they work together?”

“Not here. Another yard. He said there was a long roof and a ditch that smelled bad.”

There were long roofs and bad ditches at nearly every yard along the branch. Tín asked about the machines instead. What had her husband repaired? Which parts had he brought home to clean? Did he come back with sawdust on his clothes, grain dust, oil?

“Red dirt,” she said. “And something black that won't wash out.”

“Coal?”

“He said old grease.”

Tín asked whether the husband was home now. She shook her head.

“Then ask him what the machine does,” he said. “He doesn't need to know where he is if he can describe what he's working on.”

The woman looked at him for a long time.

“They used to come asking how much we had. You're the first one asking what he knows.”

He left money for her lost morning. She pushed it back across the table.

“I didn't sell you anything.”

Tín put it away.

At the next landing they found a yard full of roofing sheets and timber. The owner had borrowed to expand after a good season. The expansion stood half-finished behind him, its posts rising above an empty concrete floor. Two collectors sat in the shade while workers loaded materials onto a barge.

The owner stood beside a stack of steel sheets. He watched each one being carried away as if trying to remember where it had been placed.

“They say I missed a payment,” he told Thảo.

“Did you?”

“I paid the man who came.”

“Which man?”

He pointed at one of the collectors.

The collector stood when Tín came up the landing. He was older than the men at the ticket office and less interested in demonstrating that he was brave. He wiped his hands on his trousers.

“There is a dispute,” he said.

“I can hear it,” Tín answered.

“The owner is in default.”

“Then he still owns the material while you discuss it.”

“It was pledged.”

“Was the man carrying it pledged too?”

A worker had paused beneath a heavy sheet. Its sharp edge rested against the cloth pad on his shoulder. Sweat ran down his face while the collector decided whether he was allowed to lower it.

Tín took the sheet from him and set it aside.

“Go and drink some water.”

The worker looked at the owner, received a nod, and went.

The collector held out a paper. Tín did not take it.

“I paid you,” the owner said. His voice had risen. “You came on a Tuesday. My wife counted it twice.”

“You paid against the previous month.”

“You said it was settled.”

“I said that payment was settled.”

Tín looked at the loading rig above the barge. A bundle of timber hung from its cable. The boom had been positioned farther over the water than its base could safely support. Someone had piled spare material against the rear legs to keep them down.

He could see the arrangement of forces in it without calculation. A poor solution waiting for someone to alter one part.

“Stop loading,” he said.

The second collector, still in the shade, gave the winch operator a small signal.

The cable began to move.

Tín turned toward him.

The first collector stepped into his path. “You don't run this yard.”

The bundle cleared the stack beneath it. One of the men loading the barge pulled it sideways with a rope. The rear leg of the rig lifted out of the mud.

Tín moved.

He crossed the yard in three strides, vaulted a pile of steel sheets, and reached the landing as the boom began to fall. The suspended timber swung toward the workers on the barge. Its lower edge caught one man across the shoulder and knocked him into the water.

Tín jumped after him.

He caught the falling bundle instead.

His hands closed around the binding strap while his feet struck the barge's deck. The vessel rolled violently beneath him. Timber, cable, and boom dragged toward the far side, trying to carry him through the deck and into the river.

He bent his knees and pulled the load against his chest.

The binding strap tore into his palms. He changed his grip before it broke, drove his fingers between two timbers, and lifted. The bundle rose above the gunwale. He threw it toward the empty concrete floor of the unfinished extension.

It crossed the landing in a flat, impossible arc and hit the concrete with a report that silenced the entire yard. The cable snapped. The boom followed the released load and collapsed into the stack behind it.

Tín was already in the water.

The worker had gone beneath the barge. Tín found him by the trail of bubbles moving along the hull, caught his shirt, and pulled him clear. The man fought him blindly. Tín let him strike his chest until air reached his mouth and the fighting became coughing.

Ngân had brought her boat alongside. Thảo leaned over the gunwale.

“Here!” she shouted.

Tín lifted the worker into the boat. His wet shirt weighed less than the panic in his hands. He gripped the canopy support and held it while Ngân swore at him to lie down before he overturned them all.

Tín climbed onto the landing.

The collector who had blocked him was running toward a motorcycle.

Tín picked up the broken end of the loading cable and swung it once. The steel line wrapped around the motorcycle's rear frame. He pulled. The machine slid backward across the mud and struck a timber post. The collector fell over it and landed on his hands.

Tín walked toward him.

The man rolled onto his back and reached inside his shirt.

“Don't,” Tín said.

He reached anyway.

Tín stepped on his forearm. Bone broke beneath his heel. The man's scream came out before the knife cleared the cloth.

The second collector had not moved from the shade.

Tín looked at him.

“Come here.”

The man obeyed.

“You told them to keep loading.”

“I didn't know it would fall.”

“You knew I told them to stop.”

The collector's eyes moved toward the river. Tín took him by the collar and turned his face back.

“Help the owner unload the barge. Return everything you took today. Then take your friend somewhere his arm can be treated.”

“Phúc—”

“Has enough men to break one more arm himself. Yours will be easier if I start it.”

The man swallowed.

Tín released him.

They stayed until the material was back in the yard. The owner found a chair for the injured worker. His wife brought dry clothes. A boy was sent for someone who could examine the man's shoulder.

Tín sat beneath the unfinished roof while Thảo wrapped his palms with strips torn from a clean cloth. Small cuts crossed the skin where the steel binding had bitten him. They were already closing, slowly enough for a witness to believe in blood and quickly enough to make that belief uncomfortable.

Thảo noticed. She said nothing until the second hand was bound.

“Does anything hurt you properly?”

“Your questions are making an effort.”

She tied the knot harder than necessary.

“Good.”

The owner came with the disputed paper. He laid it on the table but did not ask Tín to read it.

“Will they come back?”

“Probably.”

The man's face fell.

“Ask the woman at the mill to come here when your workers finish,” Thảo said. “Compare what they've been charging you. If someone returns, send word to the crossing.”

“What good will that do?”

“It will mean someone else knows before you lose half the yard.”

Tín listened while they arranged a way to send messages. A boy who delivered ice could stop at both landings. Ngân passed twice a day. The mill owner's wife had a cousin near the school. None of it required Tín to remain beneath the unfinished roof for the rest of his life.

When they returned to the boat, Ngân looked at his bandaged hands.

“And people complain my fares are expensive.”

He sat down carefully.

“You haven't told me your fare.”

“I'm still deciding.”

They continued toward the upper cut. The banks narrowed, then opened around a bend where new earth had been piled above the waterline. A dredger stood farther ahead, its boom lowered across a work platform. Men in pale shirts moved along the bank carrying survey poles.

Dưỡng stood among them.

He raised a hand when he saw Tín. Ngân slowed the engine without being told.

“Come and inspect your suspicions,” Dưỡng called.

Tín climbed onto the bank. The new soil shifted beneath his feet. He crouched and took a handful, rubbing it between his fingers. Fine material mixed with larger clods. It had been moved recently from somewhere deeper and wetter.

“You're narrowing the channel,” he said.

“Strengthening the bank.”

“Those can both be true.”

Dưỡng looked at the water.

“The outer branch is taking more traffic. The smaller boats need a protected route.”

“They already have one.”

“They have a ditch with collapsing sides and men who argue over who should repair it.”

“And when you've finished?”

“A channel that stays open.”

“At Chấn's price.”

Dưỡng did not answer immediately.

Behind him a labourer tried to lever a concrete block into place. It slipped off the timber beneath it. Dưỡng turned, caught the block with one hand, and set it where the man had been trying to move it. The worker stepped back without thanking him, as if this were an ordinary service performed by an extraordinary machine.

“A maintained route costs money,” Dưỡng said.

“So does a blocked one.”

They stood facing each other while the dredger's engine thudded across the water.

Tín looked beyond Dưỡng at the new bank. Its shape made sense if the purpose was repair. It made a different kind of sense if the purpose was to make the old route inconvenient enough that every boat accepted a new gate.

“Whose land did you buy?” he asked.

“The owners sold.”

“Did they want to?”

Dưỡng smiled without warmth.

“You should have taken up gardening. Fewer questions with unpleasant answers.”

Tín returned to the boat.

As they pulled away, Thảo looked back at the men working along the bank.

“Is that where Lâm might be?”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

“Wrong machines. Wrong soil.”

She looked at him sharply.

He watched the dredger recede behind the trees.

“But the man who knows where he is has been standing in front of us.”


## 5. The Price of Quiet

Lan found him at home after sunset.

Tín sat beneath the house with his hands unwrapped, letting the cuts dry. Bích had put the damaged chair upright to discourage him from beginning another repair. It stood at a slight angle. Nobody trusted it enough to move the basket of mending back onto the seat.

Lan paused at the gate.

“Your wife is going to tell me this is a bad hour.”

“She'll be right.”

“Can I come in?”

“You can try.”

Bích appeared in the doorway before Lan reached the table. She looked at the visitor, then at Tín.

“If this is business, take it outside.”

“We are outside,” he said.

“Farther.”

Lan pressed her lips together. Tín stood and led her to the path beyond the garden.

The frogs had started in the ditch. Across the road, someone was repairing a radio, producing a sequence of voices that vanished almost as soon as they became intelligible.

“Phúc will meet you tonight,” Lan said.

“He can meet me tomorrow morning. I'll be less tired.”

“He says he has information about the repair crews.”

“Does he?”

“Some.”

“And you?”

She looked toward the house. Bích had gone back inside, but the door remained open.

“I found three sites. Lâm isn't at any of them.”

“Dưỡng's crews.”

Lan's expression did not change. That was enough.

“Who moves them?” Tín asked.

“Different foremen. The arrangements overlap.”

“You make it sound like bad handwriting.”

“Would you prefer me to make it sound like a speech? It will still be the same arrangement when I'm finished.”

He considered her in the weak light from the house. She had spent years listening to men explain that the ugliness in a calculation came from whoever did the arithmetic. He could hear how carefully she defended the edge between responsibility and employment.

“Where tonight?”

“The hall behind the fuel market.”

Tín knew the building. It had once stored sacks of grain. Later, men had used it for card games and fights. He had closed it twice during arguments between owners, then collected a share when it reopened.

“He's making an evening of it.”

“He wants witnesses.”

“Then he'll have to live with what they see.”

Lan nodded toward his hands.

“Will those matter?”

He flexed his fingers. The skin tightened, but the bones beneath it felt sound.

“Only if someone asks me to sew.”

The hall smelled of sweat, tobacco, and old oil carried in on the clothes of men from the fuel market. Electric bulbs hung above a square of cleared floor. Benches rose along three sides. At the fourth stood tables where men took bets with pencils tucked behind their ears.

Tín entered beside Lan.

Conversations stopped in patches. He felt recognition move through the room faster than any spoken account of his arrival. Men straightened. Some looked away. A few younger faces remained curious until an older companion touched an elbow or muttered a name.

Sơn stood near the far wall with a glass of tea in his hand.

Tín looked at him.

Sơn lifted the glass slightly, acknowledging that he had come without being asked.

Phúc sat at a table above the cleared floor.

He was broad, with a heavy neck and the composed face of a man who had discovered early that anger could be made useful. At thirty-eight he had his father's eyes. He wore a dark shirt, open at the throat, and a watch large enough to announce itself across a room.

He rose when Tín approached.

“Uncle.”

“Phúc.”

The younger man held out a hand. Tín took it.

Pressure built inside the grip.

Around them, the nearest men became very still. Phúc increased the force slowly, giving himself time to look as though nothing unusual was happening.

Tín watched his face.

The table beside them trembled as Phúc unconsciously braced against it with his other hand. The wood beneath that palm began to crack.

Tín closed his fingers a little farther.

Phúc's breath stopped.

“Sit down,” Tín said.

He released the hand. Phúc lowered himself into the chair with enough care that everyone close enough to notice understood what had happened.

Tín sat opposite him.

“You hurt my collectors.”

“They were collecting badly.”

“Khang may never use that hand properly again.”

“Then buy him a watch for the other one.”

Lan pulled out a chair at the side of the table. She gave Tín a look that suggested she had hoped for at least thirty seconds before somebody became unnecessarily cruel.

Phúc leaned back.

“You always were generous with advice.”

“Where is Lâm?”

“Working.”

“For whom?”

“A contractor.”

“Which contractor?”

“If I knew every man on every crew, I wouldn't have time to sit here being insulted.”

Below them a bell rang. Two fighters entered the square. One was thick through the chest, with a flattened nose and scar tissue along his eyebrows. The other looked barely old enough to have stopped growing. Both wore shorts. Neither wore the expression of a man enjoying the attention.

The older fighter bowed his head briefly toward Phúc's table.

Tín watched the younger one's feet. He favoured the right leg. There was an old bruise just above the knee and a newer mark on the ribs.

“I asked you here because we need terms,” Phúc said.

“Bring the workers home.”

“You keep saying that as if it doesn't leave other men owing money.”

“Tell me who paid you for Lâm's labour.”

“Tell me why you care about him.”

Tín turned back from the square.

“His sister asked.”

Phúc looked at him for a long moment.

“My father asked you for help.”

The first blow landed below them. The younger fighter retreated and tried to circle away from the older man's right hand.

“I remember,” Tín said.

“You told him to settle his own disputes.”

“He had started that one.”

“He died.”

“I know.”

Phúc placed both forearms on the table. The hand Tín had gripped lay partly closed.

“You know everything after it's finished.”

Tín let the accusation stand. Phúc's father had made a promise he could not honour, taken money from dangerous men, and come to Tín asking him to frighten them into accepting less. Tín had refused. Later the man had been killed on a road outside town. Tín had never learned which participant in the dispute ordered it.

He had paid for the funeral.

At the time he had thought that was a separate matter.

“Your father owed men who knew what they were lending,” he said. “You're taking people who never agreed to owe you anything.”

“Their families agreed.”

“Their dead fathers seem especially accommodating.”

Phúc glanced at Lan. She looked away from him first, deliberately.

Below, the younger fighter took a blow to the stomach and dropped to one knee. The older man stepped back. Someone at the betting table shouted for the fight to continue.

The younger fighter tried to stand. His right leg folded.

Tín looked at the official beside the square.

“Stop it.”

The official looked at Phúc.

Phúc kept his eyes on Tín.

“He owes for training.”

Tín rose.

The older fighter approached the kneeling man again. Tín stepped down from the platform and entered the square before he could strike.

“Finished,” he said.

The fighter stopped.

“You don't decide,” someone shouted from the benches.

Tín looked toward the voice. The speaker became very interested in the space between his own feet.

The younger fighter was trying to rise because nobody had told him he could stay down. Tín put a hand on his shoulder.

“Sit.”

The older fighter was named Hậu. Tín had heard of him. Men said he could lift the rear of a truck to let a wheel be changed. That was the sort of story men told until they needed a truck lifted and discovered how precisely the teller had arranged the circumstances.

Hậu looked at the table above them.

Phúc nodded.

The blow came from close range, heavy and technically sound. Hậu drove it toward Tín's exposed jaw, putting his weight behind it before the older man could turn.

Tín caught the wrist.

Hậu tried to pull back. Tín stepped closer and turned the arm until the larger man's shoulder rose higher than his head.

“You were winning,” Tín said. “Take the fucking victory.”

Hậu struck with the other hand.

Tín released him and moved inside the blow. His palm met Hậu's chest. There was no theatrical windup, no shout. The impact drove air out of the fighter and lifted him clear of the floor. He crossed the square and struck the wooden barrier hard enough to break it between two posts.

Men scrambled out of the way.

Hậu lay among the broken boards, trying to draw a breath that would not come.

Tín turned toward the platform.

Three men had stood. One carried a short club. Another had a knife. The third reached toward the waistband beneath his shirt.

Sơn moved beside that third man before his hand came free. He put the glass of tea on the table, took the man's elbow, and drove it backward across the chair rail.

“You're going to spill something,” Sơn said.

The man's weapon fell beneath the table.

The other two came down into the square.

Tín pushed the injured younger fighter toward the official.

“Get him out.”

The club struck his forearm. He closed his hand around it, pulled its owner forward, and drove a knee into the man's abdomen. The knife came toward his side. Tín turned the first man out of its path rather than using him as a shield, caught the knife wrist, and broke it against his own elbow.

The scream silenced the nearest benches.

He took the knife and drove it through the club, pinning the wood to the floor.

The two men stared at it.

“Leave it there,” he said.

Phúc had not moved from his chair.

Tín climbed back to the table. Sơn released the third man's arm and stepped aside. The man collapsed against the wall, clutching his elbow.

Tín put one hand on the table.

“You wanted witnesses.”

Phúc looked at the wrecked barrier, the injured men, the audience waiting to discover whether it was permitted to leave.

“You think this makes them yours again?”

“It makes them very aware of how much trouble you create for people standing near you.”

He bent toward Phúc. The younger man did not retreat, but his breathing changed.

“You will give Lan the names of every foreman who takes workers from your office. She will give them to me tomorrow. You will stop moving men until their families know where they are.”

“And if I don't?”

Tín glanced at the younger man's right hand.

“You'll find out how many things you can do with the left.”

For several seconds Phúc seemed ready to stand. Tín felt the tension moving through his shoulders. It would have been a formidable movement against almost anyone else in the room.

Then Phúc looked at Lan.

“Bring him the names.”

Tín straightened.

“And pay the boy.”

“He lost.”

“So did your men. Are you keeping their wages too?”

Lan opened her handbag and put money on the table before Phúc could answer. Tín looked at it, then at her.

“His money,” she said. “I'll explain the expense.”

Sơn took the payment to the injured fighter. Tín waited until he saw it placed in the young man's hand.

Outside, the fuel market had nearly closed. The air felt cool after the hall. Tín stopped beside a stack of empty crates and flexed his hands. One cut had opened again.

Sơn joined him.

“You could have carried him out by the throat.”

“Yes.”

“Why didn't you?”

Tín watched men leave the hall in small groups. They were speaking rapidly, correcting one another's accounts of what had happened. By morning half the river would know Phúc had been made to sit in his own chair.

“If I kill him, Dưỡng inherits the crews and Chấn inherits the collections. If I drag him home, the foremen move everyone before we find them.”

“And now?”

“Now some of his men will wonder whether they should give us an answer before I come to ask.”

Sơn looked at the door.

“And some will do something stupid to prove they aren't afraid.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Tín turned toward him. Sơn held his gaze.

“You used to have men watching the roads when you did this,” Sơn said. “You used to know which families needed moving before somebody took offence.”

Tín looked back toward the distant light of the schoolhouse.

“Then we start there.”

They walked together through the market. Behind them, a man inside the hall began clearing broken wood from the square. Another shouted that nobody was to leave without settling what he owed.

Sơn stopped.

Tín touched his arm.

“Tomorrow,” he said. “Tonight we make sure everyone gets home.”


## 6. Men Who Followed

The schoolhouse was still lit when they reached it.

Hòa had persuaded the last visitors to leave in groups. Two families remained in a side room because the collectors knew where they lived and the adults were afraid to take the children home after dark. The caretaker had brought mats. Thảo sat at a desk sorting the names people had agreed could be shared.

She looked up when Tín entered with Sơn.

“Did you find him?”

“We will have names of the foremen tomorrow.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

“No.”

She put down the pencil.

“Then don't say it like an answer.”

Sơn went to the window. He studied the road while Hòa came out of the side room carrying an empty water jug.

“People heard about the hall already,” Hòa said.

“What did they hear?”

“The usual. That you broke six men in half and ate the money.”

“Only four men. I don't eat after your mother has washed the bowls.”

Hòa did not smile.

Tín touched his son's arm.

“Sơn will stay. I want the families together until morning.”

“What are you expecting?”

“Someone proving his loyalty to Phúc.”

Sơn turned from the window.

“You go home,” he told Hòa. “Your mother should hear it from somebody who isn't trying to make the story larger.”

Hòa looked between them. He had grown up seeing the change that occurred when men who joked at his father's table began speaking in short practical sentences. As a child he had thought the change meant the adults understood everything. At forty-three he knew it meant they had stopped wasting time pretending.

“I'll bring more water first,” he said.

Tín went outside.

The air carried a faint smell of smoke. He stopped at the gate, lifted his head, and waited for the next movement of wind.

Smoke was common after sunset. People burned rubbish, cooked, cleared weeds. This was sharper, oil beneath dry timber. He looked toward the old engine yard.

A red light moved behind the trees.

“Sơn.”

The older man was beside him before he called again.

They watched the glow brighten.

“The yard,” Sơn said.

“Keep them here.”

“Tín—”

He was already moving.

The path followed the channel for a quarter of its length, then turned inland. Tín ignored the turn. He ran along the bank, crossed a narrow drainage gap in one stride, and took a larger channel in a jump that carried him over the roof of a moored boat.

The boatman looked up in time to see a man pass between him and the moon.

Tín landed on the opposite bank without slowing.

The old yard stood behind a row of houses. Workers still lived in two rooms attached to its warehouse. Tín had taken rent from those rooms for so long that the payments had become figures in a household account. Now he could see flames climbing the wall beneath their windows.

A motorcycle lay on its side in the road. Beyond it, two men were fighting beside a small truck. One held a can. The other was trying to take it from him.

Tín hit the truck with his shoulder as he passed.

The vehicle moved sideways across the road and struck a post. The two men fell apart. One ran. Tín caught the man with the can and threw him into the open back of the truck hard enough to fold the side panel beneath him.

“Stay,” he said.

The man stayed.

Tín crossed the yard.

Someone was trapped behind the first door. He could hear a woman coughing and a child calling for a man who was not answering. The wooden frame had swollen under the heat. He put his hand against it and pushed.

The door and most of the wall around it went inward.

He stepped through the dust and smoke, found the woman by the sound of her breathing, and placed her outside. The child clung to her neck. A man lay farther inside with one leg beneath a fallen cabinet.

Tín lifted the cabinet and carried the man out beneath his arm.

The second room had no voice coming from it.

He broke through the dividing wall.

Heat struck him across the face. A mattress burned near the window. Beneath a table, two children lay close together with cloth pressed over their mouths. An older woman crouched beside them, holding a pan as if it might still be used to fight the fire.

Tín took the table by its edge and tore the legs away. He laid the top on the floor and moved the children onto it. The woman tried to help, then swayed. He caught her before her head struck the wall.

Outside, people had formed a line from the water to the yard. Buckets passed hand to hand. Their contents vanished against the burning wall with very little effect.

“Clear the doorway,” Tín called.

He lifted the table top with the children on it and held the woman against his chest with the other arm. The room's ceiling lowered as a beam above them began to give.

He stepped through the wall he had broken.

Burning material struck his back. He kept the children level. A man outside took the woman. Another reached for the table.

“All of it,” Tín said. “Don't lift one corner.”

They understood and took the makeshift stretcher together.

A worker was shouting that someone remained in the loft.

Tín looked up. The upper window was a dark rectangle above the flames. He saw a hand against its lower edge.

There was no ladder.

He bent, drove both feet into the ground, and rose past the burning wall. His hand caught the window frame. He pulled himself inside as the timber tore away beneath his weight.

A man lay on the floor with his head turned toward the air. Tín took him over one shoulder. The roof above them had begun making small, rapid noises as its fastenings failed.

He went back through the window.

For a moment he hung above the yard with the man across his shoulder and fire beneath him. Then he landed beyond the line of people, knees bending deeply enough to keep the man from being broken by the impact.

Behind him the loft collapsed.

The sound silenced everyone for half a second. Then the bucket line began moving again.

Tín set the rescued man down beside the others. A woman who worked at the clinic pushed through the crowd and knelt. She gave instructions without asking who owned the yard or who had brought the injured out.

He looked toward the truck.

The man he had thrown into it was gone.

Blood marked the bent side panel. A trail of drops led toward the road. Tín followed it far enough to see one clear footprint in the mud, then stopped.

The smoke was moving toward the school.

Behind its bitter smell came the sound of two shots.

He turned and ran.

This time he took the drainage gaps without measuring them. The bank passed beneath him in dark sections. He heard a third shot, then shouting, then an engine revving hard enough to lose its rhythm.

At the school gate, a motorcycle had struck the wall. The collector in the patched shirt lay beside it, one leg trapped beneath the frame. Tín recognized him even with blood across his mouth.

Sơn crouched over him.

A man farther down the road raised a pistol toward the gate. Tín picked up the fallen motorcycle by its front fork and rear frame. He lifted it above his head and threw.

The machine struck the road in front of the gunman, bounced, and carried him into the ditch beneath its turning metal. The pistol fired once into the ground.

Tín reached him before the echo faded.

The man tried to lift the weapon again. Tín struck his wrist against the road, took the pistol, and hit him once beneath the ear with its grip.

When he turned back, Sơn was sitting against the school wall.

Thảo knelt beside him. Her hands were pressed against his lower chest. Blood moved between her fingers faster than she could stop it.

Tín crossed the road.

“Where else?” he asked.

“Back,” Sơn said.

His voice sounded annoyed, as if the wound had interrupted a sentence he intended to finish.

Tín lifted him carefully. Sơn made one sharp sound and then clenched his jaw. They carried him into the nearest classroom and laid him on a desk pushed against another to give him enough room.

Hòa had blood on his sleeve. Tín looked at it.

“Not mine,” his son said.

“The families?”

“Inside. One man cut his hand on the window. Nobody else.”

Sơn looked toward the door.

“The boy.”

“Outside,” Thảo said.

“Bring him in.”

She hesitated. Sơn tried to rise, failed, and looked at Tín.

“His leg's trapped.”

Tín went back to the gate. The young collector had begun trying to drag himself away, leaving a wet line through the dirt. He stopped when Tín stood over him.

“Please.”

Tín lifted the bent frame off his leg and set it aside.

“If I wanted you dead,” he said, “you wouldn't need to ask whether it was happening.”

He carried the young man inside.

The woman from the clinic arrived with a bag and two men carrying a stretcher. She examined Sơn and sent one of them back for transport. Tín watched her face. She kept it professionally still, but her hands moved faster after she felt his pulse.

Sơn saw it too.

“Don't look like that,” he told her. “I haven't paid you yet.”

“Then stop talking and save your money.”

He gave Tín a faint smile.

The collector lay on the floor nearby. Hòa had put folded cloth beneath his injured leg. He stared at Sơn with the fixed bewilderment of someone whose enemy had interrupted the simple order in which he understood the world.

“He pushed me out of the way,” the young man said.

Tín looked at him.

“Who fired?”

“The man in the ditch.”

“Why?”

The young collector began to shake.

“I said there were children inside. He said to move. I didn't move fast enough.”

Sơn closed his eyes.

Tín sat beside the joined desks. The woman worked around him until she asked him to move. He moved. When she asked him to hold a cloth in place, he held it with exactly the pressure she requested.

He could have crushed the desks between his hands.

He held the cloth still.

Sơn opened his eyes again.

“You'll tell Liên?”

“You'll tell her.”

“Don't start lying badly now. You were always good at it.”

Tín looked at the blood darkening the cloth.

“What do you want me to say?”

“That I was useful. Leave out the clever parts. She'll know you invented them.”

“You were useful.”

“I know. That's why I said it.”

He tried to smile again. The effort exhausted him. He turned his face slightly toward the open window, where a little night air moved through the smell of smoke and blood.

The transport arrived before he died.

Tín carried the stretcher into the vehicle himself and went with him. He sat close enough to hear every breath. When the breaths began arriving farther apart, the woman from the clinic told the driver to hurry. The driver was already moving as fast as the road allowed.

Sơn died with one hand resting against Tín's wrist.

The hand did not become heavier. That was what Tín noticed. Nothing in its weight announced the difference.

He kept holding it until the vehicle stopped.

At dawn he went to Liên's house.

She opened the door before he knocked, saw who stood there, and put one hand against the frame.

“No,” she said.

Tín took off his shoes.

He went inside and told her.


## 7. The Strongest Man on the Bank

Liên listened without interrupting.

She sat at the kitchen table with her hands folded around an empty cup. When Tín finished, she asked where Sơn was. He told her. She asked who had been with him. He told her that too.

Only when she asked whether he had been frightened did Tín hesitate.

“He knew,” he said.

Liên nodded once.

Outside, someone began sweeping the path. The small, repetitive sound made the kitchen seem farther from the world than it was.

“He left his good shirt hanging behind the door,” she said.

Tín looked toward it.

“He said he was only going out to see what trouble you were making.”

“I know.”

“Of course you know. You were the trouble.”

He did not lower his eyes. She deserved to have him receive the words without performing how difficult they were to receive.

Liên rose. Her hands shook while she took the shirt from its hook. Tín stood to help and stopped when she looked at him.

“I can carry a shirt.”

He sat again.

She folded it badly, unfolded it, and tried once more. Then she put it on the table and pressed both palms against it.

“The boy he helped.”

“Alive.”

“Does he have a mother?”

“I haven't asked.”

“Find out. Someone should tell her where he is.”

He nodded.

When she finally began to cry, she did it without hiding her face. Tín stayed until her sister arrived. He left money on the table for the expenses that were already accumulating around the death. Liên looked at it and put it in a drawer. She had no intention of making poverty part of her loyalty to Sơn.

At home, Bích met him beneath the house.

She saw the burns across his back where the shirt had torn, the blood dried on his trousers, the handprint on one sleeve. For a moment she remained so still that he understood she was controlling several responses at once.

“Whose?” she asked, touching the blood on his sleeve.

“Sơn's.”

Her hand fell.

He told her.

She sat on the low stool beside the damaged chair. For once she did not notice the loose leg lying on the table.

Mai stood at the top of the stairs. Hương came behind her and put a hand on her shoulder. Hòa was still at the school, helping identify the men who had attacked it and finding transport for the injured.

“Come upstairs,” Bích said at last.

Tín followed her.

She cut away the shirt where it had stuck to his skin. He sat on a low stool while she washed his back. The water turned dark in the basin. Once, when she reached a deeper burn, his shoulders tightened.

“So you can feel that,” she said.

“Unfortunately.”

“I was beginning to wonder whether I'd spent forty years being careful for no reason.”

He looked at her over his shoulder.

She continued working.

“Don't make that face. I'm angry enough without you deciding it's tender.”

He faced forward again.

She wrapped clean cloth around him and brought another shirt. He had worn it only for visits and family occasions. She held it open while he put his arms through.

“You're not returning covered in somebody else's blood and telling me you didn't want to ruin the good one,” she said.

He buttoned it carefully.

The town had changed by the time he went back outside.

People stood in small groups near the school. The burned yard continued to smoke. At the clinic, relatives waited beneath an awning with bags of clothes and food they hoped the injured would be able to eat. A police vehicle stood beside the road. Two officers spoke to the caretaker while another examined the wall where bullets had struck.

An inspector introduced himself as Vũ.

He was younger than Hòa, lean, with a notebook held against his thigh. He looked at Tín's face and then at the clean shirt covering the fresh bandages.

“We need to speak.”

“Yes.”

“About last night.”

“And before it.”

Vũ waited, deciding whether this was a concession or a threat.

Tín gave him the names of the injured and told him where Sơn had died. He described the men at the yard, the truck, the gunman in the ditch. He did not claim to know who had ordered the attack.

“Everyone is saying Phúc,” Vũ said.

“Everyone wasn't in the room when someone gave the order.”

“Were you?”

“No.”

The inspector looked briefly relieved to receive a fact with an edge he could see.

“Then we'll start with what people did see.”

“The boy on the motorcycle. His name is Cường. He was with them.”

“He says the dead man saved him.”

“He did.”

Vũ looked toward the clinic.

“That may make him willing to talk.”

“Or ashamed enough to lie. Ask him.”

The inspector wrote something down. Tín watched the pencil move, then looked toward the river.

A launch had arrived at the old landing. Dưỡng stepped out beneath a dark umbrella held by one of his men.

He had come to pay his respects to Sơn's family.

Tín waited by the path until he reached him.

“Walk with me,” he said.

Dưỡng looked at Vũ, who had remained beside the school gate.

“A public invitation?”

“You like witnesses.”

They went along the bank, past the place where the motorcycle had struck the wall. Dưỡng studied the damage without slowing.

“Sơn shouldn't have been standing between armed men.”

“He was standing between an armed man and a boy.”

“The boy was one of them.”

“Sơn noticed.”

Dưỡng closed the umbrella himself and handed it back to the man following them. He made a small gesture, and the man stopped.

The two of them continued alone.

“You think I arranged this,” Dưỡng said.

“I think you know who profits when families leave their houses frightened.”

“People with houses to rent.”

“People with land to buy. Channels to narrow. Men to put on repair crews who can't refuse the wage.”

Dưỡng looked at him.

“There it is. A morning's grief and you have found a whole philosophy.”

Tín stopped walking.

“Don't use his death to sound clever.”

For the first time since they had met at the restaurant, Dưỡng's manner changed. The courteous expression remained, but something beneath it set hard.

“Then don't use it to accuse me without saying what you mean.”

“Where is Lâm?”

“Which one?”

Tín stepped closer.

Dưỡng's eyes narrowed.

“You didn't enjoy that answer at the ticket office,” he said.

“I won't enjoy it here.”

They stood on a section of bank strengthened with old timber and concrete. Below them, two men were repairing the side of a moored boat. Farther along, children crossed a narrow footbridge carrying baskets toward the market.

Dưỡng looked at the boat before looking back at Tín.

“There are a great many people near you today.”

Tín saw the warning and the test together.

“Send the foremen to the school,” he said. “All of them.”

“You don't command my men.”

“Then command them yourself.”

Dưỡng struck him.

The movement began so close that an ordinary witness would have seen only a small shift of the shoulder. His palm drove toward Tín's breastbone, carrying force through a short line from the ground. Tín turned his torso and caught the wrist before the strike landed squarely.

The remaining force passed into the concrete wall behind him.

A crack opened from its top to the water.

The men in the boat looked up.

Tín twisted Dưỡng's arm and drove him away from the edge. Dưỡng went with the movement, folded his body under the grip, and struck Tín's thigh with the side of his hand.

Pain flashed through the leg.

Tín stepped into it. He caught Dưỡng by the shoulder and threw him across the path.

The younger man struck a timber post. It broke at ground level. He rolled through the impact and came up with the broken length in his hand.

Tín advanced.

Dưỡng swung the post low, aiming for the injured leg. Tín jumped over it and struck him in the chest with both feet.

Dưỡng flew backward into the bank behind the path. Earth broke away around his shoulders. The sound was heavy enough to draw heads from the houses along the road.

Tín landed and walked toward him.

Dưỡng pushed himself out of the shallow crater. Blood ran from one corner of his mouth. He wiped it with his thumb, looked at it, and laughed without amusement.

“There you are.”

“You could have asked.”

“I needed to see.”

“Now you have.”

Dưỡng dropped the broken post.

Tín saw his attention move to the timber wall beside the water. He understood what was coming a fraction before the younger man's foot struck the path.

The force passed through the buried support beneath them.

Old wood split. The cracked concrete section leaned toward the river. The boat below lay directly beneath it, with both repairmen still staring upward.

Tín turned away from Dưỡng.

He reached the edge as the wall began to fall. One man jumped clear. The other slipped between the boat and the bank. Tín caught him by the belt, lifted him above the broken concrete, and threw him onto the path behind him.

Then he jumped into the boat.

The collapsing wall struck its outer side. Tín drove his shoulder against the inner gunwale and pushed the vessel away before the full weight landed. The boat shot across the narrow channel, struck the opposite mud bank, and stopped with its bow buried among roots.

Concrete and timber fell into the space it had occupied.

Water struck Tín hard enough to tear the new shirt at its seam. He went beneath it, found the channel bed, and pushed upward. His head broke the surface beyond the falling debris.

On the bank, Dưỡng had picked up his umbrella.

Tín climbed out of the water.

The man he had thrown onto the path sat with both hands planted behind him, unable to understand why he was no longer beneath the wall. His companion had reached him and was shouting questions into his face.

Dưỡng's attendant moved between his employer and the approaching old man.

Tín looked at him once.

The attendant stepped aside.

Dưỡng remained where he was, blood drying at his mouth.

“You always could move more weight,” he said.

“And you always needed someone else standing where it would fall.”

Tín took him by the throat.

He did not squeeze immediately. He lifted until Dưỡng's heels no longer touched the path, then held him there at arm's length.

The younger man's hands closed around his wrist. Muscles rose along his neck. His feet moved once, searching for ground that remained below them.

Tín carried him to the broken edge of the bank.

“Look.”

Dưỡng's eyes moved toward the two repairmen.

“You knew they were there.”

The younger man could not answer.

Tín lowered him onto the path and released his throat.

Dưỡng bent forward, drew a harsh breath, and straightened slowly. For several seconds the only sound from him was breathing.

“Send the foremen,” Tín said. “Tomorrow morning.”

He turned toward the men beside the damaged boat.

Behind him, Dưỡng said, “You can't keep making the river small enough to fit in your hand.”

Tín did not turn back.

He helped the repairmen inspect their hull. One plank had split where the wall struck it, but the boat could be moved once the water inside was removed. They began bailing with a bucket and a cut-open container.

Tín climbed into the mud beside the bow. He set both hands beneath it and lifted until the roots released their hold. Then he lowered the boat into clear water while the men steadied it.

By the time he climbed back onto the path, Dưỡng had gone.

Inspector Vũ waited near the school gate.

He looked at the torn shirt, the wet trousers, the line of mud across Tín's chest.

“Was that a conversation?”

“Parts of it.”

“People saw you lift him by the throat.”

“Ask the men in the boat what happened before that.”

“I intend to.”

Tín nodded.

Vũ remained in his path.

“And then I'm asking you.”

For a moment the old man felt the familiar irritation of being delayed by someone who could not physically delay him.

Then he looked at the notebook in the inspector's hand.

“I'll be at Liên's,” he said. “Sơn's family is waiting.”

“Afterward.”

“Afterward.”

He went home first to change the shirt.

Bích took one look at it and closed her eyes.

“I liked that one,” she said.

Tín stood in the doorway, wet and silent.

His wife opened her eyes again. Whatever she saw in his face stopped the next sentence.

“There's another in the cupboard,” she said. “Go on.”


## 8. A School for Liars

Inspector Vũ chose the smallest classroom for their conversation.

There was one window, one door, and a low desk scarred by generations of children who had discovered that writing on furniture was more satisfying than writing in a book. Tín sat with his knees on either side of it. Vũ opened a notebook.

“You can use the larger room,” Tín said.

“People keep walking through it.”

“They'll walk through this one if they need you.”

“Then we'll hear them coming.”

The inspector began with the ticket office. Tín described the pipe, the gun, the knife. He described what he had done to each man. Vũ asked him to repeat the part about bending the pistol.

Tín repeated it.

“With your hand.”

“There was only one available.”

Vũ looked up.

Tín's expression did not change.

The inspector continued.

They spoke about the fight hall, the burning yard, the school attack, and the damaged bank. Vũ had already spoken to the repairmen. Their description of the boat's movement differed in detail but agreed on the important point: Dưỡng had struck the ground, the wall had collapsed toward them, and Tín had moved the boat before it could be crushed.

“One says you threw it,” Vũ said.

“I pushed it.”

“He was inside.”

“Then he had other things to watch.”

Vũ closed the notebook for a moment.

“People will tell a great many stories about you. I need to know which ones you are willing to say happened.”

“Ask.”

“Were you collecting debts here before you retired?”

“Yes.”

“Did men use violence for you?”

“Yes.”

“Did you tell them to?”

Tín looked at the scratched desk between them. Someone had cut a small boat into the surface with the point of a compass. Its sail was much too large for the hull.

“Sometimes,” he said. “Sometimes I gave an order and didn't ask what they needed to do to carry it out.”

Vũ opened the notebook again.

“Those are different statements.”

“Write both.”

They remained in the room until someone knocked to say that Lan had arrived with four foremen.

Tín stood. Vũ did not.

“We aren't finished.”

“No.”

“I don't want you confusing cooperation with an agreement that only other people will be questioned.”

Tín looked down at him.

There were men who delivered such sentences because they knew someone stronger stood behind them. Vũ appeared to have looked for the stronger man, found none, and decided to deliver it anyway.

“Then keep asking me questions,” Tín said.

The foremen waited in the larger room with Lan standing beside the window. Three wore work clothes. The fourth had clean cuffs and a watch he kept checking without reading.

Thảo had arranged chairs again. Hòa stood near the desk with blank paper. Two of the released workers had come back, one bringing the wife who had been afraid to speak in front of Tín.

Tín sat at the edge of the group.

“Tell us where your crews are working,” he said.

The first foreman described the mill by the northern landing. The second supervised a repair yard where old engines were stripped and rebuilt. The third managed timber loading. All three provided names of men they had employed recently.

The fourth said he supervised general work.

“What does general work produce?” Hòa asked.

“Repairs. Whatever is needed.”

“Where?”

“Different sites.”

Thảo put down her pencil.

“You all use that phrase. Do they teach it somewhere?”

Lan looked at the man with clean cuffs.

“Answer the question.”

He named a yard near the lower branch.

Tín watched his hands. The fingers were soft except for a thickened place where a pen rested. He might manage labour, but he had not spent much time beside the machines he claimed to supervise.

“When were you last there?” Tín asked.

“Yesterday.”

“How did you travel?”

“Boat.”

“From which landing?”

The man named the old crossing.

“What time?”

“After noon.”

Thảo leaned forward.

“The loading boat left there before ten. I sold the passenger spaces myself.”

“Another boat.”

“Whose?”

He looked at Lan.

She did not rescue him.

The released worker with the bandaged wrist spoke quietly.

“I've seen him at the office. He counts the envelopes.”

The man looked toward the door.

Tín moved his chair a little farther from it.

“You're allowed to leave,” he said. “But Vũ will want your name before you do.”

The inspector stood in the doorway with his notebook open.

The man with clean cuffs remained seated.

“I'm not a foreman,” he said at last.

Hòa put a line through the title on his paper.

“Excellent. We've repaired one thing.”

The clerk glanced at the teacher's hands. Something in his face changed before he looked down again. Hòa laid the pencil flat and waited with the quiet patience he used on pupils who were deciding whether to try the same lie twice.

Tín watched the recognition pass between them. His son had never mentioned meeting this man.

He did not ask about it in front of the room.

The clerk's face reddened.

Lan asked who had told him to come in place of the missing foreman. He would not answer until Vũ took him into the smaller classroom. Tín let them go.

The three actual foremen looked increasingly uncomfortable.

“You weren't asked to bring a performance,” Tín told Lan.

“I wasn't told he was replacing anyone.”

“By whom?”

“The office.”

Thảo laughed once, without pleasure.

Lan turned toward her.

“Yes. I heard it.”

They resumed with the men who could answer. One had seen Lâm at the engine yard several days earlier. He remembered the scar on the thumb. He had borrowed a file from him.

“Was he injured?” Thảo asked.

“Sore foot. He wrapped it before work.”

“Which foot?”

The foreman thought, then pointed at his own left.

Thảo lowered her eyes. Tín saw relief and fear pass across her face so closely together that neither had time to settle.

“Where did he go?”

“A contractor came for eight men. Said they could repair larger machinery.”

“Name?”

“They called him Thân.”

“His own name?”

“I don't know.”

Tín asked about the boat that took them. The foreman described a flat deck, a low roof, an engine mounted farther forward than usual. He remembered a sound in the exhaust, a hollow beat at irregular intervals.

Ngân, who had come to deliver a message to Thảo, stopped beside the door.

“Blue rail?” she asked.

“I think so.”

“Old patch at the bow?”

The foreman nodded.

She looked at Tín.

“I've carried fuel to that boat. It used to belong to Tòng.”

Tín knew the name. Tòng had operated a small landing beyond the upper bend, then sold it after an illness. He had not seen him in years.

“Where is Tòng now?”

“Same house. Less house than before.”

Vũ returned with the clerk. He did not announce what the man had said. He asked Lan to remain afterward. She agreed with the expression of someone deciding which future inconvenience to accept first.

The meeting broke into smaller conversations. Families compared names. The foremen discovered that people remembered the promises made before a job began, even when those promises had never reached the office.

Tín went outside.

Mai stood near the gate with a woman carrying a cloth bag. The woman looked exhausted and frightened. She kept asking Mai whether she had understood correctly.

Tín recognized the patched cloth inside the bag before he understood who she was.

“Cường's mother?” he asked.

The woman nodded.

“He's alive. They're treating his leg.”

She pressed both hands over her mouth. Mai put an arm around her and led her toward the clinic path.

Tín watched them go.

When Mai returned, Hòa was waiting beside him.

“Who asked you to fetch her?” Hòa said.

“Mrs. Liên told Grandmother someone should. Grandmother knew the woman's cousin. I knew the house.”

“You should have told me.”

“You were in the classroom.”

“A classroom is not an unreachable continent.”

Mai folded her arms.

“I went with her cousin. In daylight. Along the main road.”

“And if someone thought you were carrying messages for your grandfather?”

“I was carrying a message for Mrs. Liên.”

“They won't make that distinction.”

She looked toward Tín.

He did not intervene.

“I'm not asking you to become stupid because I'm worried,” Hòa said. “I'm asking you to tell me where you are before I have to hear about it from someone who saw you.”

Mai's arms lowered.

“All right.”

“And if we say a route isn't safe, you listen.”

“You have to tell me why.”

“Yes,” Hòa said. “I do.”

She considered him for another moment, then nodded.

After she had gone inside, Hòa exhaled.

“You enjoyed that.”

“Parts of it.”

“You used to tell me to do what you said and stop asking questions.”

“You became a teacher. Apparently it didn't work.”

Hòa looked at him and unwillingly smiled.

Tín touched his shoulder.

“You're better at this.”

“I have different pupils.”

That afternoon, Cường spoke to Vũ.

Tín waited outside the clinic room while the young man's mother sat beside his bed. He could hear parts of the conversation through the open window: names, a meeting place, the promise that nobody was to be hurt if the families ran when the first shots were fired.

The young man's voice broke when he reached the moment Sơn stepped between him and the gun.

His mother told him to continue.

Tín looked at her through the window. She was holding his good hand. Her face was hard with something more complicated than anger.

“Tell it properly,” she said. “Don't make me come here to hear you lie.”

Vũ waited until the young man could speak again.

When the inspector came outside, Tín rose from the bench.

“Phúc?”

“A man who reports to him. We have a name.”

“Will the boy be safe?”

“He won't be left here alone.”

Tín nodded.

Vũ looked toward the people waiting beneath the awning.

“They tell you things they won't tell us.”

“Some tell me what they think keeps them alive.”

“I noticed.”

“Ask them when I'm outside.”

The inspector considered him.

“You could make this much harder.”

“I'm old. I don't have time to make every mistake twice.”

Vũ put the notebook away.

“That will be useful if you remember it when someone irritates you.”

Tín looked at him for a moment.

Then he laughed.

It startled the people nearest the bench. One man turned so quickly that he nearly dropped the bowl he was carrying. Tín took it from him before it fell, set it on the bench, and continued down the path toward Ngân's boat.

Tòng's house lay beyond the upper bend.

For the first time since Thảo had come beneath his house, he had a direction that belonged to a boat rather than a promise.


## 9. The Empty House

Tòng had sold the upper floor of his house.

The timber had been taken away piece by piece. Four old posts remained above the roof, too substantial to remove cheaply and too bare to serve any purpose. From the river the house looked as if someone had begun an enlargement and changed his mind halfway through.

Tín remembered sitting upstairs with a view across the bend. Tòng had served tea there and complained that birds stole fruit from a tree he had planted for shade.

The tree remained. Most of the house beneath its branches did not.

Ngân tied up beside a narrow plank landing. Thảo climbed out first. Tín followed, carrying a bag of food Bích had insisted he bring.

“Don't arrive at someone's house with nothing but questions,” she had said.

He had obeyed.

Tòng came to the door with a walking stick. His left leg had stiffened since Tín last saw him, and his face had lost so much weight that the eyes seemed to belong to a younger man looking out through it.

“Well,” he said. “The river has started returning things.”

“Only the ones nobody else wants,” Tín answered.

Tòng looked at the bag.

“Your wife packed that.”

“Yes.”

“I thought so. You used to think bringing yourself was enough.”

He stepped aside and let them enter.

The downstairs room held a bed, a table, a cabinet, and several objects that had clearly once belonged in separate rooms. A framed photograph stood on the cabinet. Tín recognized Tòng's wife and understood from the cloth folded beneath the frame that he would not be seeing her.

“Three years,” Tòng said, following his glance.

Tín inclined his head.

Tòng put the food on the table and called to someone outside. A young woman came in carrying a basket of washing. His daughter, Tín guessed, then corrected himself when Tòng introduced her as his granddaughter.

Another set of years he had allowed to pass without counting.

She made tea while Thảo explained about Lâm and the boat with the blue rail.

Tòng listened with his stick laid across his knees.

“That boat was mine,” he said. “Engine still knocks?”

“Apparently,” Ngân said.

“Good. I spent money on that knock. I'd hate somebody else to fix it for nothing.”

He asked about the patch at the bow and nodded when the foreman's description was repeated.

“Sold it six years ago. A man named Tự bought it. Not much of a boatman. Knew exactly how to talk to one, though.”

“Where is he now?” Tín asked.

“Working for Dưỡng, last I heard.”

Thảo looked at Tín.

He kept his attention on Tòng.

“What work?”

“Moving equipment between repair sites. Men too. He came here last year for a part he thought I'd kept.”

“Had you?”

Tòng pointed at the bare posts above the roof.

“I haven't kept much.”

His granddaughter put the tea down. Tòng waited until she had returned to the washing before speaking again.

“Phúc's people bought the balance on the boat after I sold it. Said the sale hadn't settled the old obligation.”

“You owed on it?”

“For the engine. Paid most of it before the sale, the rest afterward.”

“To whom?”

“The office your men used.”

Tín looked around the room.

“They took the upper floor?”

“A builder took it. Paid me properly. I gave the money to men who didn't.”

He said it without changing his tone. That made the account harder to mistake for an appeal.

Tín asked which collector had come. Tòng named a man who was now dead, then another who worked for Chấn. He remembered the visits because his wife had been ill during them. The sums mattered less in his telling than the hours they had stood outside her room, arguing loudly while she tried to sleep.

“Why didn't you send for me?” Tín asked.

Tòng looked at him.

“I did.”

Nobody spoke.

Outside, the granddaughter shook water from a sheet and hung it over the line.

“Who carried the message?”

“A man from the old office. He said you'd finished with business.”

Tín set his cup down.

“I didn't receive it.”

“I believe you.”

Tòng rubbed his thumb along the handle of the walking stick.

“For a while I preferred thinking you had. A man deciding against you is easier to hate than a message that disappears.”

Thảo looked toward the photograph on the cabinet.

“Do you still have any papers?” she asked.

“Some. My granddaughter keeps them. She says I should stop putting things in tins with no labels.”

“She sounds sensible.”

“She's expensive. Sensible people usually are.”

Tín let them arrange for copies to be taken to the school. He did not ask for the originals. The papers belonged in Tòng's house unless he chose otherwise.

When the conversation returned to the boat, Ngân drew its shape on the table with a wet finger. Tòng corrected the position of the engine and the canopy supports.

“Tự changed the stern,” he said. “Made room for more cargo. It sits lower now.”

“Can it enter the smaller repair channels?”

“Empty. Not with much weight.”

Tín considered the possible routes. He knew the river's old shape, Ngân knew its current shallows, and Tòng knew the boat. None of them possessed the answer alone. Together they removed two likely yards from consideration.

The remaining sites lay around an island farther upstream, connected to the bank by a narrow raised road. One held a mill. Another had been used to store equipment. A third stood near the remains of a brick kiln.

Red dirt, old grease, large machines.

Tín asked whether Dưỡng had bought the equipment yard.

Tòng nodded.

“Leased it first. Then everyone stopped saying leased.”

Ngân looked toward the river.

“There are men at the island landing. They turn small boats away.”

“You've tried?”

“I carried a woman there looking for her son. They told us he had never been employed.”

“Had he?”

“He came home two weeks later wearing a shirt with the yard's name on it.”

Thảo drew a long breath through her nose.

Before she could speak, an engine sounded outside. Lan stepped onto the plank landing with a man carrying a portable radio. She looked annoyed at the instability of the planks and more annoyed that Tín saw her test them before crossing.

“You could have told someone where you were going,” she said.

“Apparently I did.”

“Ngân's nephew. At the fuel seller.”

Ngân looked pleased that her family remained better informed than the office.

Lan set the radio on the table.

“We found a crew. Lâm is with them.”

Thảo stood so quickly her chair struck the wall.

“Where?”

“The foreman won't give a location. He says he's waiting for instructions.”

“From whom?”

“From the man who pays him.”

Tín looked at the radio.

“Can he speak?”

Lan adjusted the control and called the boat's name. A burst of static answered. Then a man said she was late.

“Put Lâm on,” she said.

There was a delay filled with engine noise. Tín listened to it. Beneath the irregular exhaust came another sound, a repeating metallic beat that did not belong to the boat itself.

Thảo moved closer to the table.

“Lâm?”

Her brother's voice emerged through the noise, thinner than Tín had expected.

“I'm here.”

“Are you hurt?”

“I'm all right.”

“Don't tell me that because someone is standing beside you.”

A short pause.

“My foot is bad.”

Thảo closed her eyes.

“Are they treating it?”

“They gave me something to put on it.”

“Where are you?”

Another voice said something too far from the microphone to be understood. Lâm answered it, then returned.

“At the work site.”

Tín leaned over the table.

“What are you repairing?”

Silence.

“This is Tín,” he said.

The background noise changed. Someone had turned the radio or moved away from the engine. For one second the metallic beat became much clearer.

“Old drive equipment,” Lâm said. “Big pieces.”

“Does it run?”

“One unit. The other has the wrong parts.”

“What do they use it for?”

The second voice returned, louder.

Lâm spoke quickly.

“Tell my sister I still have the file. The one she said I'd lose. I didn't lose it.”

The radio crackled. Another man came on and said the conversation was finished.

“Keep him there,” Tín said.

“He's there until the account is paid.”

“No one touches him.”

“That's for the office to decide.”

The connection ended.

Thảo kept one hand on the radio. She looked at Lan as if the other woman had physically taken her brother out of the room.

“Call again.”

Lan tried. Nobody answered.

Tín looked at Ngân.

“The equipment yard by the kiln.”

“You heard that?”

“The old drive. It isn't turning a mill. Too slow. The sound travels back from a hard wall behind it.”

Tòng lifted his head.

“The kiln shed. Brick wall on the landward side.”

Tín nodded.

In his head the island arranged itself from things heard, things remembered, and the routes the loaded boat could still use. He did not see a map so much as a set of possibilities collapsing into one another. The long roof. The red ground. The deep berth. The heavy machinery that could be run in place. The wall returning the sound.

“They're using the drive to power a loading rig,” he said. “The boat must be beside it.”

Ngân studied him.

“Could be the northern yard.”

“No wall close enough. And they would have heard the mill.”

Thảo looked between them.

“You're certain?”

“Certain enough to go and look.”

“Now.”

“Before dark.”

Lan took her hand off the radio.

“They will see you.”

“I intend to be difficult to overlook.”

“If they move the crew—”

“They need the machines. They need men who can repair them. That bought Lâm the time to speak. It buys us the rest of the afternoon.”

He turned to Ngân.

“Can you get us to the lower side without using the landing?”

She looked through the doorway at the water, considering its level.

“Before the light goes. After that I'd be guessing.”

“Then we leave now.”

Thảo reached for her bag.

Tín put one hand over it.

She looked at him with immediate anger.

“Don't.”

“I need boats when we come out. More than Ngân's.”

“Send someone else.”

“They know you. They know your brother. If I send a man from my old office, they'll think they're being collected again.”

She stared at him. He removed his hand from the bag.

“You choose,” he said. “But I need someone at the bank who can tell the families where their people are going.”

Lan looked away, giving Thảo the privacy of not being watched by everyone in the room.

At last Thảo picked up the papers and put them in her bag.

“You bring him to me.”

“I will.”

“Alive.”

Tín held her eyes.

“Alive.”

Tòng rose with difficulty. He went to a shelf and took down a small coil of rope, testing a section between his hands before giving it to Ngân.

“The bank there is soft,” he said. “Don't trust a stake.”

Ngân took the rope.

“You should have told the man who bought your house that.”

“He didn't ask me where to tie it.”

Tín left the food on the table. Tòng watched him step toward the door.

“You owe me a visit when you aren't looking for somebody.”

“I'll come.”

“Bring your wife. She can supervise.”

Tín nodded and stepped out into the afternoon light.

The river had begun to rise against the landing.


## 10. Work at the Waterline

Ngân brought the boat to the lower side of the island with the engine barely above an idle.

Reeds hid the bank until they were almost against it. Beyond them, the land rose toward a long roof and the square remains of an old kiln. Tín heard the slow metallic beat before he saw the machine producing it.

He looked at Ngân.

She nodded.

“You heard that through the radio.”

“Part of it.”

“Remind me not to mutter about you.”

“I've been allowing it.”

She tied the boat to a root exposed in the bank. Tòng's rope held while the water moved beneath them.

Tín put on his shoes.

“Stay where you can leave quickly.”

“That's how I stay everywhere.”

He stepped into the reeds and climbed the bank.

The yard stood on packed red earth. Old drive assemblies, winches, and lengths of heavy pipe lay beneath the roof. A loading machine occupied the far end, connected to an engine that shook on a concrete bed. Men worked around it with their shirts open and their heads down.

Tín counted twenty-three within sight.

There would be more inside the shed. He saw cooking smoke behind it and clothes hanging beneath a separate awning. Someone had made a place to sleep between stacks of timber. Another had tied a strip of cloth around a leaking pipe to guide water into a bucket.

People stayed long enough to solve small problems here.

A guard came around the corner carrying a rifle.

“Where did you come from?”

“The river.”

“You need to use the landing.”

“Then point me toward the man who would have stopped me there.”

The guard looked at him more carefully.

“Your name?”

“Tín.”

There were many men named Tín. This guard apparently knew which one might walk alone into a closed yard and be irritated about directions.

His hand shifted on the weapon.

“Don't,” Tín said.

The guard stopped.

“Take me to the foreman.”

They crossed the yard together. Workers looked up, then returned to their tools when the guard glanced toward them. Tín watched for Thảo's brother: scar on the thumb, sore left foot, hair cut badly at the back because he did it himself when money was short.

He saw him beside a dismantled housing.

Lâm sat on a low stool with the injured foot stretched in front of him. His trousers had been rolled above the ankle. A dark bandage covered the lower leg. He held a file in his right hand.

Tín looked at the tool, then at his face.

Lâm stopped moving.

The foreman came out of the office before either could speak. He was a narrow man with a pale scar beneath his ear. Behind him stood a much larger man carrying a length of chain looped over one shoulder.

“Mr. Tín,” the foreman said. “I wasn't told.”

“I'm telling you now.”

“We aren't ready for a visit.”

“You have people ready to go home.”

The foreman glanced at the guard beside him.

“These men have contracts.”

“Good. They can take them home and read them.”

Tín stepped past him and approached Lâm.

The younger man tried to stand. Tín put a hand on his shoulder and kept him seated.

“Your sister is coming with boats.”

Lâm closed his eyes for one second.

When he opened them, he looked toward the office.

“There are men inside. They locked the room after someone tried to leave.”

“How many?”

“Nine.”

“Can they walk?”

“Some.”

The foreman had followed him.

“You don't understand the situation.”

Tín turned.

“Thân?”

The man nodded cautiously.

“Explain it while you open the door.”

“I don't have the authority to release—”

Tín took him by the back of the neck and walked him toward the office.

The larger man stepped into their path.

“Leave him.”

Tín looked up.

The man was called Quý, according to the name someone shouted from the loading rig. He stood a head taller than Tín and had the heavy, scarred hands of someone who had spent years discovering which objects could be made to move if enough force was applied. The chain on his shoulder looked ornamental until he lifted it with one hand.

“I'm talking to you,” Quý said.

“I heard.”

“Then leave him.”

Tín let go of Thân.

For a moment the foreman looked relieved.

Tín struck Quý in the stomach.

The larger man bent around the blow but did not fall. His breath came out in a hard grunt. He caught Tín's shoulder and drove a knee upward.

Tín turned, letting the knee pass beside him, and struck the same place again.

Quý staggered back two steps. He pulled the chain from his shoulder and swung it across the space between them.

Tín caught it.

The links snapped tight between their hands. Quý braced his feet and pulled. The packed earth shifted beneath him. Tín remained where he was.

Around them, workers began moving away.

Tín pulled once.

Quý came off the ground.

He crossed the yard with both hands still locked around the chain and struck a stack of empty steel housings. They collapsed beneath him in a noise that drowned the engine for several seconds.

Tín let the chain fall.

The guard raised his rifle.

Tín reached him before the weapon settled against his shoulder. He drove the barrel upward, caught the man's elbow, and struck him across the jaw with the stock. The guard collapsed beneath it.

“Down,” Tín told the workers.

They dropped behind machines, timber stacks, anything that stood between them and the office.

A shot broke a window.

Tín moved behind an engine block. A second shot struck the iron above his shoulder. He looked at the angle of the opening, the height of the gun, the reflection in a strip of broken glass still hanging from the frame.

Then he picked up the engine block.

It had stood on the ground so long that earth came away with its base. Two men nearby stared at the empty place it left. Tín carried it three steps and drove it into the office wall.

The wall folded inward.

The gunman disappeared beneath the desk as timber and machinery crossed the room. Tín set the block down against the collapsed opening, sealing the angle from which the remaining men could fire toward the workers.

“Go to the lower bank,” he called. “There's a boat.”

Nobody moved.

Lâm rose on his good foot.

“You heard him,” he shouted. “Help the men who can't walk.”

That voice worked where Tín's had not. The workers knew Lâm. They had shared food with him and watched him try to stand at the machine each morning. They began moving toward the reeds in groups.

Tín went to the locked room.

He pulled the door out of the frame. Inside, nine men sat or lay against the walls. The room was hot and poorly lit. A bucket stood in one corner. One man's face had swollen around a cut over his cheekbone.

Tín crouched beside him.

“Can you stand?”

The man tried. His legs shook and failed.

Tín lifted him and placed him in the arms of two workers who had returned to help. He carried another himself. Behind him, the remaining men came through the broken doorway without waiting to be told twice.

From the loading rig came a roar.

Quý had risen.

Blood covered one side of his face. He pulled a lever on the rig, then seized the suspended hook with both hands. The cable above him tightened as the engine turned. A heavy drive assembly lifted from its supports and swung toward the path the workers were using.

Tín saw the intended collision before the load cleared the stack.

He jumped onto a timber pile, crossed its top, and launched himself toward the rig.

Quý swung the chain at him in midair.

Tín took the blow across his back. The links tore his shirt and struck hard enough to alter the path of his jump. He hit the side of the rig, caught a brace with one hand, and pulled himself above the moving load.

The assembly swung beneath him toward the fleeing workers.

He dropped onto it.

His feet struck the broad casing. He seized the cable and pulled the whole suspended weight toward the rig. The engine laboured against him. Its mounting bolts began to move inside the concrete.

Quý wrapped the chain around Tín's throat.

The pressure came from behind. Tín let go with one hand, caught the chain beneath his jaw, and created enough space to turn his head. Quý pulled harder, using his entire body.

Tín stepped backward off the suspended casing.

Both men fell.

The chain slackened as Quý lost his footing. Tín turned in the air, caught him around the waist, and drove him into the ground beneath the rig.

The impact cracked the concrete pad.

Quý's mouth opened. Blood ran over his teeth. Tín rose, pulled the chain clear of his own neck, and struck the rig's support with the length of it wrapped around his fist.

The support broke.

He caught the leaning frame before it could fall toward the workers and turned it toward the empty yard. The suspended assembly dragged behind it. Engine, rig, and load collapsed together with a grinding explosion of broken metal.

The yard became quiet enough for him to hear men breathing.

Quý tried to rise.

Tín put a hand on his shoulder and drove him flat again.

“Stay down.”

The larger man looked up at him through blood.

For the first time, he obeyed.

Tín turned toward the lower bank.

Lâm was not there.

Thân stood beside a fuel store with one arm around his neck and a pistol pressed beneath his jaw. The foreman had taken him while the others moved. Lâm kept his injured foot off the ground, balancing awkwardly against the man holding him.

“That's enough,” Thân said.

Tín stopped.

“Let him go.”

“You let me leave.”

“Leave.”

“With him.”

Lâm looked at Tín. His face had gone pale, but his eyes were moving: the foreman's hand, the ground, the distance between them.

He still held the file.

Tín saw it beside his thigh.

Thân began backing toward the landing. He dragged Lâm with him, forcing the injured foot to touch the ground. Lâm's face tightened.

“You're going to fall,” Tín said.

Thân looked at him.

“Shut up.”

“His foot won't carry him.”

The next step put Thân on uneven ground. Lâm let his good knee fold.

His weight dropped beneath the foreman's arm. At the same moment he drove the file into the hand holding the pistol.

Tín moved before the scream.

The gun fired into the air. Tín caught Thân's wrist, tore the weapon free, and struck him in the throat with the heel of his other hand. The foreman collapsed beside Lâm, clawing at his neck.

Tín turned him onto his side so he could breathe, then lifted Lâm.

“That was foolish,” he said.

Lâm was shaking so hard he could barely speak.

“Worked.”

“Yes.”

“Tell my sister that part first.”

Tín carried him toward the reeds.

Ngân's boat had gone and returned. Two more boats waited beyond it, and a fourth approached through the channel. Thảo stood on the bank helping men descend the slippery path. She looked up when she saw her brother in Tín's arms.

For one moment she could not move.

Then she came through the reeds and put both hands against Lâm's face.

“I'm here,” he said.

She struck his shoulder once, lightly and without apparent intention.

“I can see that.”

Tín lowered him into the boat. Thảo climbed in beside him and began examining the bandage on his foot with an expression that made Lâm try to hide it beneath the seat.

“Don't,” she said.

He stopped.

Ngân looked at Tín.

“How many more?”

“I'm checking.”

He went back through the yard.

The surviving guards had gathered near the office. None held a weapon. One was helping the gunman crawl out from beneath the broken desk. Quý remained on the ground by the rig, breathing painfully. Thân lay beside the fuel store with a worker sitting nearby, watching to make sure he did not choke.

Tín checked each shed. He found two men hiding beneath a workbench and an older worker who had gone back for a bag containing his clothes and money. He sent them to the boats.

Then he returned to the guards.

“You come too.”

One stared at him.

“To the police?”

“Eventually. First to someone who can keep you alive long enough to answer questions.”

They began lifting the injured.

At the lower bank, Thảo had counted thirty-two workers.

Tín asked three different men whether anyone was missing. Each gave the same answer after thinking about the sleeping places rather than the work crews. That mattered. Men remembered whose bowl stood beside theirs even when they did not know which account had brought him there.

The last boat pushed off as daylight began to leave the reeds.

Tín stood at its stern. Behind him, the dismantled rig lay across the yard. Its engine had finally stopped. In the new silence the island seemed smaller than it had when he arrived.

Lâm sat with his head against his sister's shoulder. He had fallen asleep while she was still asking questions.

Thảo looked up at Tín.

“Alive,” she said.

He nodded.

She rested her cheek briefly against her brother's hair and watched the darkening bank move past them.


## 11. The Clean House

Lâm slept through most of the journey to the clinic and woke when his sister tried to take away the file.

“Mine,” he said.

“I'm putting it in the bag.”

“Then put the bag where I can see it.”

Thảo looked at Tín over her brother's head.

“He used to lose everything.”

“Not everything,” Lâm muttered.

The woman treating his foot told them both to stop talking long enough for him to answer her questions. Thảo obeyed. Lâm tried to make the injury sound less serious until she removed the bandage. Then he looked away and let her examine it.

Tín left them there.

The school had become crowded again. This time the men entering it had come from the same place and could recognize one another without asking which account had brought them. They sat close together even when there was room to spread out. A few kept glancing toward the doors.

Ngân had brought the last boat in herself. She sat on the school steps rubbing one shoulder while a nephew unloaded empty water containers.

“You can pay me now,” she told Tín.

He reached into his pocket.

She named a sum. He counted it out without arguing.

“That's for the fuel,” she said.

“What about the boat?”

“You owe me a new canopy support.”

“I didn't break it.”

“One of your passengers did.”

“They weren't my passengers.”

“Then you'll have difficulty collecting it from them.”

He added money for the support. She took it, checked the amount, and put it away.

“Good,” she said. “Now we can continue respecting each other.”

Inside, Hòa was making space for people who needed to lie down. Hương had arrived with food and was arguing with a man who insisted he was not hungry while staring at the pot.

“Then eat it for the exercise,” she told him.

Tín helped carry the pot to a table. Several men began standing when they saw him. He told them to stay where they were.

One asked whether they would be sent back after the machines were repaired.

“No.”

“The foreman said—”

“The foreman is learning how to swallow again. His schedule has changed.”

The man stared at him. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed. It hurt his bruised ribs, and the laugh became a cough, but other men heard it and began talking again.

Vũ arrived with two officers. He had arranged for Thân and the guards to be kept under watch while they were treated. Quý's injuries were more serious. The large man had not regained the strength to sit upright, though he had attempted it twice and frightened everyone standing near the bed.

“He asked who hit him,” Vũ said.

“He was looking at me.”

“Apparently he wanted confirmation.”

The inspector glanced toward the freed workers.

“We need accounts from them while they remember who was where.”

“They remember.”

“They'll also hear one another's stories. I want them to tell their own.”

Tín nodded.

“Give them food first.”

“I was planning to.”

Vũ went inside. Tín watched him approach the men without ordering them into a line. He asked who felt well enough to speak, then sat beside the first volunteer.

Hòa joined his father at the door.

“There are people outside asking where to bring claims.”

“To Vũ.”

“They mean money. Boats. Land. Things that happened before Phúc.”

Tín looked at his son.

“You've read more of the books.”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“Enough to stop believing the house paid for itself.”

Hương came out carrying empty bowls. Hòa took half of them without being asked.

“Not here,” she said quietly.

They went home together after midnight.

Bích had left the door open. Mai was asleep at the table with a book beneath one hand and an unfinished page beside the other. Tín lifted the book away before the edge could crease under her weight.

She woke immediately.

“I was reading.”

“The book was reading you,” Hương said.

Mai sat upright and pushed her hair out of her face. She looked at Tín's torn sleeve and the bruising across his neck.

“You found him?”

“Alive.”

Her shoulders lowered. She had been holding the question longer than he realized.

Bích came from the kitchen with a kettle.

“Upstairs,” she told Mai. “Whatever you haven't learned by now can wait until your eyes open.”

Mai gathered her books. At the stairs she paused.

“Did he have the file?”

Tín looked at her.

“Thảo told me,” she said. “She said he was always losing things.”

“He kept it.”

Mai nodded and went upstairs.

The adults sat around the table. Hương finished counting the bowls she had brought back. Bích poured tea. Hòa placed a notebook beside his cup.

Tín recognized the household accounts.

“Now?” Bích asked.

Hòa looked at his father.

“If we don't do it now, he'll be gone before breakfast.”

Tín took the notebook.

Hòa had marked the rent payments from the engine yard and the warehouse. There were other receipts from land Tín rarely visited. Some had increased sharply during years when he remembered no corresponding improvement in the property.

“We need to know which payments came from actual rent,” Hòa said. “And which were shares of collections.”

“I know some.”

“Then start with those.”

Tín named them. Hòa wrote them down.

Bích watched without speaking. She had managed the food, repairs, medical visits, school costs. She knew exactly what money had entered the house and where it had gone. What she had not known was what men had done before handing it over.

“You told me the extra payment from the upper land was because they built storage,” she said.

“That was what I was told.”

“By whom?”

“Dưỡng.”

She looked at him for a long time.

“You were very ready to be told things that let you stay comfortable.”

“Yes.”

Hương put the last bowl aside.

“What happens to the people living in those rooms if you sell the yard?”

Tín turned toward her.

“I hadn't decided to sell it.”

“Hòa says that's where the money will have to come from.”

“Some of it.”

“Then decide about the people first. They just had their rooms burned. They don't need to be congratulated on getting a new owner.”

Hòa looked at his wife. She kept her attention on Tín.

He nodded.

“The repairs first. Then an arrangement that doesn't put them out.”

“Written down,” she said.

“Yes.”

Bích refilled the cups.

“And Mai's schooling?” Hòa asked.

Tín looked toward the stairs.

“She goes.”

“That's not a source of money.”

“I still have money that doesn't come from those accounts.”

“Then we need to know which money.”

For a moment irritation rose in him again. He could have settled the practical question by putting a sum on the table. His son would once have accepted it, grateful and uneasy. Now Hòa wanted the history of the money as well as the amount.

Tín opened the notebook to a clean page.

“All right,” he said. “Let's find it.”

They worked until the kettle was empty.

The next morning, Tín visited Quý.

The large man lay on a narrow bed with his chest bound and one arm supported. His eyes followed Tín from the doorway. An officer moved his chair closer when the old man entered.

“Stay,” Tín told him.

“I intended to,” the officer said.

Quý tried to raise his head.

“Don't,” Tín said. “You've already demonstrated how badly you take advice.”

The man let his head rest again.

“How old are you?” he asked.

“Seventy.”

Quý stared at him.

“Fuck.”

“People say that more often than happy birthday.”

The officer looked down at his notebook.

Tín pulled up a chair.

“How many other crews?”

Quý's expression closed.

“I guarded one yard.”

“And men arrived from others.”

“Sometimes.”

“Where?”

“I wasn't told.”

Tín watched him breathe. The man had fought without much imagination and endured damage that would have killed an ordinary labourer. He had also obeyed the first instruction that proved more frightening than his employer.

“Who brought them?”

Quý named three boatmen. The officer wrote the names down.

“Any women?” Tín asked.

“Not at the yard.”

“Children?”

“No.”

“Families held elsewhere?”

The man looked toward the window.

Tín waited.

“There are houses,” Quý said. “People wait there while relatives bring money. I never worked those.”

“Who does?”

“A man called Nhuận.”

The officer asked him to spell it. Quý did.

Tín rose.

“Tell him the rest,” he said, indicating the officer.

“Are you going to come back?”

“That depends how much work you leave unfinished.”

Outside, Lan waited beneath the awning.

She had a bruise along her wrist. Tín saw her notice him seeing it and pull the sleeve down.

“Phúc?” he asked.

“A door.”

“Did the door have an opinion?”

“A strong one.”

He waited.

“He's moving people from the houses,” she said. “Says the weather is turning and the roads may go under.”

“Where is he taking them?”

“Toward the old crossing.”

Tín looked beyond her at the river. Cloud lay low over the far bank, darker than the afternoon required.

“How many?”

“I don't know.”

“Why tell me?”

Lan looked at the clinic doorway.

“Because he asked which families would pay fastest if they thought everyone else had already paid.”

“And you answered?”

“I told him frightened people are very bad at producing money they don't possess.”

Tín glanced at her wrist again.

“The door disagreed.”

She looked directly at him.

“If I leave now, he thinks I'm going to you. If I stay, he thinks I'll eventually go to you. So I thought I'd simplify one fucking thing.”

He stepped aside so she could sit on the bench.

“Tell Vũ,” he said.

“I came to tell you.”

“Then tell both of us.”

Lan sat. For the first time since she had entered his borrowed room above the restaurant, she looked as though she had no appointment to attend and nowhere particularly safe to be late for.

Tín went inside to find the inspector.


## 12. The Other Engine

Chấn asked to meet at a mill where the machinery was loud enough to make every conversation feel private.

Tín arrived before the appointed hour. He walked through the loading area and spoke to the owner, a woman who had inherited the business from her father and spent the first year discovering how many men believed inheritance made her ignorant.

She showed him a cracked bearing housing without being asked.

“Your friend's people supplied it,” she said.

“Which friend?”

“The one who charges for the repair after selling the broken part.”

“You may have to narrow it down.”

She gave him a look and pointed toward the office where Chấn would sit.

Tín examined the housing. The fracture was old enough to have collected grease inside it. Someone had cleaned the outer surface and painted over the line before delivering it.

“Keep this,” he said.

“I was planning to put it on his desk.”

“That would make it his.”

“I wasn't planning to use the door.”

Tín smiled and handed it back.

Chấn arrived with two men carrying food. He appeared not to notice the owner's expression when she showed them where to put it.

“Business should be conducted over a meal,” he said.

“You must be very hungry,” Tín answered.

They sat in an office whose windows overlooked the moving belt and the workers feeding grain into the machine. Every surface held a light coating of dust. Chấn wiped a place for his bowl with a napkin and inspected the result before sitting down.

“Things have gone too far,” he said.

“They went far before anyone objected.”

“I'm objecting now.”

“What would you like in exchange?”

Chấn's hand stopped above the bowl.

“Must it be like that?”

“With you? It saves time.”

The younger man leaned back.

“Phúc doesn't understand limits. His father did. Dưỡng understands them but keeps moving them to wherever he needs the bank built. I have people relying on a route that stays open.”

“The route you chained shut.”

“Controlled. There is a difference.”

“For the man holding the key.”

Chấn lowered his voice, though the mill covered it already.

“I can put boats at your disposal. Engines. Men who know the channels. I can tell you which houses Phúc has been using.”

“Tell Vũ.”

“I'm telling you.”

“That's becoming a popular way of avoiding him.”

Chấn glanced toward the workers outside.

“If this ends with Phúc gone, somebody will still have to collect the fees. Maintain the landing. Resolve disputes.”

Tín waited.

“I could do it properly.”

“You've had years to demonstrate that.”

“Under different conditions.”

“Whose?”

Chấn's mouth tightened.

Tín picked up a spoon and set it down again without eating.

“You want me to clear your competitors and leave you the gate.”

“I want order.”

“You want a receipt saying you're allowed to frighten people.”

For a moment Chấn looked genuinely offended. Tín recognized the expression. Men who had spent years calling their wishes arrangements often resented hearing them described plainly.

“You did it,” Chấn said.

“Yes.”

“And now nobody else is allowed?”

“I'm allowed to recognize a poor imitation.”

Chấn stared at him.

Outside, the mill belt changed its rhythm.

Tín heard the alteration beneath the machinery's constant noise. A slight slowing, then an uneven recovery. He looked through the window. The worker beside the control lever had moved away. A stranger stood near the wall where the power entered the building.

“Who are those men?” he asked.

Chấn looked over his shoulder.

“Not mine.”

“You brought two.”

“They're downstairs.”

There were four new men on the mill floor. One carried a sack against his chest. Another had placed himself near the door through which the workers would leave.

Tín stood.

Chấn caught his sleeve.

“Don't make a scene.”

Tín looked at the hand until Chấn removed it.

“That decision appears to have been made.”

The man with the sack lifted it toward the office window.

Tín took Chấn by the collar and pulled him beneath the desk.

Gunfire tore through the glass.

The first burst crossed the place where Tín's head had been. The second broke the wall behind him. Downstairs, workers dropped their sacks and ran toward the loading door.

The man waiting there raised his weapon toward them.

Tín went through the window.

He crossed the open space above the mill floor with his knees drawn toward his chest, passed over the moving belt, and struck the gunman feet-first. The man hit the doorframe with a sound that came from both timber and bone. His weapon fell beneath the platform.

Tín landed among the fleeing workers.

“Outside,” he said.

The man with the sack fired again. Tín moved behind a stack of filled bags. Grain burst into the air where the bullets struck. The stack began to collapse toward him.

He pushed it over deliberately.

The falling sacks blocked the gunman's view. Tín moved through the dust on the other side, following the sound of the weapon rather than the place it had last appeared.

A man stepped out with a knife.

Tín caught his wrist, turned the blade, and drove it back into the man's throat.

The man folded against him. Blood ran across Tín's hand. He lowered the body far enough to clear his path and kept moving.

Another burst of gunfire struck the machine behind him. Metal rang. A fragment cut across his cheek. He felt something hit his upper arm and stop against bone.

The arm remained useful.

He reached the gunman through the cloud of grain dust and struck him in the chest with the heel of his palm. The man's sternum gave beneath the blow. He fell backward under the moving belt and did not rise.

The last attacker had retreated toward the power housing.

He held a pistol in both hands. He was trying to find a clear line through the dust and the moving machinery.

Tín stood where he could see him.

The man fired.

Tín had already moved. He stepped onto the narrow frame beside the belt, used its motion to carry his weight forward, and crossed the gap to the upper platform. The pistol followed him too slowly.

He took hold of the platform railing and ripped it from the wall.

The attacker ducked behind the housing as the iron struck it. The blow shattered the cover and drove the entire housing off its mounts. Sparks flashed once and vanished. The mill's rhythm collapsed into a descending mechanical groan.

Silence arrived in pieces.

Tín climbed down.

The last attacker crawled from behind the damaged housing with one leg dragging. He reached toward the pistol lying beyond his fingers.

Tín stepped on the weapon.

“Finished,” he said.

The man looked at the two bodies on the floor.

He stopped reaching.

Outside, the mill owner was counting her workers. She asked twice about a man who had gone to the loading dock, then saw him coming around the corner carrying someone else's sandal.

Tín stood in the doorway until she finished.

“Everyone?”

She nodded, then looked past him at the machinery.

“My mill.”

“I will pay for the repairs.”

She stared at his bleeding arm.

“You'd better. I charge extra for idiots who conduct business in the middle of a working day.”

He accepted that.

Chấn remained beneath the office desk.

Tín climbed the stairs and pulled him out by the back of the shirt. The younger man had a cut across his forehead where glass had fallen. He began speaking before he was upright.

“I didn't know.”

Tín carried him to the broken window.

“Look at them.”

Chấn tried not to.

Tín moved him closer.

“Names.”

“I don't know all of them.”

“Start with the ones you do.”

He named the man who had carried the sack and the one who guarded the exit. Both worked for a collector who answered directly to Phúc. Chấn said he had told the office where he was going because he did not want it to appear he was making secret arrangements.

Tín turned him around.

“You made secret arrangements and announced the location.”

“I thought they would listen.”

“They did.”

He released Chấn's shirt.

The younger man stood breathing through his mouth, looking from Tín's face to the blood on his hand.

“What will you do to me?”

“Tell Vũ what you told me.”

“He'll hold me.”

“Then you'll know where you're sleeping.”

Tín left him in the office with the mill owner watching the stairs. She held the cracked bearing housing in both hands. Chấn wisely made no attempt to push past her.

By the time Vũ arrived, people from neighbouring businesses had surrounded the mill. They spoke over one another until the inspector separated those who had seen the attack from those who had arrived to see its result.

Tín sat on a low wall while the woman from the clinic examined his arm.

“Again,” she said.

“I've had a difficult morning.”

“So have I. Mine keeps arriving wearing your face.”

She worked without gentleness where gentleness would only prolong the task. Tín looked away while she cleaned the wound. Across the yard, Vũ spoke to Chấn. The conversation grew increasingly one-sided.

Lan arrived near sunset.

She saw the bodies being carried from the mill and stopped beside Tín without speaking.

“He knew I was here,” he said.

“Chấn told him.”

“I know.”

“Then you know how little separates them when they get frightened.”

She looked toward the river.

“Dưỡng is moving the dredger tonight.”

“Where?”

“The old relief channel. He's closing the lower entrance before the weather gets worse.”

“That leaves Chấn's gate.”

“Yes.”

“And the houses beyond it?”

Lan's jaw tightened.

“They'll need boats.”

Tín stood. The woman treating him caught his wrist.

“I haven't finished.”

He sat again.

Lan watched him obey.

“If he closes it,” she said, “Phúc can tell every family waiting for someone that the river is too dangerous to search. He can move people through the remaining passage while the small boats sit on the bank.”

Tín looked at the darkening water. He could already see the arrangement: one protected route, one gate, a storm making every alternative worse. Dưỡng had built a machine out of geography and fear. Phúc only needed to operate the narrow end of it.

The woman tied the bandage.

“Now,” she said, releasing his arm.

Tín went to find Ngân.


## 13. The Last Partnership

The dredger's lights made a white corridor across the black water.

Ngân stopped outside it. Her engine idled while Tín studied the shapes beyond the glare: the long floating body, the lowered boom, the work platform drawn close to the bank. A smaller vessel had brought concrete sections and timber. Men were moving material toward the mouth of the relief channel.

Above them the sky had disappeared behind cloud.

“He picked his hour,” Ngân said.

“He picked more than that.”

Tín watched a line tighten between the dredger and the bank. The machine was being positioned to push material into the narrow entrance. Once the opening was obstructed, water would still find its way through, but small boats would not. They would have to approach Chấn's gate or risk the outer branch in weather that was already turning against them.

Farther upstream, families would see the river growing rough and believe the men who said there was only one safe way out.

Dưỡng had never lacked imagination. He preferred plans that made the victim explain the necessity to himself.

“You can put me beside the lower platform,” Tín said.

Ngân looked at the men above it. Two carried rifles. Another stood beside a mounted light, turning its beam slowly across the channel.

“I could also throw you at them. Save a little fuel.”

“You'd strain your shoulder.”

“Your concern touches me.”

She moved the boat into the shadow of the bank. The dredger's engine covered the smaller engine's sound. Water struck the hull in short uneven blows as they crossed the disturbed current beneath the machine.

Tín put a hand against the canopy support to steady himself. Ngân glanced at it.

“That one is still mine.”

He removed his hand.

When they reached the platform, he stood and caught a hanging rope. He climbed without using the knots, lifting himself hand over hand as if the wet line were a staircase that had been built badly.

At the top he stepped onto steel.

A guard turned toward him.

Tín took the rifle before the man's surprise became a decision. He bent the barrel against the rail and set it down between them.

“Dưỡng.”

The guard pointed with both hands empty.

Tín crossed the deck.

Dưỡng stood beneath the forward light wearing a heavy work jacket. He had changed after their fight on the bank, but the bruise along his jaw remained. He watched Tín approach with the expression of a host who had expected an unwelcome guest and was disappointed to have been correct.

“You could have come tomorrow,” he said.

“You would have finished.”

“Exactly.”

Tín stopped several paces from him.

The deck trembled beneath his feet. He could feel the engine's pulses through the steel, the slower movement of the hull beneath them, the changing pressure where the work platform touched the bank. Every part of the machine carried information. He let it reach him without trying to turn it into words.

“Move the dredger out.”

“The bank is unsafe.”

“So you brought concrete to make the water safer.”

“I brought material to contain a failing edge.”

“Across the entrance.”

Dưỡng looked toward the channel.

“A temporary closure.”

“At the same time Phúc is moving families toward the old crossing.”

“I don't run Phúc's affairs.”

“You buy the labour. You control the route. You own the machines. How many pieces of a thing can belong to you before you admit you own the thing?”

Dưỡng's eyes returned to him.

“Careful. That's a question your son has begun asking.”

Tín took one step closer.

The guards behind Dưỡng moved.

“Keep them out of this,” Tín said.

“You didn't bring men.”

“I brought myself. You've had enough demonstrations of what that means.”

Dưỡng held his gaze, then lifted one hand. The guards stopped advancing but did not put down their weapons.

“When you retired,” he said, “you left people expecting the river to obey them because you had once promised it would. Engines failed. Banks collapsed. Boat owners borrowed against good seasons that never arrived. Somebody had to make those promises useful.”

“You made them expensive.”

“I built things.”

“Yes.”

The agreement interrupted him more effectively than an accusation.

Tín looked around the dredger. The machine had been repaired carefully. He could see newer welds, replaced cables, guards fitted where men might catch their clothing in moving parts. Dưỡng did not neglect his equipment. He understood that a machine which worked was worth more than one which merely looked impressive.

“You kept channels open,” Tín said. “You built good landings. You knew where to put a support before anyone else could see the bank moving.”

Dưỡng waited.

“You didn't need to own the people using them.”

“I needed to be paid.”

“You're being paid.”

“Not enough to carry everyone's failures.”

“Then stop buying failures cheaply and pretending they're people.”

Dưỡng struck before the last word had finished crossing the space between them.

Tín turned the first blow away. The second came beneath his guard, fingers closing against the wounded upper arm. Dưỡng squeezed directly over the place where the bullet had stopped.

Pain travelled through the shoulder.

Tín stepped into the grip and drove his forehead into Dưỡng's face.

The younger man released him and staggered against the rail. Tín caught his jacket, turned, and threw him across the deck toward the empty platform.

Dưỡng struck the steel on his side and rolled beneath the first kick. Tín's heel hit the deck hard enough to punch a shallow crater into it.

A guard raised his rifle.

Tín took a loose shackle from the rail and threw it through the weapon. Metal struck metal. The rifle burst apart in the man's hands, and he fell backward screaming.

The second guard fired.

Tín dropped below the rail as bullets struck the place where his head had been. He seized the rail's lower bar, pulled it free of two mounts, and drove it upward through the guard's abdomen.

The man fell against it and went still.

Dưỡng reached Tín before he could turn.

His palm struck the deck beside the old man's foot. Force moved through the plate and into Tín's leg, bypassing the angle he had prepared to defend. His knee bent unexpectedly.

Dưỡng hit him in the chest.

Tín crossed the deck backward and struck the base of the boom. The impact rang through the machine. Something moved inside his ribs, sharp and deep.

He rose.

Dưỡng stood with blood running from his nose, one hand open and low.

“Seventeen years,” he said. “You thought I spent them sitting still.”

Tín drew a breath. The injured place in his chest protested. The rest of his body remained entirely willing.

“You should have called,” he said. “I'd have told you what was wrong with that.”

Dưỡng came at him.

Tín met the next strike with his forearm, not stopping it but turning its line. His other hand caught Dưỡng behind the elbow. He shifted his weight a fraction before the younger man expected him to and let Dưỡng's own force carry the arm across his body.

Then he struck the shoulder.

Bone broke beneath the blow.

Dưỡng cried out and drove a knee into Tín's stomach. Tín accepted the impact long enough to take him around the waist. He lifted him, turned him in the air, and brought him down against the deck.

The plate beneath them buckled.

Tín released him and stepped back.

“Stay there.”

Dưỡng's good hand closed around a cable.

He pulled.

The movement was small. The consequence was not. A retaining piece tore out of its housing. Above them, the loaded boom began to swing without the restraint that had kept it over the work platform.

The suspended concrete moved toward the crew gathered at the rear of the dredger.

Tín turned.

He saw the whole arrangement in one instant: the length of the boom, the position of the load, the engine still driving the machine, the hull moving beneath it. The crew stood in the wrong place because every place left on the deck had become wrong in a different way.

He ran toward the boom's base.

Dưỡng caught his ankle with the good hand.

Tín stamped down on the wrist. The fingers opened. He kept running.

At the base he seized the broad support where the boom transferred its load to the deck. He did not try to stop the swinging weight. He turned the entire support with it, changing the path by an amount too small for the men watching to understand.

The suspended concrete passed above the rear rail instead of through the crew.

“Down!” Tín shouted.

They fell flat.

The load struck the empty supply vessel tied alongside. Its deck collapsed beneath the impact. Water rose over the gunwale. The cable tightened again, dragging the dredger toward the sinking boat.

Tín stepped between two structural beams and took the cable in both hands.

He pulled against the weight of the sinking vessel.

The line cut into his palms. The deck beneath his feet bowed. For a moment both boats stopped moving as if something much larger had taken hold of them below the water.

Tín drew the cable toward his chest.

The supply vessel's bow rose.

Water poured off it in a black sheet. Men on the dredger stared at the shape emerging from the river, at the man holding it, at the narrowing interval in which their lives remained undecided.

Tín pulled once more, lifting the damaged vessel high enough to clear the dredger's side.

Then he turned and threw the cable toward the open river.

The supply vessel swung away. The line tore free of its damaged mount. Concrete, timber, and hull fell into deep water beyond the channel entrance, where they could no longer be used to close it.

The impact sent a wave across the dredger's deck.

Tín stood through it.

Behind him, Dưỡng had reached the side rail.

He was trying to climb into a small launch. His broken arm hung against his body. An attendant held the launch steady while looking over his shoulder at Tín.

The old man walked toward them.

The attendant let go of Dưỡng and raised both hands.

Tín caught the younger man by the back of his jacket and lifted him out of the gap between the boats.

He set him on the deck.

Dưỡng tried to stand. His legs failed. He remained on one knee, breathing through blood.

“You could have let the load fall,” he said.

“You knew I wouldn't.”

“Yes.”

Tín crouched in front of him.

“Then you should have known what would happen when I finished.”

Dưỡng looked at him for a long time.

Something changed in his face. Not regret. Tín had no reason to grant him that. It was the recognition of a calculation completed with a result he could no longer dispute.

He lowered his head.

Tín rose and turned to the crew.

“Shut the engine down. Keep the pumps running if you need them. Nobody moves this vessel until someone checks where the hull took the impact.”

The men obeyed the instructions they understood. One asked about a damaged line. Tín walked over, examined it, and told him to leave it alone until the load on it was gone.

There was enough work to keep fear from becoming panic.

Ngân appeared beside the lower platform with another boat behind her. Inspector Vũ stood in it holding the canopy support with both hands.

“You brought him,” Tín said.

“Lan brought him,” Ngân answered. “I supplied the questionable transport.”

Vũ climbed onto the deck. He looked at the dead guard, the wounded men, the torn machine, and Dưỡng kneeling near the rail.

“Nobody else leaves,” he told the crew.

They appeared willing to accept this as the least alarming instruction of the night.

Tín helped move the injured to the waiting boats. Dưỡng did not resist when he was lifted. He looked at the old man's hands beneath him, the cuts across the palms and the blood drying between the fingers.

“You still believe you can finish it,” he said.

“I intend to finish what is in front of me.”

“Phúc won't stop because I fell.”

“Then I'll go and tell him you weren't enough.”

For the first time that night, Dưỡng almost smiled.

The expression vanished when the movement of the boat disturbed his shoulder.

Vũ sat beside him. An officer took the place opposite. There was no instrument in either man's hand capable of making Dưỡng harmless if he chose to fight again. Tín saw the inspector understand that and remain seated anyway.

Dưỡng looked back at the dredger.

He did not fight.

Tín stayed until the channel entrance was clear and the crew had accounted for every man aboard. He made them call names rather than point at groups. When they reached the dead guard's name, the man reading paused.

“Count him,” Tín said.

They did.

The rain began as Ngân took him back toward Bến Sậy.

It struck the canopy in separate heavy drops, then joined into a continuous sound. Tín sat at the bow with his hands open on his knees. His body hurt in places he could identify and in several he would need to examine later.

Ngân looked at him from the stern.

“You owe me another support.”

He looked up. During the journey he had closed one hand around the metal above him without noticing. It had flattened where his fingers rested.

“Put it on the account,” he said.

“I intend to. Mine comes with honest interest.”

He let go and listened to the rain.


## 14. Where the Lights Went

The rooms near the old crossing were empty.

Vũ had sent men there while the dredger was being secured. They found bedding, cooking pots, clothes too worn to have been worth carrying away. One room held a row of cups with names scratched into the sides. Another smelled strongly of bodies that had spent too long close together.

No one remained to answer questions.

Tín stood in the doorway with Lan beside him. An officer held a lamp while Vũ looked through the space beneath a bed.

“They were here this evening,” Lan said.

“How many?”

“I saw schedules. I didn't count people.”

The words came out more harshly than she intended. She looked at the cups, then at the empty sleeping places.

Tín walked to the back of the room. A small window overlooked a path leading to the water. Fresh footprints crossed the mud beneath it. He followed them outside.

The path ended at a wooden platform. A rope had cut a new groove into one post. Another post leaned toward the channel, its base loosened by a load greater than the small landing was built to hold.

Something large had been brought alongside.

Tín crouched at the edge. White paint marked a place where a hull had rubbed against the wood. Beneath it lay older layers of blue and black. The vessel had been painted more than once, and none of the men doing it had spent much time removing what came before.

Lan stood above him.

“The ferry?”

“Which one?”

“An old vehicle ferry. Phúc bought it after it stopped running. They use it for storage.”

“Does it have an engine?”

“Not now.”

Tín looked along the channel. The water was high enough to move an unloaded vessel through the bend. With people aboard and a shallow tow, it could still have passed. Not comfortably, but the men deciding would not be the ones pressed against the inside walls.

“It was taken out before the level fell,” he said.

Vũ joined them.

“Where could they put it?”

Lan named three places. Tín added two more. Ngân, waiting below in her boat, shouted up that one of them had been too shallow since a bank collapsed.

Vũ sent men toward the roads leading to the remaining berths. There were not enough officers to search every boat and building at once. Tín could hear him making choices without pretending they were complete solutions.

“I'll take the inner channel,” the old man said.

The inspector looked at him.

“If you find people, get word to us before the entire place becomes timber in the water.”

“I'll consider the condition of the timber.”

“Tín.”

The old man met his eyes.

“I will send word.”

Lan climbed into Ngân's boat before anyone asked whether she was coming. She sat beneath the canopy with her handbag held between her knees. The wrist beneath her sleeve had begun to swell.

Ngân looked at it.

“You should wrap that.”

“I will.”

“People keep saying that on my boat. Usually while bleeding on it.”

She gave Lan a strip of cloth kept beside the engine tools. Lan wrapped her wrist one-handed. Tín reached across when she struggled with the knot and tied it for her.

“Thank you,” she said.

He nodded.

They searched the first berth beneath lights from a warehouse. Its owner came out carrying a lantern and a long stick. He recognized Tín, lowered the stick, and immediately began explaining that his payments were current.

“We're looking for a ferry,” Tín said.

The owner looked at Lan. Recognition made him more frightened rather than less.

“I don't hold anybody.”

“Then let us look,” she said.

He did.

They found sacks of feed, two sleeping labourers, and a family sheltering from the rain because the roof of their house had begun to leak. A girl of about ten sat on a rolled mat holding a chicken against her stomach.

The bird watched Tín with absolute hostility.

“It bites,” the girl warned him.

“So do I.”

She considered his face and tightened her grip on the chicken.

Her father showed them the moorings. An old barge occupied one. The other was empty. He had heard a tow pass after sunset but had not seen the vessel behind it.

“No lights on the big one,” he said. “I thought the battery had failed.”

Tín asked which direction the engine noise had gone. The man pointed downstream, then hesitated.

“It turned somewhere. The sound came back.”

That could mean a bend, a wall, another engine starting. Tín asked him to describe what he had heard rather than choose an explanation for it.

The account ruled out one berth and made another more likely.

They went there next.

The second place held three workboats and a cargo hull with a torn canopy. Men slept under tarpaulins. One woke when Tín stepped aboard and reached for a knife before his eyes focused.

Tín caught his wrist and held it gently enough not to break anything.

“Easy.”

The man stared at him, then at the lamp behind him.

“Police?”

“Looking for people.”

“So are they.”

Tín let go of the wrist.

The man sat up. He had been waiting for a cousin who had gone to the old crossing that afternoon after a collector said his mother was ill. The mother was alive and well in the house where he had left her that morning.

Lan looked away while he spoke.

“Name,” she said at last.

The man gave it.

“I'll remember,” she said.

He looked at her wrist, then at her face.

“Remembering isn't bringing him back.”

“No,” she said. “It isn't.”

They returned to the boat.

For a while Lan sat without moving. Rainwater ran off the canopy behind her and struck the engine cover in a steady stream.

“I liked being the person they had to ask,” she said suddenly.

Tín looked at her.

“Men came in furious. They threatened the collectors, shouted about the terms, said someone had to give them an answer. Then they sat in front of my desk and waited for me to decide what the figures meant.”

“You were good at it.”

“I was very good at it.”

She rubbed the edge of the bandage on her wrist.

“Don't make me sound as if I wandered in by accident.”

He kept his eyes on the water ahead.

“I wasn't going to.”

Lan gave a short laugh.

“Of course not. You recognize the furniture.”

Ngân slowed as they approached a narrow bridge. The rain had raised the channel enough that the canopy nearly touched the underside. Tín lowered his head. Lan did the same. They passed beneath with wet concrete close above them and the sound of water magnified around the hull.

On the far side, a family stood beside a house whose lower steps had disappeared beneath the channel. A man tried to move an old woman into a boat while she argued that he was leaving her cooking pot behind.

Ngân brought them alongside.

Tín stepped ashore and carried the pot into the boat himself. It was large, blackened, and still warm. The old woman stopped arguing long enough to inspect where he put it.

“Not on its side,” she said.

He set it upright.

“Now you can go,” he told her grandson.

The young man looked grateful and embarrassed. Tín helped him push off.

Lan watched from beneath the canopy.

“You take instructions better when they don't come from men,” she said.

“The women tend to know what they want.”

Ngân snorted and started the engine.

Near dawn they returned to the school.

They had found two missing men and several frightened families. They had not found the ferry. Vũ's officers had searched the road approaches to the other berths without seeing a vessel large enough to match the description. Someone had moved it while the weather, darkness, and ordinary river traffic concealed the movement.

Tín stood beneath the school awning, wet through, and let Hòa pour tea into his hands.

“Sit,” his son said.

“I need—”

“To sit for ten minutes while people tell you what they found.”

Tín sat.

Thảo arrived with Lâm walking beside her on a stick. His foot was freshly wrapped. He moved slowly, but the expression on his face suggested he intended to argue with anyone who described him as helpless.

“You should be lying down,” Tín said.

“You should be seventy,” Lâm answered.

Thảo looked at him sharply, then failed to hide a smile.

Tín took another mouthful of tea.

“Sit beside me. We can both disappoint someone.”

Lâm lowered himself onto the bench. He had brought the file in his bag. Tín saw its handle when he put the bag down.

“I know the smaller loading boats,” Lâm said. “Some came to the yard. If they used one to tow the ferry, I might know who was steering.”

“Tell Vũ.”

“I will. I'm telling you first because he has six people standing in front of him.”

Tín listened.

The names added another piece to the night's incomplete picture. Two towboats could have moved the ferry. One was being repaired. The other belonged to a man who worked from the engine yard behind the old crossing.

Thảo stood very still.

“We began there,” she said.

Tín looked toward the road. The first light was reaching the upper branches beyond the school wall.

“Yes.”

Hòa took the empty cup from him.

“Eat something before you go.”

He brought a bowl and stood beside his father until he began eating. Lan sat at the other end of the bench with her eyes closed. Ngân argued with a nephew over which boat had enough fuel. In the classroom behind them, Vũ asked another man to begin his account at the first thing he had actually seen.

Tín finished the bowl.

As he stood, a boy ran through the gate shouting for Thảo.

The boy was covered in mud. He had come from the engine yard.

“There's a man in the boat,” he said. “They sank it. My father heard him knocking.”

Tín put the bowl into Hòa's hands.

“Which boat?”

The boy pointed toward the old crossing, still trying to get enough breath to speak.

“Bảy's.”

The old man was through the gate before he finished the name.


## 15. What the Water Held

The workboat lay on its side in the narrow channel behind the engine yard.

Tín knew the bend, the depth beside the bank, the posts where boats had once waited for a repair. He had owned the yard when Bảy's father brought an engine there on a borrowed cart. He remembered the engine clearly. He remembered the man less well.

Now Bảy's cabin roof broke the brown surface below him.

The boy who had come to the school stood beside his father near the trees. Blood had dried beneath the father's nose. One eye had begun to swell. He held a length of rope that had been cut through close to the end.

“I tried to get it under the hull,” the man said. “They came out of the shed.”

“How many?”

“Three. Another went down the road.”

Tín looked toward the engine shed. Its door stood partly open. Beyond it, he could hear a man speaking, then the metallic scrape of something being moved across concrete.

“Go to the school,” he told the father. “Take your son.”

“Bảy—”

“I'm going in.”

The man looked at the water. His son tugged his sleeve. At last he let himself be led away.

Thảo arrived in one of the boats waiting at the new crossing. She brought it close to the bank without tying up. Her face showed no surprise that Tín had reached the yard before her boat.

“Where do you want me?”

He pointed to the sheltered side of the wreck.

“Stay behind the hull until I call.”

She looked toward the shed and understood.

Tín removed his shoes and shirt. The bandages across his back and arm had darkened during the night. He left them where they were. There would be time to discover which wounds needed attention after the man inside the boat could breathe without counting the air.

He stepped into the channel.

Visibility vanished at his waist. Mud rose around his legs. He found the submerged gunwale with his foot, took a breath, and went under.

The hull rested at an angle against the channel bed. He followed its seams by touch. There was damage near the lower side, a place where water passed into the boat more freely than it should. He moved beyond it and found the cabin hatch.

A chain secured it from outside.

He put his palm against the steel.

For a moment nothing answered. Then something struck the other side: one weak impact, a pause, another.

Tín struck twice in reply.

He did not pull the hatch open. Water stood above it. Opening it here would release the remaining air before he could know where Bảy's face was inside.

He moved beneath the cabin frame instead.

The mud took his feet almost to the ankles. He shifted until he found firmer ground below it, settled his shoulder against a structural beam, and began to stand.

The boat resisted with the combined weight of its hull, engine, and trapped water.

Tín kept rising.

Something tore beneath the mud. The hull shifted. Water inside it moved toward the lower side, and the boat tried to roll over him. He adjusted the pressure through his shoulder and left hand, preventing the movement from becoming a collapse.

The roof emerged.

Tín's head broke the surface beside it. He drew a breath through his nose and lifted until the hatch stood clear of the water.

A torn seam cut into his shoulder. Warm blood moved beneath the bandage.

“Bring the boat,” he called.

Thảo came alongside, keeping the workboat between her and the shed. Tín held the hull with his left hand and tore the hatch out with his right.

Steel came away with the chain still attached.

Bảy crouched in the remaining air pocket beneath the cabin roof. Water reached his mouth. His face had turned a colour Tín disliked. When light entered, the mechanic tried to stand and fell sideways instead.

Tín laid the torn hatch on the exposed deck.

“Bảy. Look at me.”

The man's eyes moved toward him.

“I'm bringing you out.”

He reached inside, caught Bảy beneath the arms, and drew him through the opening. The mechanic's clothes dragged against the frame. Tín lifted him higher, keeping his face clear, and turned him toward Thảo.

She took his shoulders. Together they lowered him into her boat.

Bảy coughed so violently that Thảo had to turn him to keep his face out of the water collecting on the floor.

“Anyone else?” Tín asked.

The mechanic shook his head.

“Get him around the bend.”

Thảo pushed off. The boat moved slowly at first beneath the added weight, then caught the current. Tín watched it pass behind the bank before lowering the empty workboat.

An engine started inside the shed.

He felt the new vibration through the hull before he saw the cable tighten. Someone had attached a line to the wreck. The winch drew it sideways, trying to roll the boat across the place where he stood.

A gunman appeared beside the shed door.

Tín went under as the first discharge struck the water above his head.

He found the gunwale again. The wreck came down against his shoulder with the slow, enormous force of a building changing its mind about standing upright.

There was nobody left inside it.

That simplified the work.

Tín drove his feet through the mud, turned beneath the falling mass, and lifted.

The empty boat rose out of the channel.

On the bank, men shouted. He could not make out their words through the water pouring off the hull. He could feel the strain passing through every fastener, the cable pulling in the opposite direction, the point at which the machine on the bank would be forced to choose between breaking and moving.

He changed the angle.

Then he threw the boat.

It surfaced keel-first, rolled through the air, and struck the bank hard enough to break its own ribs. The cable snapped. Water erupted across the engine shed and flattened the grass beyond it.

The gunman stumbled backward out of the spray.

Tín came ashore through the wreckage. He found the torn hatch in the mud, took it by one edge, and raised it as the shotgun fired again.

Shot rattled against steel.

He crossed the distance before the man could bring the gun down from the recoil. The hatch struck him broadside. His back hit the shed wall, and the breath went out of him in a wet burst.

Tín set the hatch down.

The winch operator reached for a machete lying beside the engine.

Tín seized the machine by its mounting frame and pulled it free of the concrete. Bolts and broken aggregate struck the roof. He turned the engine in his hands and set it down between them, its exposed weight blocking the operator's path.

The man looked at the machine and then at Tín.

His hands rose slowly.

“Move away from the blade,” Tín said.

He obeyed.

The third man had already reached the far corner of the shed. Tín picked up a loose brick and threw it into the wall beside his head. The brick went through the wood and broke against the ground beyond.

The man stopped.

“Bring your injured friend out,” Tín said. “Put him where he can breathe.”

They did it together, watching him whenever their hands were free.

“Who locked the hatch?”

Neither answered.

Tín looked at the winch operator.

“You were trying to roll the boat over me. You didn't know whether Bảy was still inside.”

“They told us he was dead.”

“You heard him knocking.”

The man's mouth moved without producing a word.

Tín stepped closer.

“Which one of you?”

The third man pointed at the operator.

The operator turned toward him with an expression of pure astonishment, as if betrayal had been an occupation reserved for other people.

Tín took the chain from the torn hatch and put it around the operator's wrists. He twisted the loose ends together until the man could no longer pull his hands apart.

“At the school,” he told the other man. “Tell the police exactly how you found him.”

The man nodded rapidly.

Thảo returned when Tín called.

Bảy sat low in the boat with a blanket around his shoulders. His breathing had steadied enough for him to look at the wreck lying across the bank.

“That was my boat,” he said.

Tín took his shirt from Thảo.

“Yes.”

“You could have left some of it in one piece.”

“It was being used against me.”

Bảy looked at the broken ribs of the hull, then at the man standing above him.

“Poor fucking boat.”

Thảo helped him ashore. They sat him beneath the shed roof while the defeated guards moved away with their injured companion. Tín gave Thảo the names he had obtained and told her what they had admitted. She wrote them on the back of an old ferry ticket.

Then she looked at Bảy.

“What happened?”

The mechanic stared at his own hands. They shook in his lap even when he pressed them together.

“Phúc sent for me. Said the old gantry needed inspecting before they used the berth.”

“When?” Tín asked.

“Yesterday. They kept me there overnight.”

“The ferry?”

“Came back before first light. They'd taken it out while people were searching the rooms. Towed it around the outside and waited behind the storage barges.”

Tín looked toward the channel. The night had given them too many hulls to examine and too few eyes. Now he could see the route Phúc had used.

“How many aboard?”

“Sixty-three. People from the houses. A few children.”

Thảo's pencil stopped.

“You counted?”

“They asked how the ferry would sit with that weight aboard. I count when somebody asks me about weight.”

“Where is it now?”

“The inner berth. Under the gantry, behind the warehouse.”

Tín leaned forward.

“And the inspection?”

Bảy swallowed.

“The upstream support was damaged. I told them it needed bracing. Phúc asked how much could be cut before the rest would fail.”

The rain thickened against the shed roof.

“I wouldn't do it,” Bảy said. “Another man did. Nhuận. They made me watch.”

He described the cuts and the remaining supports. Tín asked him to separate what he had seen from what he feared would happen. The questions irritated him at first, then made his account more precise.

The loading ramp remained connected to the framework. The ferry had been tied in a way that let its movement work against the weakened structure. If the water rose and the wind shifted, the gantry could come down across the shelter and vehicle deck.

“Why?” Thảo asked.

Bảy looked at her.

“He asked me how many people a falling beam would kill. I didn't ask him why.”

Tín stood.

The mechanic caught his sleeve.

“My father brought a boat to your yard.”

Tín looked down at him.

“I remember.”

“You kept it. He stood outside until it was dark, waiting for someone to tell him what else he could pay with.”

Tín saw the borrowed cart again. The engine. The man beside it, made indistinct by all the attention he had given the machinery.

“I remember the boat better,” he said.

Bảy held his sleeve a moment longer.

“Then learn his name before you tell someone what happened.”

He said the name. Tín repeated it.

“Will you speak to Vũ?” Tín asked.

“Will you?”

“I have begun.”

“Then I'll finish what I saw.”

Tín nodded.

Thảo helped Bảy toward the boat. He turned once to look at the wreck, then looked away from it and climbed in with her support.

Tín put on his shirt. The wet cloth pulled against his damaged shoulder. He fastened the buttons anyway.

Beyond the bend, the old gantry stood against a sky the colour of dirty metal.

Now he knew where everyone was.


## 16. An Open Channel

The relief channel remained open, but the rescue boats were gathered at the new crossing, on the other side of the exposed bend.

They could reach the old berth by going out into the main water and returning against the wind. Some of the larger boats could manage it. The narrow craft people had brought from their houses would be thrown sideways before they reached the warehouse.

The short connector through Chấn's gate would keep them sheltered almost the entire way.

Chấn had closed it.

Tín heard this from Ngân before he reached the school. She met him on the path with rain running from the brim of a borrowed hat.

“He says the water is too dangerous.”

“For whom?”

“Apparently for people who haven't paid.”

Thảo had taken Bảy inside. Vũ was listening to him while a woman brought dry clothes. The mechanic kept stopping to look toward the door, as if he expected the men who sank his boat to arrive and object to the version he was giving.

Tín told Ngân what he had learned.

“Sixty-three,” she repeated.

“And guards. There may be injured.”

She looked toward the boats at the crossing.

“We need the engines he's holding.”

“Come after me with the owners.”

“Not every owner will come.”

“Bring the ones who will.”

He went down the path before she could ask how long to wait.

Chấn had returned from his conversation with Vũ and stationed himself beneath the awning of the gate office. Two iron lighters remained chained across the channel mouth. A smaller opening beside the concrete landing had been closed with a heavy gate.

Three families waited in a motorboat while a collector stood with the ignition key in his hand.

Tín saw the children first. One had wrapped a plastic sack around a schoolbag. Another sat on a woman's lap, staring at the rain with the exhausted concentration of a child who had cried until there was nothing left in it.

The collector recognized him and stepped back.

“Give him his key,” Tín said.

The man looked toward Chấn.

“We're controlling passage,” Chấn said. “For safety.”

“Give it back.”

The collector obeyed before his employer could answer.

The boatman caught the key. His first attempt to start the engine failed. On the second it coughed and settled into a rough idle.

“The gate,” Tín said.

Chấn made a small movement with his chin. A man on the landing pulled it clear. The boat passed through, carrying the families toward the new crossing.

Tín watched until it rounded the bend.

“The engines you've taken,” he said. “Bring them out.”

“They were left as security.”

“Their owners need them.”

“Then settle the accounts.”

Tín turned toward him.

Chấn looked worse than he had at the mill. The cut on his forehead had been cleaned and covered, but dried blood remained in his hair. He had not slept. Fear had made him less cautious instead of more; he was gripping the last thing he controlled hard enough to hurt himself.

“You told Vũ you would cooperate,” Tín said.

“With his questions. Not with the theft of my business.”

“Nobody wants your business, Chấn. They want their engines.”

“Some of them are mine.”

“Then you can explain which after sixty-three people are ashore.”

Rain struck the awning and ran between them. On the platform above the gate, a man shifted a rifle into a position from which it could be used quickly.

Tín looked up.

“Put it down.”

The man looked toward Chấn.

Tín returned his attention to the younger man.

“You've been in one room today where someone tried to kill me. Look carefully at who walked out.”

Chấn's jaw moved. He said nothing.

The guard above them lowered the rifle.

Tín went to the shed.

The door was locked. He looked back at Chấn.

“Key.”

Chấn remained beneath the awning.

Tín took the door by its outer edge and pulled. The hinges came out of the frame. He set the door upright against the wall.

Inside, six outboard engines stood beneath a tarpaulin. Two had been stripped for parts. A workbench held components arranged in the vague order of a job that had been postponed whenever something more profitable arrived.

The mechanic standing beside it raised both hands.

“I didn't take them.”

“Which can run?”

He pointed at four engines.

“That one too, if we put it back.”

“How long?”

“Half an hour. Maybe less if nobody keeps asking.”

Tín stepped aside.

“Then I'll be silent somewhere useful.”

He examined the sixth engine. Its shaft had bent under an impact. Someone had tried to make the damaged part fit by altering the pieces around it. The result would turn, but not for long.

The mechanic watched him.

“That one needs a replacement.”

Tín lifted the assembly and looked along it. He turned it slowly, watching the irregular movement at its far end. Then he placed two fingers against one side and his thumb against the other.

The metal moved.

Not far. Enough.

He turned it again, felt the resistance, and made a second adjustment too small for the mechanic to see.

“Try that,” he said.

The man took it as carefully as if it were still hot. He fitted it into place, turned it, and looked up.

“How?”

“It was bent.”

“I knew that.”

“Then we agree on half the work.”

Outside, voices rose. The owners had arrived with Ngân and several relatives. A woman carried a broken clamp from her own engine to identify the matching piece. A fisherman pointed to a weld he had made. Two men began arguing over a fuel tank until an older woman slapped the side of it and told them whose paint was beneath the new coat.

Tín made Chấn's men carry the engines into the open.

He did not distribute them. The owners knew the damage, repairs, and small modifications better than he could. He listened when a dispute arose and asked the people who had actually used the object to speak first.

Chấn watched from the office.

“You're giving away my security.”

“You were very secure behind it.”

“Phúc will come for them.”

“Then he'll know where the engines went. You can stop worrying about being blamed.”

Ngân came beside Tín.

“The gate is too narrow for two boats to pass. We need them returning while the next ones go through.”

He looked at the lighters chained across the rest of the entrance.

“Are there people aboard?”

“Four of his men.”

Tín called to them.

“Come ashore.”

One began to climb down. Another looked at Chấn and hesitated.

“Now,” Tín said.

They came.

He waited until the last man stood on the landing. Then he stepped into the water beside the nearer lighter.

Ngân moved the owners back from the edge. She did not ask him what he intended. By now she had learned that the answer was usually visible in the direction he told people to stand.

Tín went beneath the bow.

The bottom had settled against the mud. He found a broad beam where the hull could carry the pressure, braced his shoulder beneath it, and lifted until water moved into the space it left.

The bow rose.

The chain connecting the two lighters pulled tight. The second vessel tilted toward the first. Tín shifted his grip and raised the nearer hull farther, drawing both away from the narrow passage.

Men on the landing backed into the office wall.

Chấn stood with his mouth open.

Tín took a step toward the bank.

The lighter scraped across the channel bed. He took another. The chain parted with a report like a rifle shot, and the second vessel swung free into the mud beyond the landing.

Tín carried the first boat sideways until its bow hung over the concrete apron.

Then he lowered it across the passage between the office and the weapons shed.

The concrete cracked. Iron settled with a long, fading groan.

He climbed out of the water.

The channel mouth lay open behind him. The gate had become a small useless object beside an opening large enough for the boats to pass in both directions.

Chấn stared at the stranded hull.

“That was worth money.”

“It still is,” Tín said. “You'll need a better way to move it.”

The mechanic came out of the shed carrying the repaired engine with another man. They set it beside the others. Its owner touched the cover, inspected the connections, and began crying without making a sound.

Nobody commented. Ngân told two men to help him fit it to his boat.

Within minutes the first engines were running.

Tín stood beside the open channel while owners checked fuel and found places for passengers. One of Chấn's guards approached him slowly with the rifle held by its stock and the barrel pointing at the ground.

“What do I do with this?”

“Put it inside. Tell Vũ where it is when he comes.”

The man nodded and went.

Chấn remained beneath the awning.

“And when you die?” he asked.

Tín turned toward him.

“You've asked me that twice now. It's becoming rude.”

“Somebody will take the gate.”

Tín looked at the ruined arrangement of iron, chain, and narrow passage. Then he looked at the boat owners working around it without asking Chấn whether they were allowed to move.

“They'll have to build another.”

He walked down the landing.

Thảo waited in the leading boat. Bảy was at the school with Vũ. Lâm sat behind her with his injured foot raised and a coil of line beside him. He looked ready to defend his presence before anyone questioned it.

Tín stepped aboard.

“You steer?” he asked Lâm.

“Better than I walk.”

“That isn't a demanding standard.”

Lâm grinned and started the engine.

Ngân brought the second boat alongside.

“We go in pairs,” she called to the others. “Leave room to turn. Listen to the people at the landing before you decide you're in a hurry.”

The owners answered one another across the water.

Tín looked toward the old warehouse. Beyond it, the gantry rose above the trees. Wind had begun moving the upper branches in long, coordinated waves.

“Keep behind the bend until I signal,” he told Thảo.

She met his eyes.

“We need the doors open.”

“You'll have them.”

The first boat moved into the channel.


## 17. The Man Who Inherited

The ferry occupied the inner berth behind the warehouse.

From the road, a person could see its upper shelter and mistake it for another part of the building. From the channel, the warehouse wall concealed most of the approach. Phúc had chosen a place where every ordinary view was incomplete.

Tín left the boat beneath the bend and came along the bank alone.

Rain flattened the grass. The old loading gantry stood above the ferry, its cables dark against the sky. He could see the upstream support Bảy had described. The cut had been placed where rust and shadow made it difficult to distinguish from years of neglect.

He kept moving.

Two sentries stood on the upper walkway. A third smoked beneath the stairs. The first two carried guns. The third held his cigarette in the same hand as a knife, an arrangement that told Tín he was more accustomed to making people frightened than to being attacked.

Tín picked up a stone from the bank and threw it through the nearer lamp.

Glass fell into the water.

He crossed the open ground before the sentries finished turning toward the sound. One raised his weapon. Tín jumped the gap between bank and ferry, landed behind him, and struck him once below the ear.

The man dropped. Tín caught the gun before it hit the deck.

The second sentry fired.

Tín folded the first weapon across his wrist, turning the shot into the river. The next blow drove the sentry against the railing hard enough to bend it outward.

Below, the smoker dropped his cigarette and began climbing the stairs with the knife in front of him.

Tín took the top rail in one hand and pulled it down across the man's shoulders. The sentry fell beneath it, striking the steps with his knees and face. The knife went through a gap in the boards and vanished into the water.

The entire exchange lasted less time than the smoke from the dropped cigarette took to disappear in the rain.

Tín left the three men breathing and entered the shelter.

Phúc sat at a table with a bottle of tea and a clean ashtray.

He had changed his shirt since the hall. This one was pale. It made the bruising around his wrist more visible. Three armed men waited behind him, arranged so that anyone entering would have to choose which weapon to watch.

Tín watched the reflections in the window instead.

“You could have sent word,” Phúc said.

“I did. Several times.”

“You sent demands.”

“They were clearly addressed.”

Phúc looked at the ruined gun in Tín's hand.

The old man set it on the table.

“Open the lower deck.”

“You look tired.”

“You look surrounded by men about to regret being here.”

One of the guards shifted his weight. Tín saw the movement in the glass without taking his eyes off Phúc.

“Where is Dưỡng?” the younger man asked.

“With Vũ.”

Phúc's expression changed before he could control it.

“He agreed to that?”

“Eventually.”

“Is he alive?”

“Yes.”

The younger man looked toward the window. Beyond it, the river moved against the ferry in short heavy surges. The escape he had imagined might still have existed somewhere, but the men who would arrange it were becoming fewer.

“Then he told you this was my operation.”

“Bảy told me what he saw.”

Phúc's eyes returned sharply.

Tín let him understand that as well.

“Open it,” he said.

“Do you know what happens if all those people walk away? Contractors come to the houses of men who guaranteed the work. Collectors owe money they can't produce. Boat owners stop paying because they think the whole arrangement has fallen apart.”

“You've understood the last part.”

Phúc rose.

The guards lifted their weapons a little higher.

“My father kept your rules.”

“I know.”

“He died keeping them.”

“He died after breaking an agreement with men who killed for money. You can hate me for refusing to rescue him. Don't make him less responsible to make yourself sound better.”

Phúc's face hardened.

Beneath the floor, someone struck the metal enclosure. Once. Twice. A child's crying stopped between the blows. Whoever was striking the wall was trying to cover the sound.

Tín heard a cough from farther forward and the scrape of a door being moved below.

“Nhuận,” he said. “Where is he?”

“Doing his work.”

“Stop him.”

“He works for me.”

Tín stepped closer to the table.

Phúc put his hand on the ashtray. Thick glass cracked between his fingers.

“You think I don't know what I am?” he said. “Every man I met as a boy told me about you. How you could lift an engine. Break a wall. Hear someone lying before he finished speaking. My father would come home with blood on his clothes and tell me he was doing what you asked.”

Tín remained still.

“Your son got books. Teachers. A house where nobody knocked on the door asking why his father was late with money. I got men telling me I should be grateful to carry your name into rooms you no longer wanted to enter.”

“Hòa carried things you didn't see.”

“Did he collect from a dying man?”

“No.”

“Did he have to?”

“Neither did you.”

For a moment Phúc seemed unable to decide whether to strike him or keep speaking.

“You don't get to say that now.”

“I get to say it while you're keeping children beneath the floor.”

Tín looked past him at a small photograph tucked beneath the clear cover on the table. Phúc stood beside a boy of perhaps six. The child's hand rested against his father's knee. Both faced the camera with the solemnity of people who had been told the picture mattered.

“Your son?” Tín asked.

Phúc moved his hand over the photograph.

“Don't.”

“Where is he?”

“With his mother.”

“Good.”

Tín looked back at his face.

“Leave him there.”

Phúc threw the ashtray.

Tín turned his head. Glass struck the wall behind him. The guards fired as Phúc moved sideways out of their line.

Tín kicked the table upward.

It struck the ceiling, cutting across the firing angles and throwing the bottle of tea against the windows. He took the nearest gunman by the collar and hurled him into the other two. All three struck a steel cabinet. Weapons and men fell together beneath it.

Phúc reached him before the cabinet stopped ringing.

The blow lifted Tín off the floor and drove him through the shelter wall.

He landed on the open deck in heavy rain. Wood splinters slid down his shirt. He drew a breath and felt the injured rib answer with a sharp pressure deep inside his chest.

Phúc came through the hole carrying a section of steel railing torn from its mounts.

Tín rolled as it struck. The deck dented where his head had been.

He rose inside the next swing and drove his elbow into Phúc's jaw. The younger man staggered, spat blood, and caught him around the body.

The grip tightened.

Phúc was stronger than he had been in the hall. Not because strength had appeared overnight, but because he had stopped spending any of it on looking in control. He lifted Tín and drove him backward against the outside of the shelter.

The wall gave.

Tín felt the edge of a support enter the wound across his back. He put both hands against Phúc's temples and struck forward with his forehead.

The younger man's nose broke.

His grip loosened for half a heartbeat. Tín turned inside it, caught one wrist, and drove Phúc's shoulder against the steel upright beside the stairs.

The upright bent. Phúc roared and struck Tín in the side with his free hand.

Below them, people screamed as the impact travelled through the ferry.

Tín heard it and changed his stance.

Phúc noticed.

He began driving him toward the lower stairwell, toward the weakest section of deck above the captives. The younger man was learning the same lesson Dưỡng had used: there were places where Tín would spend strength preventing a blow from continuing rather than delivering the next one.

Tín let him take three steps.

On the fourth, he dropped his weight and pulled Phúc across his hip.

The younger man went over him and struck the solid steel above the engine space, clear of the passenger enclosure. The impact sounded like a loaded cart falling from a height.

Tín followed him down and drove a fist into his ribs.

Phúc caught the next blow and pulled him close. His teeth were red. He struck Tín's throat with the heel of his hand and rolled them both toward the rail.

The old man put one foot against the deck and stopped their movement.

The steel beneath his heel folded.

Phúc looked down at it. That moment cost him. Tín took him beneath the chin, straightened his arm, and lifted him clear of the deck.

The younger man's hands clamped around his wrist.

Behind Phúc's shoulder, one of the gunmen appeared in the broken shelter wall.

Tín turned them both.

The gunman fired into Phúc's back.

The younger man jerked against Tín's hand. His grip loosened. Tín lowered him enough to keep him between the gunman and the opening to the lower deck, then seized the discarded length of railing with his free hand.

He threw it through the gunman's shoulder into the wall behind him.

The weapon fell. The man remained upright for one impossible-looking second, pinned by the iron, then sagged against it.

Phúc struck Tín across the face.

Tín let go of his throat. Blood filled his mouth. He spat it onto the deck and looked at the younger man standing in front of him, swaying but still determined to arrive at the end of the argument with his hands.

“You could have opened a door,” Tín said.

Phúc came at him one last time.

Tín met him without retreating.

He broke the first grip. He turned the second blow. His palm struck Phúc beneath the breastbone, and the younger man's feet left the deck again. Tín caught him before he could fall across the weakened stairwell.

He carried him two paces onto solid steel.

“You don't get to leave this for your son.”

Then he drove him down.

The wooden covering burst apart. The steel beneath it buckled around the shape of the body. Phúc convulsed, tried to draw air, and failed until Tín turned him onto his side.

The next breath came in a wet, painful pull.

Tín waited for another.

It came.

He searched Phúc's pocket and found the keys. There were too many for the locks he could see. The younger man had made a practice of carrying access to other people's rooms.

Tín took them all.

He went to the shelter, pulled the wounded gunman clear of the railing, and laid him where the rain would not enter his mouth. The other two were alive beneath the fallen cabinet. He shifted it enough to free their legs.

“Stay here,” he said.

One nodded without lifting his head.

At the stairs, Tín heard voices below. A man was ordering the captives away from the door. Another voice answered, angry and frightened.

Tín looked toward the bend beyond the warehouse.

He raised one hand and held it there until he saw Thảo answer from the boat.

Then he descended into the lower deck.


## 18. Below the Waterline

The lower enclosure had been made from sheet metal fixed to the ferry's original supports.

It was not a room designed for people. Air came through narrow gaps above head height. Rainwater collected along the floor where the deck sloped toward the side. Someone had put a bucket beneath one leak and given up on the others.

Tín unlocked the outer door.

It opened a hand's breadth and stopped against something inside.

“Move back,” he said.

Nobody answered.

He listened. Breathing. A child trying not to cry. Feet shifting away from the door, then a man's voice ordering them to remain where they were.

“Nhuận.”

The name changed the breathing nearest the door.

“Who is that?” the man called.

“The person opening it.”

Tín put his hand against the metal beside the lock. He pressed slowly, feeling resistance on the other side. A bench had been wedged against the frame. He moved the door just enough to shift it without sending it into the people crowded behind.

The gap widened.

A pistol appeared through it.

Tín caught the barrel and turned it upward. The shot went through the top of the enclosure. He pulled the man holding it against the door and struck his forearm with the edge of his hand.

The pistol fell.

Tín pushed the door open and stepped inside.

People filled the space between the supports. A woman held a sleeping child against her chest with both hands. Two boys crouched beneath a workbench, their knees drawn together above the wet floor. A man with one eye swollen shut stood in front of them, making himself larger because he had nothing else to offer.

Nhuận stood near the far wall.

He was smaller than Tín expected, with a neat haircut and a shirt that had remained dry where everyone else's clothes were damp. A pistol hung at his side. Beside him, another guard held a long knife pointed toward the floor.

The man whose arm Tín had broken lay against the doorframe.

“Upstairs,” Tín told the captives. “Leave your things.”

The woman with the child moved first.

Nhuận raised his pistol.

The guard with the knife stepped between him and the woman.

“Let them go,” he said. “It's finished.”

Nhuận shot him in the head.

The guard fell against the bench. The two boys beneath it screamed. For one instant the entire room seemed to draw inward around the body.

Tín had been moving before the shot, but the crowd stood between him and the gun. He could have crossed the distance by knocking them aside. He had spent that fraction of time finding a route that did not break the people he came to save.

The fraction had belonged to Nhuận.

The man turned the pistol toward the woman again.

Tín threw the keys.

They struck Nhuận's wrist before his finger finished closing. Bone broke beneath the small mass moving at a speed no ordinary hand could have given it. The gun discharged into the floor and flew from his grip.

Tín crossed the room.

Nhuận seized a child by the back of the shirt, trying to pull him into the space between them. Tín caught the child's shoulder first and moved him behind his own body. The boy struck the wall and slid down unhurt except for the terror already inside him.

Nhuận drew a second knife.

Tín took the wrist, turned it, and drove the man against the steel support behind him.

“Hands empty,” he said.

Nhuận spat into his face and struck with his broken arm.

Tín drove his palm beneath the man's jaw.

The neck broke against the support.

Nhuận's body became heavy in his hand. Tín lowered it to the floor and turned toward the room.

Nobody moved.

Blood spread from the dead guard beneath the bench. One of the boys stared at it as if he could stop it reaching his feet by watching closely enough.

Tín crouched beside him.

“Come out.”

The boy shook his head.

“Your mother is at the door.”

The woman standing there held out one hand. Tín had seen her looking from the child in her arms to the two beneath the bench. Now the smaller boy crawled toward her.

The older one stayed.

Tín put his hand on the floor, palm upward, far enough away that the child could refuse it.

“You can hold this,” he said.

The boy looked at the cuts and dried blood across the palm.

Tín turned the hand and offered two fingers instead.

The boy took them.

He led him to the door and placed his hand in the woman's.

Then he stood where everyone could see him.

“The men upstairs are down. Boats are coming. Go one at a time until you reach the stairs, then keep moving.”

This time they obeyed.

A mill worker took the injured guard by his good arm and helped him toward the door. Another captive stopped beside Nhuận's body and looked at the old man.

“Leave him.”

Tín looked down at the dead man.

“Get the living out first.”

The captive nodded and followed the others.

As the room emptied, Tín found another door at the forward end. It had been painted the same colour as the wall. A narrow opening near its top had been covered from outside with a board.

He heard someone on the other side.

The key ring lay beside Nhuận's hand. Tín picked it up, tried the largest key, and found that its teeth had bent in the impact against the man's wrist.

He straightened it between two fingers and tried again.

The lock turned.

Inside, men lay on the floor in a space that smelled of waste and fever. Most had been brought there because they were too injured to be useful elsewhere or too angry to be left among the families. One had tied his own shirt around his knee. Another held a folded cloth against his mouth and coughed into it.

“Can you stand?” Tín asked the nearest.

“Can you open that farther?” the man answered.

Tín pulled the door out of its frame.

“That enough?”

The man looked at the door in his hand.

“I was going to ask about the hinge.”

“It's no longer involved.”

The man gave a short, painful laugh and began helping the person beside him sit up.

Tín moved through the compartment, assessing what each person could do without asking him to prove more than necessary. Two could walk if supported. Three needed carrying. The rest were weak, thirsty, and frightened but able to follow instructions once they believed the instructions led outside.

He took one injured man across both arms and carried him to the stairs.

Lâm stood at the upper landing with a line looped over one shoulder.

“I told you to steer,” Tín said.

“The boat is tied up.”

“Your foot.”

“Still attached. Give him here.”

Another rescuer stood beside him. Together they took the injured man and carried him onto the open deck.

Tín went back for the others.

The ferry shifted while he was inside the forward compartment. A low metallic complaint passed through the roof above him. He stopped long enough to feel the movement under his feet.

The hull had turned farther into the current.

A mooring line struck the outer side with a heavy slap. Then something above the shelter made a dry cracking sound.

He kept moving.

The next man could not bear weight on either leg. Tín lifted him onto the door he had removed. Two of the stronger captives took one end while he took the other. They carried it through the enclosure and up the stairs, keeping it level despite the narrow turn.

Outside, rain struck the man's face. He opened his mouth and let it enter.

Thảo stood beside the ramp with a list in her hand that had become almost useless in the water. She was counting people as they passed, asking names where she could hear them and sending the injured toward the boats with room to carry them flat.

Ngân had brought her boat against the lower side. Another waited behind it. Their owners held the vessels steady with hands and lines while passengers climbed down.

Tín saw the makeshift ramp between the ferry and the old landing. It had begun to twist. The loading gantry above it leaned by an amount that would have escaped notice if Bảy had not described where to look.

“Keep them going,” he told Thảo.

“How many still inside?”

“I'm checking.”

“The guards?”

“Six on the upper deck. Phúc too. One injured below. Two dead.”

She looked at him.

He saw her decide not to ask which two.

“I'll send people for them.”

“Ask. Don't order.”

She nodded.

Tín went back down.

He checked the forward compartment again, then the spaces behind the workbench, the small washing room, the recess beside the original engine access. He found an older man sitting on the floor with both hands over his ears.

The man had heard the shooting and stopped responding to voices.

Tín sat beside him for a moment.

There was no time to explain much. He put his hand where the man could see it and waited until the eyes moved toward him.

“We are going upstairs,” he said.

The man shook his head.

“You don't have to walk.”

Tín lifted him. The man struck his shoulder twice, weakly, then held on.

At the stairs, Lâm took the man's weight with another rescuer. Tín waited until they had him securely before letting go.

He returned to the two bodies.

Two former captives stood beside the dead guard. One had folded a cloth over his face. The other looked toward Nhuận and then away.

“This one tried to stop him,” the first man said.

“I saw.”

They carried the guard upstairs together.

Tín lifted Nhuận and followed.

On the open deck, someone shouted for everyone to get away from the shelter.

The first major support broke.

The sound passed through the ferry as if something enormous had struck it beneath the water. The loading ramp tilted, lifting its shoreward end clear of the landing. Men carrying an injured passenger stopped at the gap and nearly lost their footing.

Tín put Nhuận's body down beside the rail.

He looked up.

The gantry was coming down.


## 19. The Weight of the Crossing

Tín jumped.

His feet struck the base of the concrete pier. His hands met the falling crossbeam. The impact drove him backward and broke the surface beneath his heels.

For a moment the beam continued downward.

Then it stopped.

Forty tonnes of collapsing steel hung against his palms. The gantry twisted above him, carrying its lifting gear and the dead weight of the loading ramp. Its far end still clung to the opposite support. Here, where the broken leg should have been, stood one man.

Tín bent his knees and turned the beam upward.

Concrete split beneath him. He shifted one foot onto the buried edge of the pier, found material that would hold, and pushed again. The crossbeam rose enough to clear the ferry roof.

People on the deck stared at him.

“Move!”

They moved.

The men carrying the injured passenger reached the damaged ramp. Its shoreward end had fallen sideways against the landing, leaving a narrow length of steel over which people could climb if they held the outer rail. A woman went first, turned, and reached for a child.

Thảo stood on the landing side. She took the child, put him into another woman's arms, and guided the next passenger across.

Tín felt the weight change as people left the ferry.

It was not simply the gantry resting on him. The remaining cables connected the ramp to the framework and the framework to a vessel moving in the river. Every person crossing the damaged span altered the load. Every gust pushed the high steel farther from the position in which it had been designed to stand.

He could lift the forty tonnes.

He could not make the pieces remember they were one structure.

He followed the changes through his hands, shifting before one became a failure. An inch at the elbow. Less at the shoulder. A turn through the hips that kept the far beam from pulling the ramp away while three people were on it.

The concrete pier complained beneath his feet.

That was the part he could not strengthen by wanting it harder.

Rain ran down the beam and entered his eyes. He shook his head once, clearing enough water to see the ferry's upper deck. Lâm and another rescuer were carrying a man toward the ramp on a door. The injured man's legs had been tied loosely to keep them from falling over the side.

The door stopped at the narrowest part of the crossing.

“Turn him,” Thảo shouted.

“His back,” Lâm answered.

Tín heard the exchange through the noise of wind striking steel.

“Wait,” he called.

He raised the beam another hand's breadth and moved its nearer end toward the river. The ramp followed enough to open a wider path against the landing.

“Now.”

The men carried the door across without turning it.

The moment their weight left the ramp, he brought the beam back to the position where the remaining support strained least.

Thảo looked at him from the landing. He could see her understanding what he had done and the beginning of a question about whether he could do it for every injured person.

He nodded before she asked.

She turned back to the ferry.

The first boat pushed away from the lower side carrying children and adults who could sit upright. Ngân steered it past a loose mooring line and gave a sharp instruction to the boat waiting behind her. The next owner brought his bow into the space she had left.

They had organized the movement while Tín fought below. He saw the result without having to issue it: boats coming in with room, leaving with people, turning far enough from the berth not to collide with the next arrival.

The river tried to break the pattern at once.

A surge drove the second boat against the ferry. Its owner lost his grip on the line and struck his shoulder against the canopy frame. A passenger began to stand in panic. Another pulled him down.

Ngân brought her departing boat around just long enough to take the second vessel's bow against a padded side. The two owners shouted at one another until their engines and lines agreed on the direction their voices had failed to establish.

Then they separated and continued.

Tín returned his attention to the beam.

The gantry's far support had begun twisting around a weakened connection. He could feel the movement as a slow increase in pressure against his right palm. If he held the nearer end completely still, the far end would tear itself free.

He allowed it to descend a fraction.

The ramp lowered. People crossing it cried out.

“Keep moving,” Thảo told them. “He has it.”

Tín let the far connection settle into a new angle, then raised the near end again.

In his mind, the structure ceased to resemble a gantry. It became a changing arrangement of lengths, loads, and material already damaged beyond the assumptions under which it had been built. He followed each path by which weight could reach the ground. Most ended in something that would fail. A few remained usable if he kept changing which one carried the greatest share.

He moved through those possibilities faster than the water moved beneath the ferry.

His father had once taught him to stand on loose ground by letting each foot learn what the other had found. He had been a boy then, proud of how hard he could strike a post. The old lesson had seemed insulting in its simplicity.

Now the pier broke beneath one heel, and he shifted onto the other before the movement reached his hands.

The beam stayed up.

On the ferry, a man refused to help carry one of the gunmen.

Tín heard the refusal because it came during a pause in the wind.

“He put my son in that room.”

The injured gunman lay on a sheet, conscious enough to hear him. He looked away.

Thảo did not argue with the father. She asked two other men whether they could take the sheet. One agreed. The other shook his head and went to help an older passenger down the ramp instead.

Lâm took the empty corner.

His injured foot touched the deck. Tín saw pain change his face, then leave it as he concentrated on the man he was carrying.

The gunman began trying to say something.

“Save your breath,” Lâm told him. “You're heavier when you talk.”

They brought him across.

A crack opened through the concrete beneath Tín's left foot.

He dropped onto one knee.

The beam lowered abruptly. Above the ferry, one hanging cable snapped tight and tore a strip from the shelter roof. People screamed and crouched.

Tín forced the beam back up.

His knee broke through the pier's outer surface and found the buried reinforcement beneath it. Pain moved through his thigh. The steel inside the concrete bowed against him, but it did not break.

He shifted his knee along it until the load spread across a wider section. Then he stood again.

His shoulder wound opened. Blood ran down his ribs beneath the wet shirt. He could feel the warmth for only a moment before the rain took it away.

He was not weakening in the way the people watching imagined. The muscles remained capable of more. The difficulty was finding somewhere for that strength to go without destroying the thing it was trying to preserve.

He could have thrown the gantry into the river at any time.

The far cable would pull the ferry over with it.

He held still.

Thảo shouted a number from the landing. He heard fifty, then another voice covered the rest. He tried to count the people passing the last visible support but lost the sequence when rescuers returned for the injured.

Some faces appeared twice. Some disappeared into boats below his line of sight. Rain turned the far bank into a grey surface with shapes moving behind it.

He could hold the crossing or watch every person using it.

He could not do both.

“Thảo!”

She looked toward him.

“Count them.”

“We are.”

“Only the captives. Sixty-three.”

She lifted one hand, showing him she understood, then turned back to the people coming down the ramp.

He gave his attention to the beam.

A mooring line broke below the ferry's forward quarter. The hull began to turn into the current. Its movement drew the damaged framework sideways, increasing the pressure against Tín's arms with a speed that made every smaller adjustment useless.

He turned with it.

The pier shed another piece beneath his right heel.

“The lower mooring,” he shouted. “Keep it from turning!”

Ngân heard him. She had returned with her boat empty except for one man who could handle a line. The man threw it to a rescuer on the ferry. It fell short, struck the hull, and vanished into the water.

He pulled it back.

Tín watched the hull move another fraction toward the current.

The second throw reached the ferry.

The rescuer caught it and secured it where Lâm pointed. Ngân turned her engine against the movement. Another boat came beside hers. Together they began pulling the ferry away from the angle that was tearing the gantry loose.

The pressure in Tín's right hand eased.

He lifted the beam higher and allowed the ramp to settle more firmly against the landing.

The next group crossed quickly.

Among them was the older man Tín had found with his hands over his ears. A woman walked beside him, talking steadily. He did not appear to understand her words, but he followed the movement of her arm.

At the end of the ramp he stopped and turned toward the river.

For a moment Tín thought he would try to go back.

Then the man bent and picked up a sandal someone had lost. He gave it to the woman and continued with her.

Thảo called again.

“Sixty-one!”

Tín did not look away from the beam.

He heard feet running on the upper deck. The last two captives had remained to help carry the man on the door. One now supported the other, whose legs had begun shaking too hard to trust the ramp.

They crossed together.

“Sixty-three!”

Thảo raised both arms, then lowered one when she saw the gunmen still being carried from the shelter.

Tín kept holding.

Three men came down the ramp carrying Phúc on another door. Tín recognized two former captives among them. They had stripped cloth from their own shirts to bind his wounds. Phúc's face was turned toward the sky, his eyes half-open beneath the rain.

As they passed, his gaze found Tín.

The younger man looked at the beam above the old man's head.

Something moved in his expression: disbelief, hatred, perhaps the terrible recognition that the stories had been too small rather than too large.

Tín watched the structure instead of answering it.

Phúc was carried onto the landing.

The three sentries came next, two supported and one carried. Behind them the last gunman from the shelter was lifted over the narrow place where the ramp bent. The guard with the broken arm from below followed with a rescuer holding him under the shoulder.

Eight living men from the crew that had held the ferry.

The two bodies remained.

Tín saw the volunteers return for them.

The father who had refused to carry the gunman stood beside the landing. He watched the men go back, then moved forward to hold the end of the ramp steady while they crossed. It was the help he could give. Nobody asked him to call it forgiveness.

The dead guard came over beneath the cloth covering his face.

Nhuận followed.

The ferry's deck emptied.

Thảo remained at the end of the ramp, looking into the shelter, then down the stairs. A rescuer came up from below and held out both hands to show there was no one with him. He said something Tín could not hear.

She made him repeat it.

Then she sent him ashore and crossed after him.

The boats moved away from the berth. Ngân kept her line attached until the last vessel had cleared the falling structure's path. Then she released it and turned into the sheltered channel.

Tín felt the ferry begin to move again.

Thảo stood on the bank above the landing.

“Clear!” she shouted.

He looked at her.

She raised both hands.

He believed her.

Tín turned toward the open river and drove forward.

The far support tore loose. The gantry rose, immense and black against the storm, and rolled away from the landing. Cables snapped free of the empty ferry. Steel struck the water beyond it with a concussion that threw spray above the warehouse roof.

The abandoned vessel tipped after the collapsing framework, broke its remaining line, and settled against the wreck.

For several seconds there was only the sound of water returning to the space the steel had entered.

Tín stood inside the fractured pier with his arms lowered.

His hands were empty.

He stepped onto the bank. The first step was easy. The second required him to remember that the ground no longer needed the exact pressure he had been applying. On the third, his injured knee bent without permission.

He sat in the mud before it could turn the movement into a fall.

Thảo crouched in front of him.

Her mouth moved. He could see her speaking, but it took several seconds for the words to separate from the sound of rain.

“All sixty-three,” she said.

He nodded.

“All the others too.”

He nodded again.

She put a hand against his shoulder and felt the blood beneath the cloth.

“You stupid, impossible old man.”

“I prefer the second part.”

Her face broke into something that was almost a laugh and almost not.

Lâm came up behind her carrying clean cloth. He lowered himself carefully onto the bank, keeping the injured foot out of the mud, and handed it to his sister.

Thảo began binding Tín's shoulder.

Behind them, people were still calling names and receiving answers.


## 20. Every Name Called

The first person to kneel in front of Tín after Thảo was a man he did not recognize.

The man had lost one sandal. Blood ran from a cut beneath his chin. He held both hands together so tightly that the knuckles had gone white.

“My wife,” he said.

Tín tried to understand whether it was a question.

The man pointed toward a woman standing beneath the warehouse awning with three children pressed against her. The older boy still held two fingers out from his hand, as if he had not quite released the grip Tín offered beneath the workbench.

“My wife,” the man said again.

Tín nodded.

The man bowed his head until it nearly touched the mud.

Tín let him finish. He did not mistake gratitude for a form of payment that settled anything between them. When the man raised his face, the old man pointed toward the awning.

“They're waiting.”

The man rose and went to them.

Lâm sat beside Tín, holding the end of the bandage while Thảo tied it. His injured foot had begun to bleed through its wrapping. He kept turning it away from his sister's view.

“I can still see it,” she said.

“Then stop looking.”

“You've been useful for one morning. Don't become expensive for a month.”

Tín looked at the foot.

“She has a professional interest in accounts.”

Lâm gave him an offended look.

“I thought we were on the same side.”

“Only against people making worse decisions.”

Thảo finished the knot. She sat back on her heels and looked at both of them as if deciding whether either could be trusted not to stand the moment she turned away.

“Stay,” she said.

They stayed until she had walked far enough not to notice Tín shifting onto his good knee.

Lâm caught his sleeve.

“She'll know.”

“I'm not planning to hide successfully.”

The old man stood.

His legs carried him. The world remained where it belonged. He took a few slow steps to let his body discover that there was no longer a collapsing structure above it requiring constant correction.

People moved aside as he approached the warehouse awning. Most did it before they consciously recognized him. They had seen something their bodies understood more quickly than their thoughts could arrange.

He stopped beside the injured.

The woman from the clinic was working beneath a sheet held against the rain by two men. She looked up and saw him standing.

“No,” she said.

“I haven't asked anything.”

“You were about to.”

“Who needs carrying?”

She considered him, then pointed at a man lying on a door.

“To the larger launch. Slowly. If you use him to demonstrate something, I'll find a part of you that still hurts.”

Tín lifted the door with two other men. He let them determine the pace. The patient watched his face while they carried him, then closed his eyes as if recognizing the arrangement had relieved him of one remaining task.

Vũ stood beside the launch with an officer recording names.

The inspector looked toward the ruined berth. The gantry lay half-visible in the river. The ferry rested against it at an angle from which it would never carry passengers again.

He turned back to Tín.

“You found them.”

“Sixty-three.”

“Thảo told me.”

“Eight alive from Phúc's crew. Two dead.”

Vũ nodded. “We're accounting for them.”

Phúc lay on a stretcher farther along the landing. An officer stood beside him while another helped bind the wound in his back. He was conscious intermittently. Each time he opened his eyes, he looked first toward the ferry and then toward Tín.

The old man went to him.

Phúc tried to speak.

Tín bent close enough to hear.

“You should have killed me.”

“You tried very hard to arrange it.”

The younger man's mouth moved in something that could have been a smile if it had not hurt him so much.

“They'll tell the story.”

“They've already started.”

Phúc looked toward the families beneath the awning.

“My son will hear it.”

Tín remained beside him.

“Then leave him enough truth to know which parts belong to you.”

The younger man closed his eyes. Tín did not know whether he had listened or merely run out of strength to answer.

He stepped away so the men could lift the stretcher.

The two bodies lay beneath separate coverings.

One was Nhuận. The other was the guard who had stepped in front of the woman. A fellow guard, his broken arm supported against his chest, stood beside the second body.

“Bình,” he said when Vũ asked the name.

The inspector wrote it down.

“Family?”

The guard named a landing and a house behind a shop. He began to explain something about why Bình had taken the job, then stopped when he saw that explanation would not make the body move.

“Tell them he stopped,” Tín said.

Vũ looked at him.

“He refused to let Nhuận shoot a woman. Nhuận killed him.”

The injured guard stared at Tín, then nodded rapidly.

“That's what happened.”

The inspector wrote that down too.

The last boats left the old landing in groups. Ngân remained until the people still on shore could fit into the vessels waiting for them. She counted seats, argued with one owner who wanted to overload his boat, and made another return for a woman he had assumed was going with someone else.

Tín watched the correction happen. Nobody treated it as a failure. They changed the arrangement and continued.

He had spent much of his life believing that a command became weaker when someone improved it in public.

Ngân appeared to regard improvement as part of the fare.

She brought him back with Lâm and Thảo in the final boat.

The engine sounded rough from the work it had done against the ferry's weight. Ngân listened to it with the expression of a person hearing an expensive complaint she intended to address later.

“It held,” Tín said.

“So did you. I'm not giving either of you permission to make that the normal working condition.”

They passed the open connector beside Chấn's office. The stranded lighter lay where Tín had put it. Chấn stood under the awning with two officers. He watched the rescued families pass without calling out about fees.

Tín met his eyes for a moment.

Chấn looked away first.

At the schoolhouse, the courtyard filled with people arriving from different directions at once.

Some had followed the boats. Others came when the names were carried to their landings. They brought dry clothes, food, blankets, photographs of people who had not returned. The rooms could not hold everyone, so neighbours opened houses along the path.

Tín sat on a bench while the clinic woman cleaned his shoulder again. Hòa stood nearby with a basin. Bích had come with Hương and a bundle of clean clothes, but she remained a few paces away while the wound was being examined.

Tín looked toward her.

She raised one hand to show she had seen him looking. It was not yet an invitation to speak.

Mai stood beside her mother holding a bag of cups.

She had heard the story before she saw him. He could tell from the way her eyes moved from his face to his hands and then to the ordinary bench beneath him.

Hòa noticed too.

“Give her something to do,” he told his wife quietly.

Hương sent Mai with the cups to the classroom. The girl went, grateful for an instruction that did not require deciding what her grandfather had become.

Liên arrived near midday.

She carried folded shirts from Sơn's cupboard. Some would fit the men who had come ashore with nothing dry to wear. She gave the bundle to Hương and came to stand in front of Tín.

For a while neither spoke.

Then she looked at the wound being cleaned.

“He would have complained you waited until he couldn't see it.”

Tín looked up at her.

“He saw enough.”

“He'd have disagreed.”

She sat beside him, leaving room for the woman treating his shoulder. Across the courtyard, Cường's mother was helping serve food. The young collector himself remained under watch while his leg was treated, but his mother had come because she knew what she could carry without anyone explaining the terms.

Liên watched her.

“You told her.”

“Mai found her with a relative.”

“Good.”

She smoothed the edge of her sleeve.

“The boy sent a message. Says he doesn't know how to repay it.”

Tín waited.

“I told him to become less fucking stupid. It would be a start.”

For the first time since Sơn died, Tín laughed without immediately regretting the sound.

Liên looked at him and let herself smile for one brief moment.

Then a woman near the gate began calling a name, and someone inside the classroom answered it. The smile changed into something harder to see clearly.

Bích came over when the bandaging was finished.

She took the clean shirt from Hòa, shook it open, and held it while Tín put his arms through. The left shoulder did not move easily. She waited without pulling.

“I have another after this,” she said. “Then you'll be borrowing from your son.”

“His sleeves are narrow.”

“That will encourage you to stop tearing them.”

She fastened the upper button herself and sat beside him after Liên rose to help with the clothing.

For a long time they watched people enter and leave the rooms.

“Did you think you would die?” she asked at last.

“Not when I was holding it.”

“When?”

He considered the question honestly.

“At the house this morning. Before I left the first time. I thought about not coming back.”

She looked at his hands resting on his knees.

“And then you went.”

“Yes.”

“I know.”

He turned toward her.

She kept watching the courtyard.

“I don't need you to tell me it had to be done,” she said. “I need you to understand that knowing that doesn't make waiting a small thing.”

He took her hand.

She let him hold it, though she did not lean toward him or make the conversation easier by smiling.

Vũ came to the bench after the last stretcher had been moved.

“Tomorrow,” he said. “If you're able.”

Tín knew what he meant.

“Here?”

“We can use the office. There will be fewer people listening through the wall.”

“There should be someone taking it down.”

“There will be.”

The inspector looked at Bích, then back at Tín.

“You understand I'm asking about your part as well.”

“I understood the first time.”

Vũ nodded and went back to his officers.

Bích stood.

“Then tonight you come home.”

Tín let her help him rise.

He did not need the strength of her arm. He took the help anyway.


## 21. What He Would Say

Tín put on a clean shirt and went to the office the next morning.

He did not take men with him. Hòa came because Vũ had asked about the records from the house, carrying the cloth bag in which he had first brought the books to the school. Bích walked with them as far as the road, then turned toward Liên's house with a basket of food.

“I'll find my way home,” Tín told her.

“That has never been the part that worried me.”

She continued without waiting for his answer.

The office stood above the flood line. Its lower walls held marks from years when the water had risen farther. A young officer showed them into a room with a long table and several chairs that had been repaired with different kinds of wood.

Tín tested the nearest one with a hand before sitting.

Hòa watched him.

“Now you're cautious about furniture.”

“I've had experience.”

Vũ entered with another man who would take down the statement. He placed the previous notes on the table, explained what they were going to cover, and asked Tín to correct anything he heard that was wrong.

“Even if the correction makes it worse for me?” Tín asked.

“Especially then. Otherwise we'll have to come back to it.”

The old man nodded.

They began with the founding arrangements of the Compact.

Tín described the first loans. He had bought shares in boats because moving goods was more reliable than waiting for other men to bring them. When an engine failed, he advanced money for the repair. When a boat owner could not pay, Tín took a larger share of the earnings. It had seemed efficient to keep the arrangement in one set of hands.

“Your hands,” Vũ said.

“Yes.”

“And if someone refused?”

“I stopped the boat working.”

“How?”

“I held the engine. The hull. Sometimes the cargo.”

The man writing paused.

“Held in storage?”

Tín looked at him.

“Usually.”

The man resumed writing.

Vũ asked about the collectors. Tín gave their names where he remembered them, the places they worked, the instructions he gave them. He distinguished what he had ordered directly from what he learned after it happened. When he did not know which man struck a borrower, he said so. When he had been present, he did not describe the event as if it had occurred somewhere outside his reach.

Hòa took the oldest volume from the bag.

Tín opened it at the instruction about the next of kin.

The handwriting looked smaller beneath the office light than it had in the school. He remembered leaning over that page, irritated because a dispute had taken half a day he wanted to spend elsewhere. Writing a rule had made the interruption feel finished.

“This was mine,” he said.

Vũ read the lines.

“You intended the family to assume the balance.”

“I intended to be paid.”

“That isn't the same answer.”

“It's the answer I used to avoid the other one.”

Hòa looked toward the window. Tín let him look away without trying to call him back into the conversation.

The questions continued.

They spoke about Bảy's father. Tín used the name Bảy had given him and described the boat he had held. He remembered the repair, the disputed amount, the place the vessel sat after it was taken out of service.

“Did you know what the family used to live on after that?” Vũ asked.

“No.”

“Did you ask?”

“No.”

The man taking the statement wrote both answers separately.

Tín watched him do it.

There had been years when he would have objected to the repetition. He would have called it unnecessary emphasis. Now it seemed useful that the two failures remained distinct.

They spoke about Tòng's message, which Tín had not received, and the properties whose payments continued after his retirement. Hòa provided dates from the household accounts. Some contradicted Tín's recollection by a month or a season. He accepted the corrections.

“Why did you leave?” Vũ asked.

Tín sat back.

The question brought him to an afternoon in 1975, at the edge of a boatyard that no longer existed in the same shape.

He had been fifty-three. Dưỡng was forty and already more patient with complicated machinery than with the men who used it. Sơn stood beside a loading platform, waiting for Tín to decide what should happen to a boat whose owner had missed three payments.

The owner said his wife had been ill.

Tín believed him. The belief did not alter the sum.

There were men watching, and he had become accustomed to thinking of every exception as a lesson the watchers would misuse. If he let one obligation slide because a household had suffered, another man would arrive with a story of suffering. Eventually his business would consist of listening to stories while money remained elsewhere.

That was how he arranged the problem in his head.

The owner offered to work at the yard until the difference was paid. Tín refused because the man knew boats better than machinery and would be less useful ashore. He told him to find another source of money.

“With what?” the man asked.

Tín was already turning away.

The man caught his sleeve.

Sơn moved to pull him back, but Tín lifted a hand. He took the boat owner by the wrist and removed the fingers from his shirt one at a time. He did it slowly enough for everyone watching to understand that the slowness was a choice.

Then he walked to the vessel, stepped beneath its raised bow, and lifted it out of the shallows.

The boat came ashore with water pouring from its hull.

He set it beyond the reach of an ordinary launching crew and told the owner it would remain there until payment arrived.

The man's wife had been waiting under a tree. She stood when the boat left the water, but she did not approach. Tín remembered her face only later, after he had begun to understand that she had been measuring what kinds of appeal remained possible and finding none.

That evening he went to Hòa's house.

Mai had been born only days earlier. Hương lay resting while Bích helped prepare food. Hòa stood at the door with an expression Tín had never seen on his son: pride so intense it made him look frightened.

He put the baby in his father's arms.

Tín remembered the weight. He had lifted a boat that afternoon and could recall almost nothing about the effort. The small body against his forearm required an attention the boat never had. He changed the angle of his wrist because Mai's head had turned slightly. He stood still when she settled.

Hòa watched him.

“You can sit,” his son said.

“I know.”

“You look as if someone ordered you not to move.”

“Someone has.”

Hòa smiled, then stopped when he saw the blood on his father's cuff. It was only a small mark. The boat owner's wrist had split where Tín's thumb pressed it against a rough edge of cloth.

“Whose?” Hòa asked.

Tín looked at the cuff.

“Business.”

His son's face changed.

He did not reach for the baby immediately. That would have been easier for Tín to resent. Instead he stayed where he was and let his father hold the child while the answer remained between them.

“Don't use that word when you mean a person,” Hòa said.

Tín remembered almost answering sharply. He had come to visit his granddaughter, not be corrected in a doorway by a man whose house he helped pay for.

Then Mai opened one hand and closed it around his finger.

He had been strong enough to make most questions leave the room. He could not imagine using that strength on the small demand holding his hand.

He told the office he was retiring within the month.

The decision was not as generous as he later made it sound to himself. He wanted mornings at home. He wanted his wife to stop watching the road when he was late. He wanted Hòa to bring the child without measuring his mood first. He wanted to keep the buildings and receive the income while somebody else dealt with the details that arrived carrying faces.

In the office in 1992, Vũ waited.

“I wanted to be left alone,” Tín said.

Hòa looked back from the window.

“Was that all?” the inspector asked.

“No. But it was the part I arranged most successfully.”

They took a break when the man writing needed to replace his pen.

Hòa went into the corridor. Tín followed after a minute and found him standing beside the water jar.

“I remember the cuff,” his son said.

“I know.”

“I didn't know about the boat.”

Tín stood beside him.

For several seconds they listened to someone moving chairs in another room.

“I thought if you didn't enter the business,” Tín said, “I had kept it from you.”

Hòa looked at the bag of books through the open door.

“I still lived in its house.”

“Yes.”

“And Mai.”

“Yes.”

His son poured water into two cups and handed him one.

“I don't want you deciding that means her future belongs to your punishment.”

Tín turned toward him.

“It doesn't.”

“You make large decisions quickly. Then the rest of us discover which rooms we're expected to leave.”

The old man held the cup between both hands.

“Tell me before I do it.”

“I'm telling you now.”

Tín nodded.

They returned to the table.

The afternoon concerned the recent events. The forged thumbprint. The worker transfers. The attacks. The island yard. Dưỡng's dredger. The ferry. Tín described what he had seen and done, including the men he killed. He did not turn the account of violence into a contest over who had been most frightened or most justified.

Vũ asked where other witnesses stood. Hòa marked names to be contacted. The statement became longer than Tín expected because actions he remembered as one movement contained several decisions when someone asked him to say them aloud.

Near evening, the man taking it down read the account back.

Tín corrected a place name. He changed one sentence that made it sound as though Dưỡng had fallen accidentally. He added that he had given Lan demands before knowing how many people she could locate and that the first six men were released after she made the calls.

“You want that included?” Vũ asked.

“It happened.”

The inspector nodded.

Tín signed where he was asked to sign.

His handwriting was steady. He took no satisfaction in that. A steady hand had produced the earlier rules too.

Vũ gathered the pages.

“There will be more questions.”

“I know.”

“Other people will decide what follows from these answers.”

“That's why I came here.”

The inspector looked at him for a moment, then stood.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Water dripped from the roof into a line of small holes it had made in the ground. Hòa carried the bag of books, lighter now because some would remain to be copied and examined.

They walked home together.

At the turn toward the house, a man came up the road carrying a basket on his shoulder. He recognized Tín, hesitated, then moved aside.

Tín moved aside too.

The man passed between them and continued without knowing what to make of it.

Hòa watched him go.

“You'll confuse people.”

“They've been managing.”

His son smiled faintly.

When they reached the house, Bích had food on the table and Mai had spread three notebooks across the space Tín usually occupied. She looked up as they entered.

“I can move them.”

Tín took another chair.

“Finish what you're doing.”

She bent over the page again. Hòa set the bag down beneath the table, where the books could wait until everyone had eaten.


## 22. No Balance Carried

Three weeks after the ferry came down, Tín returned to the room above the restaurant with fewer possessions than he had owned when he first called the meeting there.

The room looked unchanged. The pencil still lay on the window ledge. The owner had repaired the hinge Dưỡng disturbed. A fresh sheet covered the table, and the spare chair near the stairs still rocked when anyone leaned backward in it.

This time Hòa asked the owner for another table.

“For the papers,” he said.

Tín looked at the amount of space already available.

“We're not buying the restaurant.”

“Then stop spreading your elbows over the work.”

Lan arrived carrying a bag of accounts and a separate envelope containing her own statement. She had given it to Vũ already and brought a copy because people had begun asking what she claimed not to know. The bruise on her wrist had faded to a yellow mark. She wore the same controlled expression, but she no longer waited for men to finish inventing polite descriptions of what they had done.

“If anyone says the office made the decision,” she told Hòa, “ask which person held the pen.”

“Including you?”

“Especially me. Otherwise they'll give me everything they don't want to remember.”

They began with the payments that could be established without argument.

Some families had receipts. Others had witnesses who remembered making a payment together or lending the money used to make it. The old books from Tín's house confirmed parts of earlier accounts. More recent evidence came from the people who had handled the transactions and the materials Vũ's men obtained during their investigation.

No single book contained the whole arrangement. It had lasted partly because every person could point toward something held by someone else.

Tín watched the missing pieces meet on Hòa's table.

He could calculate a balance faster than his son could write down the figures. At first he kept answering while Hòa was still explaining the entries to the family sitting opposite them. After the third interruption, Hòa put down his pencil.

“I know you have the answer.”

“Then why are we still adding?”

“Because they need to know how we reached it.”

Tín looked at the couple across the table. The husband had been nodding whenever the old man spoke, but his wife still held their receipts in the same tight stack she brought into the room.

“Continue,” Tín said.

Hòa did. By the time he finished, the woman had separated the receipts into the order in which the payments had actually been made. She pointed out one entry her husband had forgotten. The balance changed.

Tín considered the new figure and nodded.

His son did not look at him for approval before writing it down.

The engine yard was the first major property Tín committed toward restitution. He could not simply sell it vacant. People lived there, and the fire had destroyed rooms he had allowed to remain unsafe while collecting rent on them. Hương insisted the housing be repaired before any transfer and that the people living in it understand what would happen next.

They met beneath the unfinished roof with the tenants, a builder, and a prospective buyer who had hoped the discussion would concern only the price of the land.

It did not.

“I can make more use of that side,” the buyer said, pointing toward the rooms.

“People are using it now,” Hương answered.

“I mean commercially.”

“They pay rent. That was commercial enough when the roof leaked.”

Tín stood beside her and watched the buyer reconsider which person should receive his argument.

The man turned toward him.

“You understand a sale requires flexibility.”

“I'm being flexible about whether I sell to you.”

The buyer looked back at the rooms.

They worked out an arrangement the tenants could read and question before agreeing. It did not solve every future dispute. It did establish who could remain, what repairs would be made, and which promises were part of the sale rather than things said kindly while everyone stood in the rain.

Hương had the important passages read aloud twice.

Tín signed after the tenants had finished asking questions.

The money went into accounts administered for specific payments instead of being put into his hands to distribute according to his mood. He disliked the delay until he saw a woman receive the amount agreed while he was absent from the room. Nobody had needed to wait for him to return from the river.

Tòng came to the restaurant with his granddaughter and a tin containing the receipts she had persuaded him to keep.

He placed it on the table.

“She labelled it,” he told Tín. “In case I forgot that the tin with all the money papers contained the money papers.”

His granddaughter opened the lid.

“Last month it contained nails.”

“Useful things.”

“They went through a receipt.”

“Then we know that one is real. Nobody forges a hole.”

Lan took the papers before the argument could develop into an account of every object Tòng had ever stored badly.

The repayment he made after selling the boat was there. So were later demands that ignored it. Some amounts had been collected by men no longer available to answer questions. Others had reached people whose names Tín could still identify.

He named them.

Tòng listened with both hands resting on the walking stick.

“I don't need the upper floor back exactly as it was,” he said when they discussed the amount.

His granddaughter looked at him.

“I want a room where she doesn't have to move the table to lie down,” he continued. “And stairs that don't require somebody to pull me up by the elbows.”

Tín nodded.

“We'll have someone look at the house.”

“Someone who builds houses. Not you. You'll decide a wall is in the way and move it into the river.”

“You used to enjoy that sort of improvement.”

“I used to have more walls.”

The granddaughter smiled despite herself.

Tín had a builder visit that afternoon. He did not go with him.

Other claims were less straightforward.

A woman brought an account her husband had told her was paid. Two witnesses remembered lending him money for the payment. A third remembered seeing him at the gambling room that evening. The receipt he gave his wife did not match the collector's handwriting.

The husband had since disappeared from the district.

Lan began separating what could be proved from what remained uncertain. The woman listened with her face growing harder.

“So because he lied to me, they can still take the boat.”

“No,” Tín said.

Hòa looked at him.

The old man kept his attention on the woman.

“We know they took payments after the date they claim the boat was already theirs. We can settle that part. The rest needs answers we don't have yet.”

“And while you look?”

“Nobody from my old arrangement comes to take it.”

Lan wrote down the disputed portions separately. Hòa made sure the woman left with copies and understood which decisions had been made and which had not.

After she went, his son turned toward him.

“You nearly decided the whole thing because she was frightened.”

“She had reason.”

“So did the next person outside. If you decide differently depending on who is sitting in the chair, we're building the same office with better intentions.”

Tín looked at the door.

“Then stop me when I do it.”

Hòa tapped the pencil against the table.

“I seem to have found a second job.”

Lan looked between them.

“Make him pay overtime. He respects an invoice.”

There was not enough money to replace everything taken.

Tín knew this before the first sale was complete. He had entered old accounts into memory with the same precision he applied to a machine under load. The sums were manageable. The years were not. A boat returned now could not supply meals missed ten years earlier. A rebuilt room did not bring back a woman who had died in the smaller house. Some families refused to meet him at all.

He did not send men to persuade them.

He made arrangements through people they trusted and accepted that the answer might remain no.

At home, the smaller household account took shape beside the larger reckoning. Bích identified savings from businesses she had run herself and money inherited from her own family. Hương and Hòa set out what they earned and what Mai's schooling would require. Tín brought the income he could trace without asking everyone to accept his confidence as proof.

For the first time in years, they discussed a purchase without the conversation ending when he said he would cover it.

He found it slower.

He also found that Hương had noticed three expenses he had not considered and Bích had negotiated a price he would have paid without question.

“You should have been at the office,” he told his wife.

“I was at an office. It was called keeping this house standing.”

He accepted that without asking her to soften it.

One evening a collector returned to a house near the mill.

He said the recent confusion had not cancelled private obligations. He brought a second man and waited until the husband went outside. The wife sent her eldest child through the back door to the neighbours.

By the time Tín heard about it, six adults had come from the surrounding houses and stood along the path. One carried a lantern. Another had the copies Hòa helped prepare. A third had already gone to fetch an officer.

The collector left before the officer arrived.

Tín went there the next morning.

The husband met him beside the boat and described what happened. His wife corrected the part where he made himself sound calmer than he had been. The neighbours came over and corrected other parts.

Tín listened to the account improve through argument.

“Did he threaten to return?” he asked.

“He said we'd regret embarrassing him.”

“Name?”

They gave it. Tín knew the man.

He found him that afternoon at a store near the road.

The collector saw him enter and set down the cup he was holding.

Tín took the chair opposite.

“You're frightening people again.”

“I have a claim.”

“Then bring it to the people examining claims.”

“It's private.”

“Not after you take a man to someone's house at night.”

The collector looked toward the store owner, who had stopped arranging bottles on a shelf.

Tín leaned back.

“I'm not going to break anything today if you can manage the same.”

The man swallowed.

“You can't watch every house.”

“Apparently I don't need to.”

He left the man sitting with an untouched cup.

On the way home, Tín passed the new crossing. A boat owner was arguing with Ngân over whose passengers had reached the landing first. She pointed out that the boat owner had arrived early only because he abandoned a load he had promised to collect.

The argument had nothing to do with Phúc, the Compact, or Tín.

He stopped long enough to buy a packet of food from a stall. The woman counted his change twice, caught her own mistake, and gave him the missing coin.

He put it in his pocket and continued home.


## 23. The Last Claim

Phúc's wife came to the schoolhouse on a morning when most of the remaining work concerned where people would sleep after they stopped sleeping there.

She held her son's hand. The boy wore clean sandals and a shirt buttoned incorrectly at the collar. He looked around the courtyard with the suspicious interest of a child who had been brought somewhere after adults lowered their voices about it.

Tín recognized him from the photograph.

The woman introduced herself as Thu.

“I know who you are,” he said.

“You knew who my husband was. We haven't met.”

He inclined his head.

“Then sit. Both of you.”

She took the bench beneath the awning. The boy remained standing against her knee until she told him he could sit beside her.

“Nghĩa,” she said when Tín looked at him.

The boy gave the greeting his mother expected, then stared at Tín's hands.

Thu saw where he was looking and put a hand over one of his.

“Phúc asked whether I had come,” she said.

“To see him?”

“To see you.”

Tín waited.

“He thinks you will decide what happens to us.”

“He thinks too many things still belong to one man's decision.”

Her mouth tightened.

“That didn't begin with him.”

“No.”

The boy shifted beside her. Tín looked toward the classroom where Hương was folding blankets. She saw him glance and came over.

“Would you help me count these?” she asked Nghĩa.

The boy looked at his mother. Thu nodded. He followed Hương inside, still looking back at Tín until the doorway hid him.

Thu waited until he was out of earshot.

“I knew men were afraid of my husband,” she said. “I knew money arrived with stories nobody finished when I entered a room. He said it was collections. He said everyone complained when they owed.”

Tín listened.

“Some nights I believed him. Some nights I preferred believing him. I won't pretend those are the same.”

“What do you need?”

“To know which things in the house belong to people who will come asking for them. To know whether the boy can stay with my mother without someone deciding he's a way to reach his father. And to know what I should tell him when he asks why everyone looks at us.”

Tín considered the questions separately.

“Vũ can tell you what is being examined. Hòa can help you make a list of what you brought into the marriage and what came later. As for anyone using your son, send word if somebody approaches him.”

“To you?”

“To Vũ. To the school. To me if I'm nearest. Don't wait because you think there is only one person allowed to answer.”

She looked toward the classroom.

“And what I tell him?”

“The truth you know. In pieces he can carry.”

Thu rubbed her thumb along the edge of her sleeve.

“Phúc was kind to him.”

“Then that's true too.”

For the first time her controlled expression faltered.

“People act as if saying that means I'm asking them to forget everything else.”

“They may be afraid you are.”

“I'm not.”

Tín nodded.

He did not offer her money before she asked about the immediate bills. When she did, he told her which medical costs were already being covered and which questions she should take to the people administering the payments. She wrote the names down in a small notebook.

The notebook was carefully ruled. He recognized the impulse to make a difficult life fit inside columns and did not comment on it.

Nghĩa returned carrying two folded towels, which Hương had apparently decided constituted a useful amount of responsibility.

He put them on the bench beside his mother and looked at Tín.

“Did you hit my father?”

Thu closed the notebook.

Tín answered the boy directly.

“Yes.”

“Hard?”

“Hard.”

Nghĩa considered this.

“He said nobody could.”

“He was wrong.”

The boy's face changed. Tín saw the hurt arrive before the question that would explain it.

“Is he going to die?”

“He's alive. People are treating his injuries. Your mother has seen him.”

Nghĩa looked at Thu. She nodded and put an arm around him.

“Why did you hit him?”

“He was hurting people and would not stop.”

The boy leaned against his mother. He did not ask another question immediately. Tín let the silence remain small and ordinary instead of filling it with an explanation meant to make adults more comfortable.

After a while Nghĩa pointed at the bandage still visible beneath Tín's collar.

“Did he hit you?”

“Several times.”

Something like relief entered the boy's face. His father had not vanished completely from the story he knew how to understand.

“Did it hurt?”

“Yes.”

Nghĩa looked satisfied with that answer in a way that made Thu close her eyes briefly.

Tín did not take the small satisfaction away from him.

He saw them to the gate. One of the boy's sandals had come loose. Thu began to bend, but Tín crouched first and fastened the strap.

Nghĩa watched the large hands perform the small task.

“Can you lift a boat?” he asked.

“Some boats.”

“My father said he could.”

“He was strong.”

The boy nodded, storing the answer where it could coexist with the others.

Thu took his hand and led him along the road.

Tín watched until they turned out of sight.

That afternoon he went to see Dưỡng.

The younger man had been moved from the clinic once his condition allowed it. His shoulder remained bound, and one wrist rested in a support. An officer stayed in the room while they spoke. Dưỡng appeared to regard the arrangement as inconvenient rather than surprising.

“You're getting around,” he said.

“So are people who were waiting for your permission.”

Dưỡng looked at the chair offered to Tín.

“Sit before you make this a speech.”

Tín sat.

The room had a high window and a table with one short leg. Someone had folded paper beneath it to stop the rocking. Dưỡng kept looking at the unevenness as if it were an insult placed there for his particular benefit.

“You could ask them to fix it,” Tín said.

“I did.”

“Apparently you were less persuasive than usual.”

Dưỡng gave him a thin smile.

They spoke first about the dredger. The surviving crew had described the damage and the repairs needed before it could work again. Its ownership and use were being examined along with the companies tied to it. Several men feared they would lose honest wages because their employer had used the machine for dishonest purposes.

“They should keep working,” Dưỡng said.

“Then answer the questions about which work belongs to them.”

“You think I haven't?”

“I came to find out.”

Dưỡng looked toward the officer, then back at Tín.

“The bank repairs were real. The dredging was real. I didn't invent every need because I profited from it.”

“No.”

“Say that when somebody asks.”

“I have.”

The younger man's face changed slightly. He had prepared for an argument and found himself holding its first sentence without somewhere to put it.

Tín let him keep it for a moment.

“You also narrowed a route so the people using it would need your gate,” he said. “You bought labour from men who held families. Both things will be said.”

Dưỡng looked down at the supported wrist.

“Phúc became careless.”

“You became confident you could use that.”

“I had used worse men.”

“Yes.”

The old partnership sat between them, stripped of enough courtesy that neither could pretend the other was talking about someone else.

Dưỡng leaned back carefully.

“Were you always that much stronger?”

Tín considered the question.

“You were faster at finding something that would break.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

“Then yes.”

Dưỡng laughed once, which hurt his shoulder and made him stop.

“You could have been modest for the sake of an injured man.”

“It would have worried you. You'd think I was planning something.”

The younger man looked at him for a long time.

“I thought the years had done more.”

“They did plenty.”

“Not enough.”

Tín rested his hands on his knees. The cuts had mostly closed. Faint new marks crossed older scars, and the skin had regained the ordinary appearance that made his strength seem like a lie until something large began to move.

“You stopped resisting,” he said.

Dưỡng glanced toward the officer again.

“You wanted me to tear through the boat on the way back?”

“You could have tried.”

“And then what? Kill men whose names I don't know? Hide until you found me? Watch every honest part of the companies collapse while I proved I could still break a door?”

He looked at the rocking table.

“I'm capable of calculating a loss.”

Tín nodded.

He believed the answer. It was not repentance, and it did not need to be disguised as repentance to explain why the man remained seated.

“People will keep making claims,” Dưỡng said. “Some true. Some useful to them. You won't be able to tell the difference by looking at a face.”

“No.”

“That used to irritate you.”

“It still does.”

Dưỡng smiled faintly.

“Good. I was afraid they'd make you pleasant.”

The officer looked down at his notes. Tín suspected he was hiding a smile and respected the effort.

When it was time to leave, Dưỡng asked one more question.

“What happens to the old crossing?”

“The wreck comes out. The channel opens. People decide what they need there.”

“And you?”

“I'll help move the steel.”

“Of course you will.”

Tín stood.

Dưỡng lowered his head once, a small acknowledgement made without witnesses he needed to impress. Tín returned it and left the room.

On the way home he stopped at Liên's house.

Cường sat outside with his injured leg stretched along a bench. His mother had brought him to speak to Liên after arranging it with the people supervising his treatment and questioning. He looked as if he would rather face Tín at the ticket office again than complete the conversation waiting beyond the door.

“She's making tea,” he said when Tín approached.

“That sounds survivable.”

“I don't know what to say.”

“She'll notice if you invent something.”

The young man looked down at his hands.

“I came there to frighten people.”

“Yes.”

“He knew.”

“Yes.”

“And he still—”

The sentence stopped.

Tín sat at the other end of the bench.

“You don't have to make it a puzzle he died to answer. Tell her what happened. Answer the questions people ask you. Then decide what you do when someone offers you that work again.”

Cường nodded, though the movement looked more like an attempt to keep his face still than agreement.

Liên opened the door with three cups on a tray.

She looked at Tín.

“You're early.”

“I wasn't expected.”

“Exactly. Sit somewhere that isn't in the way.”

He moved to a stool beside the wall.

Liên put a cup in front of Cường. The young man looked at it, then at her.

“I'm sorry,” he said.

She sat opposite him.

“Tell me what you remember.”

Tín stayed while the young man began.


## 24. The River Works

Bảy made Tín wait while he checked the lines.

The old man stood on the bank with his sleeves rolled up. The wounds across his hands had healed into pale marks. His shoulder remained stiff when he first rose in the morning, but the stiffness left once he moved. He had stopped pretending to rest after Bích found him carrying a heavy water jar with the arm she had been told he should spare.

Now the salvage crew moved around the remains of the old landing, and Bảy refused to let him touch anything until the men in the boats had confirmed where the submerged steel lay.

“I can see the beam,” Tín said.

“You can see one end.”

“I held the other.”

“Then you'll appreciate the opportunity to inspect it.”

Tín waited.

The gantry lay diagonally across the berth, half in the water and half caught against the wrecked ferry. Some pieces could be cut free and moved separately. Others held the wreck in a position that would change as soon as their weight was removed. A man strong enough to lift them did not eliminate the need to know what would fall afterward.

Bảy had explained this to the crew before Tín arrived.

Tín approved of the explanation and disliked hearing it repeated for his benefit.

The mechanic noticed.

“You hired me to be irritating about the details,” he said.

“I thought it was included.”

“That was the informal service. Now you're paying.”

He went back to the edge and called to the men in the nearest boat.

Vũ had finished the examinations required at the wreck. Things belonging to the captives had been collected where they could be recovered safely. Some people wanted their clothes and tools. Others said they never wanted to see anything that had been inside the ferry again.

The workers respected both answers.

On the bank, a row of recovered objects waited beneath a tarpaulin: a cooking pot, a pair of sandals tied together, a tin lunch container, several bundles of clothing. Nothing in the row suggested the size of the event that had separated the objects from their owners.

Tín recognized the older boy's sandal. A strap had split when someone stepped on it during the evacuation. Thảo had asked that it be kept because the family could repair it and because buying another pair still mattered after a rescue made them briefly important to strangers.

He moved the sandal farther beneath the cover when rain began to threaten again.

Bảy came back with a drawing made on a board.

“This part first,” he said.

Tín studied the arrangement. The piece Bảy indicated had lodged beneath the ferry's outer edge. Removing it would let the hull settle lower but away from the men working inside.

“The far corner will move before it drops,” Tín said.

“How far?”

He looked at the water, the exposed beam, and the angle at which the ferry's weight rested on it.

“Farther than that line allows.”

Bảy looked back at the drawing.

Tín took the pencil and marked the new position. Then he added another mark showing where the line would pull if the hull moved the way he expected.

The mechanic studied it. He called one of the crew leaders over and asked him to look at the actual connection rather than accept either drawing.

The man returned after several minutes.

“He's right.”

Bảy grunted and changed the plan.

Tín handed back the pencil.

“A professional service,” he said.

“I'll deduct it from the boat you owe me.”

They began when the crew stood clear.

Tín went into the water beside the beam. Its lower edge rested in mud. He found a place where the metal remained sound, braced his feet, and lifted until the weight came away from the channel bed.

The ferry shifted exactly as he had said it would.

The adjusted line took the movement without pulling a worker's boat toward the hull. Bảy called for the men to hold their position. Tín turned the steel clear and carried its nearer end toward the bank.

To the people watching from the road, it looked as if a section of the ruined crossing had decided to walk out of the river.

Water ran from it in sheets. The long end rose above the landing. Tín moved one foot at a time, feeling the ground before committing the full weight to it. At Bảy's direction he lowered the piece onto timber supports prepared on the bank.

The ground shuddered when he released it.

One of the younger workers stood with his mouth open.

Bảy pointed at the next line.

“You've seen him. Now look at your job.”

The worker closed his mouth and went.

They moved three more sections before stopping for food. Each required a different approach. Tín could not simply lift higher whenever the wreck resisted. Sometimes resistance meant one piece was holding another that needed to remain supported. Sometimes it meant a line had caught where nobody could see it. His strength made the work possible, but only attention kept it from becoming another collapse.

He found himself enjoying the precision.

There was no opponent trying to make the wrong movement attractive. No one held a child where a falling beam would go. The men wanted the same result and could say plainly what they had found. When a calculation changed, they changed the work instead of defending the earlier answer as a matter of reputation.

Tín had commanded more profitable days.

He could remember few that used his body better.

At the meal break, the crew sat beneath the warehouse awning with bowls balanced on their knees. Ngân had brought food from the new crossing. She set a separate bill beside Tín's bowl.

“Fuel, two canopy supports, and a cushion somebody took to put under an injured man.”

“Did you get the cushion back?”

“Would you want it back?”

He paid the bill.

Lâm sat nearby with his foot resting on an overturned bucket. He had been allowed to help sort fittings and check engines on shore, a compromise reached after Thảo and the clinic woman explained at length why walking less was not evidence of cowardice.

He turned a small component over in his hands and looked toward Tín.

“Could you do that with the whole ferry?”

“Not from where it's lying.”

“If it were clear?”

Bảy looked up from his food.

“Don't encourage him. I'm trying to remove it, not discover which surrounding buildings he can throw it through.”

Lâm grinned.

Tín ate without answering the question.

That made the younger man more interested rather than less.

Thảo arrived with the owner of the recovered sandal. The boy wore a borrowed pair too large for his feet. His father walked beside him carrying a bag for the other things they hoped to find.

Tín watched them go through the objects beneath the tarpaulin. The father found the lunch container and opened it, then closed it quickly because of the smell. The boy found his sandal and held it up with the satisfaction of recovering something he could understand.

He looked toward Tín.

For a moment the old man thought he would come over.

Instead the boy took his father's hand and showed him the broken strap.

They discussed how to repair it.

Tín returned to his food.

In the afternoon they reached the largest remaining section of the gantry.

It lay partly beneath the ferry's shelter. Bảy wanted the hull shifted enough to free it before the steel was moved. The crew had prepared the supporting lines, but one attachment remained beyond reach beneath a folded part of the deck.

Tín went under to inspect it.

The water closed around his head. Mud and rust made the world dark. He moved by touch, following a beam until he found the point where the deck had folded over it. The shape was more complicated than it appeared from above. Two members had twisted together, each holding the other against a movement that would free it.

He surfaced and described the problem.

Bảy listened, drew it, and frowned.

“We cut here.”

“That releases the side toward the workers.”

“Then here first.”

“That puts the full weight on the damaged joint.”

The mechanic looked at the wreck and then at Tín.

“What are you seeing that I haven't drawn?”

Tín took the pencil.

He drew the hidden members as he had felt them, then the forces passing through them. The marks spread across the board. He did not need to work through the calculation line by line. The answer arrived as the relationships became clear: one part lifted, another turned, the weight transferred before the cut could release it in the wrong direction.

He marked a sequence of movements.

Bảy studied the drawing for a long time.

“You did that under the water?”

“Most of it.”

“With no light.”

“There wasn't any to use.”

The mechanic passed the board to the crew leader, who read it slowly and asked two questions. Tín answered both. Bảy changed one part of the working arrangement because the men handling the line would need more room than the drawing allowed.

Tín nodded.

They moved the boats.

When the final cut was made, the folded section opened without falling. Tín took the exposed beam in both hands and raised it clear of the hull. For a moment the entire remaining structure hung above the berth, immense, dripping, and free of the river that had held it.

He turned toward the bank.

The men there stepped back until Bảy told them they were clear. Tín carried the steel ashore and lowered it onto the waiting supports.

Nobody spoke until his hands came away.

Then Bảy looked at the work and nodded once.

“That will do.”

Tín wiped water from his face.

“Your enthusiasm is exhausting.”

The mechanic smiled for the first time that day.

By late afternoon the channel had opened enough for the small boats to pass safely while work continued on the ferry hull.

The first through belonged to a woman carrying produce to the new crossing. She slowed to look at the steel on the bank, then at Tín standing beside it. She raised a hand in greeting and continued before anyone could make the passage ceremonial.

Ngân watched her go.

“She'll tell everyone she opened it.”

“She was first,” Tín said.

“She also owes me for a basket.”

“A complicated history.”

“All the important ones are.”

Later, Hòa came from school with Mai walking beside him. They stood above the landing while the crew put away tools. Mai looked at the great beam resting on the bank and then at the ordinary path her grandfather would use to come home.

“You moved that?” she asked.

“Parts of it.”

She pointed at the largest section.

“That part.”

“Yes.”

Hòa examined the supports beneath it.

“Somebody checked where you put it?”

Bảy answered before Tín could.

“Several people. He was very patient about being annoyed.”

“We're working on it,” Hòa said.

A timber support shifted beneath the great beam. The movement was small, followed by a heavier settling that made Bảy turn sharply. Hòa's hand rested against the end of the steel. For an instant Tín could not see how much weight had left the other supports, only that the beam had stopped where his son touched it.

Hòa waited until a worker drove the loose support firmly into place. Then he put his hand in his pocket.

Bảy looked from the beam to Tín.

“Your son too?” he asked quietly.

Tín looked at Hòa, who was listening to Mai ask about the drawing board.

“He keeps his own accounts,” the old man said.

He offered no measurement, and Bảy was sensible enough not to ask for one.

Mai went closer to the drawing board. She read the marks without pretending to understand all of them, then asked her grandfather what one of the arrows meant.

He explained where the weight had been moving.

She asked why another arrow pointed in the opposite direction.

“Because stopping something isn't the same as making its weight disappear,” he said.

She looked at the gantry again.

“So where did it go when you were holding it?”

Tín pointed at the broken pier.

“Through me. Into what was left of that.”

Mai studied the fractured concrete. For the first time she seemed to see the danger as something more exact than a story about a strong man beneath falling steel.

“It could have broken under you.”

“It did.”

“And then?”

“I found another place to stand.”

Hòa put a hand on her shoulder.

“Enough for today.”

She let the question go, but Tín could see she had not finished with it.

Bảy walked them to the path. A replacement workboat had been brought to the yard for him to inspect. It was used, sound, and plainly repaired rather than painted to conceal its history. He had chosen it himself after rejecting two that Tín thought looked better.

“The inside matters,” he had said.

Now he looked toward it and then at Tín.

“I'll take passengers again when this is finished.”

“And repairs?”

“Some. Work I can leave at the end of the day.”

Tín nodded.

The mechanic held out a hand.

They shook. Bảy's grip was ordinary, rough with work, and offered without fear.

“Come down next week,” he said. “As a passenger.”

“What's the fare?”

“Same as everyone else's.”

Tín accepted the terms.

He walked home with his son and granddaughter while the river moved through the open channel behind them.


## 25. The Chair and the River

By the time the old crossing reopened, people were saying Tín had held the gantry with one hand.

Some said he had used the other to carry children ashore. A man at the fuel stall claimed the river stopped moving while the old man stood beneath the steel. Another said he saw lightning strike the beam and turn aside rather than touch him.

Tín heard that version while buying charcoal.

“It rained,” he said.

The man telling the story turned, recognized him, and became uncertain whether the correction was an objection or a test.

“That was what I heard.”

“Then the rain was louder where you were.”

The stall owner put the charcoal in a sack. She gave Tín the price he paid before the storm and did not ask him to confirm the part about lightning.

He appreciated that enough to buy another small item he did not particularly need.

At home, Mai was waiting with an exercise book and a question she had been postponing until she could decide how to ask it.

“Did you use both hands?”

“Yes.”

“People at school said one.”

“They weren't holding it.”

She sat beside him beneath the house. The damaged chair remained against the wall, though Hòa had begun taking its joint apart properly. The work now occupied less space and looked more likely to end than it had when Tín spread the pieces over half the table.

Mai watched her grandfather set down the charcoal.

“Could you have used one?”

He considered the question. She was asking about the story, but also about the limits of a man whose limits had become difficult to imagine.

“I needed to control both ends of what I was touching,” he said. “One hand would have made that harder.”

“That isn't exactly an answer.”

“It's the part I know happened.”

She looked at his hands.

“Did you think everyone would get out?”

“I thought I could give them time.”

“Were you frightened?”

He remembered the view blocked by rain, the people disappearing below the edge of the ferry, the count he could not keep while the steel moved against his palms.

“When I couldn't see whether everyone was out.”

“But you held it.”

“Yes.”

She waited.

“Someone else had to count,” he said.

Mai opened the exercise book on her lap. For a while she turned the pages without reading them.

Then she asked about her father.

“At the salvage. When the beam moved.”

Tín looked toward the house. Hòa was upstairs, speaking with Hương about a school expense. His voice had the same patient irritation it acquired whenever a simple total concealed several decisions nobody wanted to make.

“What about it?”

“Dad touched it. Then it stopped.”

“I saw.”

“How much was he holding?”

Tín looked back at her.

“You would have to ask him.”

“Do you know?”

He smiled slightly.

“Your father has always kept some things private.”

“From you?”

“From people who assume they know a man because they've known him a long time.”

Mai studied his face. He let her see that the answer was intentional and that he would not improve it by pretending there was nothing to ask.

Hòa came down carrying the chair's repaired joint.

“You're encouraging questions again,” he said.

“I thought that was your profession.”

“It is. I prefer them before I start gluing things.”

Mai looked at the wood in his hands.

“Was the beam heavy?”

Hòa glanced at his father before answering.

“Heavy enough that it needed to stay where Bảy put it.”

“That's the same kind of answer.”

“A family habit,” Tín said.

Hòa set the joint on the table. He began fitting it into the chair with movements so ordinary that anyone passing the gate would have seen a teacher repairing furniture on a free afternoon. Tín watched the care with which he used a tool when his hands could have done something else.

He did not ask his son for a demonstration.

Mai put the question away without abandoning it. Tín could tell by the way she returned to the exercise book with a little more force than the pages required.

“This one,” she said, pointing.

He read the problem.

The answer appeared immediately. He was about to give it when he remembered Hòa across the accounts table, making him wait while a family learned how a number had been reached.

“What have you tried?” he asked.

Mai showed him. Her first approach failed halfway through because she had assumed something the problem did not give her. Tín pointed at the assumption and let her find the consequence herself.

She crossed out a line, wrote another, and paused.

He could have filled the page faster than she could follow the pencil. Instead he answered the question she asked and waited until she asked the next.

When she reached the result, Hòa looked over from the chair.

“Did he give it away?”

“Eventually,” Mai said.

“I was patient.”

“You looked pained.”

“An unfamiliar muscle.”

Hòa laughed and went back to the joint.

They took Bảy's boat the following morning.

Bích insisted everyone be ready at the same time. Hương packed food. Hòa checked the house before locking it. Mai carried her satchel, still tied with string because she had put enough books inside to turn a short visit into an opportunity for study.

Tín looked at the string and made himself remember the promise without saying he remembered it. He had announced that particular intention often enough.

At the landing, Bảy collected their fares one by one.

When Tín reached for money for the whole family, Bích put a hand over his.

“We have it.”

He paid his own fare.

Bảy gave him the same small ticket he gave the other passengers. Tín folded it once and put it in his shirt pocket.

The boat left the landing with a modest load: their family, a woman taking cloth to a relative, a man carrying spare parts, and two children whose mother had arranged for someone to meet them at the next stop. There was room for everyone to sit without turning sideways.

The engine ran evenly.

Bảy listened to it with the satisfaction of a man whose machine was doing what he asked without adding an opinion. The name he had chosen for the boat was painted near the stern. Tín recognized the name Bảy had spoken beneath the shed roof.

He said nothing until Bảy looked back and saw where he was looking.

“He didn't get the old one back,” the mechanic said.

“I know.”

“I wanted this one to begin with him.”

Tín nodded.

The boat passed the old crossing. Most of the gantry lay on the bank in sections now. The ferry's damaged hull remained under work, but the channel beside it was clear. Men stood on a temporary platform measuring something that would be built only after enough people had agreed what it needed to carry.

Chấn's gate had not been replaced.

The office remained. It would have uses when the claims around it were settled, and nobody pretended that removing a barrier meant the land and buildings ceased to matter. For now, people passed the opening without handing a man their ignition key.

Mai watched the place recede.

“Will they build another gantry?” she asked.

“If they need one,” Hòa said.

“Who decides?”

“The people paying for it and the people expected to use it should both have something to say.”

She looked at her grandfather.

Tín watched the water moving through the gap.

“And somebody who knows where the weight goes,” he added.

Bảy raised a hand from the stern without turning around.

They stopped at Tòng's house.

The builder had begun the new room. Fresh timber stood against the older walls, and the unfinished stairs occupied a part of the yard where weeds had grown when Tín last visited. Tòng sat beneath the tree directing work he had been expressly told he did not need to direct.

His granddaughter came out to meet them.

“Please keep him talking,” she told Bích. “He has corrected the same measurement four times.”

“Was it wrong?”

“The first time. Now he enjoys remembering that.”

Bích went to sit beside him.

Tòng looked at Tín and the family arriving together.

“You brought supervision.”

“You requested it.”

“I didn't expect such enthusiasm.”

They ate beneath the tree while the workers took a break. Tòng's granddaughter served food from the bag Hương brought and another dish she had prepared herself. The conversation moved through weather, school, repairs, and the argument over whether the new stairs should begin closer to the path.

Tín listened more than he spoke.

Once, when the talk paused, Tòng looked toward the photograph visible through the open door.

“She would have liked the room,” he said.

His granddaughter put down the bowl she was holding.

Nobody tried to make the sentence happier.

After a moment Tòng asked Hòa about his pupils, and the conversation continued around the space the absent woman occupied.

Tín understood that the room could be rebuilt and that this would remain. Neither fact cancelled the usefulness or the limits of the other.

Before they left, Tòng made him sit for another cup of tea.

“You came without needing to find anyone,” he said.

“I promised.”

“You're becoming dangerously reliable.”

“Give it time.”

Tòng smiled, then rested both hands around the cup.

“Come again.”

“I will.”

This time the promise contained no task large enough to conceal the simple visit inside it.

On the return journey they passed Thảo at the new crossing.

She stood beneath the awning with fares in one hand and a passenger's complaint in the other. Lâm sat at a workbench nearby, repairing a small engine with his foot resting on a box. The file lay beside his hand.

Thảo saw Tín in the boat and raised two fingers, then went back to the passenger who insisted he had paid for a larger bundle than the one recorded.

She made him put it on the scale.

Tín watched long enough to see her win the argument without lifting her voice.

Lan had found work sorting the accounts of several small businesses whose owners knew exactly where she had worked before. Some required another person to check her figures. Others refused to employ her. She had told Tín she preferred an honest refusal to a friendly arrangement in which nobody said what they distrusted.

Her own part in the Compact remained under examination. So did his. The questions had acquired dates, names, witnesses, and people who expected answers. He could no longer make them disappear by going home.

Phúc and Dưỡng remained in custody. Their businesses no longer moved men between sites because a collector said a dead father had borrowed again. The workers who chose new jobs were paid in their own names. The men who had held them were being made to answer for the holding.

There was still work in every room where people waited for an answer.

Tín had stopped using that as a reason not to begin the part in reach.

When Bảy brought the boat to their landing, Hòa stepped ashore first and held it steady while Bích and Hương climbed out. Mai followed with the satchel pressed against her chest.

Tín remained a moment longer.

Bảy looked at him.

“Comfortable journey?”

“Yes.”

“Good. Try not to make the next one famous.”

Tín stepped onto the landing.

At home, Hòa put the repaired chair beneath the house and sat on it himself before anyone else could volunteer.

It held.

He shifted his weight, leaned back a little, then set all four legs firmly on the ground.

“A chair,” he announced.

Mai applauded twice, solemnly.

“An ambitious project,” Tín said.

“I had a difficult example to improve.”

Bích brought tea and put the mending basket on the table instead of the chair. She sat in the chair after her son rose. The old wood took her weight without a sound.

Tín went to the shelf for the small screwdriver.

Mai saw what he held and brought the satchel without being asked. She untied the string, took out the books, and placed them beside him in the order she would need them tomorrow.

The catch was bent. He inspected the hinge, removed the small screw, and laid the pieces on a clean cloth. The damage was minor. It had remained unfinished because larger things kept presenting themselves with louder demands.

Hòa stood nearby, wiping glue from his hands. For a moment father and son watched each other over the ordinary work between them. Tín thought of the cabinet stopped without a glance, the clerk who recognized something in the teacher's hands, the great beam settling only as far as Hòa allowed.

He did not ask how far those hands could go.

Hòa looked at the satchel and nodded toward the loose screw.

“Don't lose that.”

Tín picked it up between two fingers.

“Your confidence sustains me.”

His son smiled and went to wash his hands.

Mai sat beside her grandfather while he straightened the catch. Through the open floorboards above them came the sound of Hương putting books away. Bích drank her tea in the repaired chair. Beyond the gate, a neighbour called to someone carrying vegetables along the path.

The river was close enough to hear when a boat passed, and far enough that nobody in the house needed to rise because of it.

Tín fitted the hinge, replaced the screw, and pressed the catch shut.

It clicked.

He opened it once more and tested it, pulling firmly enough to be certain without asking the small piece of metal to prove anything grander than its purpose.

Then he placed the satchel in his granddaughter's hands.

“There,” he said.

Mai put her books inside.

# Book II: The Man at the Door

## 1. A Place of His Own

The man wanted Minh to put the broken part back in the engine and tell his employer it had never been broken.

He made the request quietly, with two fingers resting on the counter and his other hand holding a cigarette he had forgotten to light. His name was Cường. He drove a small refrigerated truck for a distributor of frozen fish. He had waited through Minh's explanation without looking at the part once.

“The old one,” he said again. “Can you put it back?”

Minh turned the fractured casting beneath the lamp. He could see where someone had ground the broken edge flat, coated it in paint, and reinstalled it.

“It will fail.”

“Not immediately.”

“Yes. Immediately.”

“It got me here.”

“You got here with the engine switched off.”

Cường looked through the open shutter. His truck stood half in the lane, rear wheels secured with bricks. Two men had helped him push it the last thirty meters. One now sat across the street drinking coffee and staring at the vehicle as if it had personally offended him.

“They'll say I broke it.”

“You didn't.”

“You're going to come explain that to my boss?”

Minh put the casting down.

“Give me his number.”

The driver looked at him properly for the first time. Minh wore an old gray shirt and had a line of black grease along his cheek. His hair needed cutting. There was a bowl beside the vice containing rice, a piece of fish, and a spoon buried under a rag because the morning had refused to end. Nothing about the shop suggested that its owner could afford to spend time in an argument with someone else's employer.

“You don't have to do that,” Cường said.

“I know.”

The telephone stood on a shelf above the invoices. Minh dialed while the driver recited the number. When the employer answered, Minh gave his name and explained the fracture. He spoke slowly enough for the man to interrupt him three times. On the fourth interruption, he stopped speaking entirely.

The silence became long.

“Are you still there?” the employer asked.

“I'm waiting for you to finish.”

“You said the repair would be cheap.”

“I haven't quoted you.”

“My driver said—”

“Your driver asked whether the broken part could be put back. Someone already tried that. The paint is still soft beneath the mounting bolt. Whoever charged you for the last repair owes you money.”

The voice changed. The anger remained, but it moved in search of a better address.

Minh listened, took a pencil from behind his ear, and wrote a number on the invoice. It was less than the driver expected and more than Minh had intended to charge before the conversation started.

“That's the price,” he said. “He keeps the damaged casting. You can show it to your other mechanic.”

He hung up.

Cường stared at the invoice. “You added money.”

“For the telephone call.”

For the first time that morning, the driver smiled. Minh pointed at the smile with the pencil.

“Don't do that in front of him.”

Outside, a horn sounded continuously.

Minh looked up.

The bricks beneath the truck's rear wheels had shifted. The vehicle was moving down the lane, backward, gathering speed toward the intersection. Its rear door was open. Beyond it, a woman carrying two baskets on a shoulder pole had stopped in the middle of the road, unable to decide whether to go forward or back.

Cường ran.

Minh passed him before he reached the shutter.

He did not move like a large man accelerating. He was at the counter, then beside the truck, with no useful interval between. His left hand caught the rear frame. The metal folded around his fingers. His shoes struck the concrete hard enough to crack the heel of one sole.

The truck stopped.

Its load continued moving inside. Boxes hit the rear door, which tore off its hinge and fell toward the woman.

Minh caught it with his other hand.

For a moment he stood holding the truck in his left and the door in his right. The woman looked at him through the empty rear opening. A single plastic-wrapped fish slid across the floor and fell between them.

“Are you hurt?” he asked.

She shook her head.

“Then please step back.”

She took three measured steps, the baskets swinging with her. Only afterward did she begin to breathe loudly.

Cường reached the front of the truck and pulled the brake. Minh waited until he heard it take. He lowered the door against the wall and went around to the rear wheel. One brick had crushed. The other had been kicked aside by a child chasing a ball.

The child was gone. His ball remained beneath the tailoring shop's chair.

Minh retrieved it and set it on the chair. He replaced the bricks with wooden chocks from his shop.

Across the lane, the man drinking coffee had forgotten his glass halfway to his mouth.

“It was moving,” he said.

“I saw.”

“You stopped it.”

“Help him collect the fish.”

The man put his glass down and did as he was told.

By noon, four people had come to the shop to ask about the truck. Two had brought actual repair work. Minh accepted the work and refused the conversation. When one customer asked whether he had learned to do that in the army, he said he had learned to use wheel chocks by making mistakes.

He had never been in the army. He had never found an explanation for himself that improved when repeated.

Mai arrived at half past twelve carrying a cloth bag and wearing the expression she used when someone had paid an invoice in coins.

“The woman from the fruit stall says you lifted a truck over your head.”

“I didn't.”

“Then the woman at the pharmacy says you lifted two.”

He washed his hands at the tap. “I stopped one.”

“How disappointing for the pharmacy.”

She put the bag on the workbench and took out two food containers. He moved the tools before she had to ask. They had been married long enough for some arguments to begin at the sight of an object.

“You haven't eaten,” she said.

He gestured toward the bowl by the vice.

She lifted the rag. “Neither has this.”

“There was a truck.”

“Apparently there were two.”

He accepted the fresh bowl she handed him.

Mai was twenty-five. She had small wrists and an exact way of handling paper, as if the edges of a sheet told her things its words did not. At her shop she printed invitations, menus, forms, mourning notices, and the handbills people pasted up for lost animals. She also kept accounts for several businesses whose owners trusted her more readily than they trusted banks. She had once spent half a day finding an arithmetic error worth less than the ink used to correct it, then worked until midnight to recover the time.

Minh had watched men who could face him across a knife look helpless when she asked to see a receipt.

“Lan found a larger room,” she said while they ate. “Behind her office. Enough space for binding and finished orders.”

“How much?”

She told him.

He calculated the additional orders she would need, the increase in paper purchases, the money tied up between paying suppliers and receiving deposits. The figures arranged themselves without effort. He saw the three slow months in the previous year as clearly as if the ledger lay open between them.

“You can afford it,” he said.

“I know.”

“Then why are you making that face?”

“Because Lan knows I can afford it too. She wants six months in advance.”

“Offer three.”

“I offered two.”

“And?”

“We're having dinner on Thursday to continue insulting each other.”

Minh ate another mouthful. At the front of the shop a customer politely cleared his throat. Mai looked at the clock and held up five fingers. The customer withdrew to the shade without argument.

“I have money,” Minh said.

“Yes.”

“If you need it for the deposit.”

“I know where you keep it.”

He waited.

Mai closed one container and pushed it aside. “Do you want me to ask?”

“I want you to have the room.”

“Then help me carry things when I get it.”

That answer had cost her thought. He could hear it. Her shop had paid most of their living expenses while his found customers. She had never made him feel small for that. He would not make the loan a test of her affection.

“Tell me when.”

“Tomorrow, if she accepts three.”

He looked at her.

She took his empty bowl. “You gave good advice. Don't become difficult about it.”

After she left, Minh found a folded sheet beneath his invoice book. Mai had drawn the binding room's dimensions and marked where the shelves would go. At the bottom, beside a large rectangle labeled TABLE, she had written: Please carry it. Please do not discover that the doorway is too small after you have already picked it up.

He put the drawing in his shirt pocket.

The afternoon brought heat, a failed starter motor, and a man who insisted the rattle beneath his seat had started only after Minh changed his tire. Minh found six loose coins and a bottle cap inside the seat compartment. The man listened to the explanation with the solemn disappointment of someone whose accusation had deserved a better enemy.

At four, Cường returned for the truck. His employer had sent the full amount and a request that Minh call him about maintaining the rest of the fleet.

“Do you want the work?” Cường asked.

Minh looked at the vehicles already waiting.

He wanted it. He wanted the shop next door when the tailor retired. He wanted a proper lift, a second mechanic, a roof that did not leak onto the electrical shelf. He wanted enough steady work that he could choose which jobs to refuse. He had worked for men who spoke of ambition as if they had invented it. They had usually meant their own.

“Tell him to come see me.”

The driver hesitated. “He likes being called sir.”

“Then he'll enjoy meeting someone who doesn't.”

Cường laughed before remembering the warning about his employer. He paid, collected the damaged casting, and drove away carefully.

The last visitor arrived near closing.

He wore a white shirt tucked into dark trousers and had polished his shoes for a lane full of engine oil. He waited outside until Minh finished replacing a brake cable. When Minh looked up, the man smiled and placed a red folder on the counter.

“Mr. Khang asked me to bring this personally.”

Minh's hand remained around the brake lever.

“Then you should bring it back personally.”

“It's a business matter.”

“I have a business.”

“He knows. He was pleased to hear you're doing well.”

The messenger's smile had become effortful. He opened the folder to reveal an invoice, two photocopied receipts, and a typed notice. Minh saw his shop's address in the first line.

“What is it?”

“An account transferred to Đông Lợi. Mr. Khang thought you'd prefer to settle it between friends.”

The account belonged to a supplier who had sold Minh three used machines. Minh had paid for all three. He recognized the receipts because he had written the dates himself.

On these copies, the final payment had disappeared.

He took the folder. The messenger's smile returned too quickly.

“You can tell him I'll look at it,” Minh said.

“He'll be glad.”

“I doubt that.”

After the man left, Minh sat on the low stool beside the counter. The lane emptied by degrees. A woman swept her doorstep. Someone called a child inside. Upstairs, his landlord moved a cooking pot across her stove.

He read the notice once more, then turned it facedown.

His hands were clean. His shop smelled of work he understood. In his pocket lay the drawing Mai had made of a room she meant to pay for herself.

Minh took out the drawing and spread it beside the folder.

There was enough space for the table if they removed its legs first. He found a pencil and wrote that beneath her note.

Then he picked up the telephone and called Khang.

A secretary answered. Minh gave his name. There was no request to wait, no music, no shuffle of papers. The next sound was the click of a receiver being lifted in another room.

“You received it,” Khang said.

“I paid that account.”

“Then we should have no problem.”

“Return it to the supplier.”

“He sold it to me.”

“He sold you something he doesn't own.”

“That would make us both victims.”

Minh looked at the red folder. The copies had been made on good paper. Someone had spent more money making the false debt respectable than Minh had spent printing his first business cards.

“What do you want?”

“To see you.”

“You could have asked.”

“Would you have come?”

“No.”

Khang made a small sound that might have been amusement. “Then I have saved us a conversation. Tomorrow. Eleven.”

“I'm working.”

“So am I. We are both busy men.”

Minh could hear other people in Khang's office: a chair moving, someone laughing too loudly at a distance. Khang had liked keeping his telephone calls audible. It gave the men around him an opportunity to understand which parts of his day were performances for their benefit.

“Listen carefully,” Minh said. “The machines are mine. The premises are rented. My wife owns her business. You don't have a share of any of it.”

The room at the other end became quieter.

“I didn't mention your wife.”

“Then you've heard more than you said.”

Khang let the silence remain for a moment. “You always were quick.”

“You used to pay extra for it.”

“I paid you very well.”

“You paid what we agreed.”

“And now you think agreement is a receipt in a drawer.”

Minh put a finger on Mai's drawing to keep it from moving in the fan's draft. He had listened to variations of this speech across too many tables. Men who wanted permanent obedience were especially fond of explaining why a completed payment could never complete a debt.

“I'll bring the original receipt,” he said.

“Eleven.”

“Nine. Before my customers arrive.”

“You're asking me to change my morning.”

“I'm giving you the only part of mine you can have.”

The receiver clicked before Khang could answer.

Minh remained seated until the dial tone began. Then he put the telephone down, carried the repaired brake assembly to its shelf, and lowered the shutter.

He would tell Mai about the folder when he got home. She would ask why he had called before showing her. She would be right to ask. He knew the shape of that conversation as well as he knew the sound a healthy engine should make.

Khang had been right about one thing. Minh remembered being paid. He remembered the amounts, the dates, what the money had bought. He remembered the first time Duy had looked at him as if the difficult part of a job had just ceased to exist.

It had happened two years earlier, beneath an engine that weighed more than a car.

## 2. The Fallen Engine

In 1998, Duy had come to collect an engine and found Minh reading the manufacturer's plate through a layer of grease.

“If you can tell what that says, I'll buy lunch,” Duy said.

Minh wiped the plate with his thumb. “The engine is older than the crane.”

“Most things are older than the crane. The crane was made yesterday by a man who hates everyone.”

They stood inside a riverside repair shed. Beyond the open wall, brown water slapped the timber landing. A dismantled vessel occupied the slip. Its owner sat in the office with a handkerchief held against his mouth, watching the two visitors through dusty glass.

Khang had sent them to retrieve a loaned marine engine. The vessel's owner claimed the loan had become a purchase after the third payment. Khang's office claimed three payments constituted rent. There was a written agreement somewhere. Neither of the men sent to enforce it had been given a copy.

At twenty-five, Minh had accepted that as someone else's problem.

He had already made the owner's problem plain. The engine would leave that afternoon. If the workers refused to disconnect it, Minh would disconnect it himself. If the owner wanted to stand in front of the truck, Minh would move him too. He had said all of this while the owner stood at his desk holding an unopened bottle of beer.

The handkerchief came later, when the owner threw the bottle and Minh returned it.

“He should have thrown something cheaper,” Duy said when they left the office.

Minh had not answered. He was watching the overhead crane.

The workers had rigged the engine badly. Not carelessly. They knew what they were doing with the equipment they had. The trouble was that the equipment required them to pretend it remained what it had been when new. One lifting eye had elongated. A cable passing through the upper block changed its sound when the load moved. Minh could hear the difference beneath the crane motor.

“Stop it,” he told the operator.

The man looked at the owner in the office.

“Don't look at him. Stop the crane.”

The motor ceased. The engine hung waist-high above its wooden supports. Two mechanics waited beside it with guide ropes. A third stood on the opposite side, half hidden by the bulk of the cylinder block.

Minh crouched and examined the lower mounting points. Duy came beside him.

“What?”

“Cable.”

“It holds.”

“For now.”

The owner emerged from the office, handkerchief lowered. “We use it every day.”

“Then you've been lucky every day.”

“Do you repair cranes now?”

Minh stood. “Get everyone back.”

The owner looked at Duy, seeking the lesser man and finding the wrong kind of sympathy.

“You heard him,” Duy said. “He usually starts politely.”

One mechanic let go of his rope and backed away. The other began to follow. On the far side, the third man bent to retrieve a wrench from beneath the engine.

The cable broke.

It made a small sound first, almost delicate. Individual wires parted in quick succession. Minh saw the upper block shift, the slack beginning to fall, the worker's shoulder still beneath the engine.

He put both hands under the load.

The falling mass drove him into a crouch. His feet broke through the thin concrete over the old slipway. The engine stopped with its lowest corner two handbreadths above the mechanic's spine.

A length of cable whipped across Minh's wrist. He felt it open the skin to the tendon. Hot oil poured over his forearm from a broken pipe. The engine was no longer simply heavy. Its balance changed as the disconnected crane hook struck its top and slid down one side.

Minh moved his right hand six inches.

The engine steadied.

“Out,” he said.

The mechanic did not move. He had gone flat to the ground, hands covering his head, waiting for the world to finish falling.

Minh could see the man's wedding ring between two greasy fingers.

“Duy.”

Duy went down on his knees and dragged the mechanic by the belt. Another worker caught his ankles. They pulled him clear. The injured cable continued striking the floor, each movement smaller than the last.

Minh rose.

He had expected resistance to become impossible at some point. It did not. Pain increased. The engine remained in his hands. He straightened until he stood beneath it, the whole weight resting across his palms and one shoulder.

No one in the shed spoke.

“Where?” Minh asked.

Duy stared at him.

“Where do you want the fucking thing?”

The owner pointed at the timber cradle beside the truck.

Minh took three steps. The concrete broke beneath each one. He lowered the engine onto the cradle carefully enough that none of the wood split.

When he let go, his left hand remained closed.

He looked down. The cable had laid the wrist open. Blood ran along his fingers and fell onto the cylinder casing. Duy took his shirt off and wound it around the injury. The owner moved toward the office telephone.

“Hospital,” Duy said.

“We haven't loaded it.”

“Look at your hand, you stupid bastard.”

Minh tried to open the fingers. He could. It required a separate decision for each one.

The mechanic they had pulled clear came toward him slowly. He was a broad man with hair thinning at the temples. His shirt had torn down the back. He touched Minh's uninjured elbow, then withdrew his hand.

“My name is Tấn,” he said.

Minh nodded.

“I have a wife.”

“Go home to her.”

Tấn looked at the engine, the damaged floor, the two places where Minh's palms had dented the casing.

“I will.”

At the hospital, Duy argued with a clerk until a nurse told him that shouting did not change the number of people ahead of them. He sat down beside Minh and held a fresh cloth against the wrist while they waited.

“You're paying for my shirt,” he said.

“It was ugly.”

“It's uglier now.”

Minh leaned his head against the wall. The muscles between his shoulders had begun to tremble. He could still feel the engine balanced across his arms. He suspected he would feel it in bed that night, the same way a day spent on a boat continued moving beneath a man after he reached land.

Duy watched the corridor.

“Have you always been able to do that?”

“No.”

“When did it start?”

“I don't know.”

“That's a hell of an answer.”

“What answer would you like?”

Duy thought about it. “Something I could understand.”

“Then ask me about the crane.”

The injured worker came to see Minh before the dressings were finished. Tấn had changed his shirt. Beside him stood his wife, Liên, carrying a plastic bag with a thermos inside. She thanked Minh once, plainly, and set the thermos on the chair beside Duy.

“He says you work for the man taking the engine,” she said.

“I do.”

She held his eyes long enough to make him uncomfortable, then nodded.

“He says you saved him.”

“He was underneath it.”

“I know where he was.”

She left with her husband. Duy unscrewed the thermos and found sweet tea. He poured some into the lid and gave it to Minh.

“Drink. She looked like she'd come back to check.”

Khang paid for the hospital. He paid for the broken crane cable, though not for the owner's damaged floor. He gave Minh a bonus large enough to replace every shirt Duy owned and asked him to come to the office when the stitches had been removed.

The meeting took place beneath a wall clock that had stopped. Khang's secretary kept accurate time for everyone who needed it. Khang appeared to enjoy owning a clock that no longer felt obliged.

“People are talking,” he said.

“People saw it.”

“You could have let the engine fall.”

“Tấn was beneath it.”

“I know his name now. I've paid his employer twice in a week.”

Minh sat with his bandaged wrist resting on his knee. Duy stood near the window, wearing a new shirt bought with Minh's money.

Khang opened a drawer and took out an envelope.

“This is for the work.”

“You paid us.”

“This is for what the work has become.”

Minh did not take it immediately.

Khang smiled. “Don't look offended. I'm not trying to own your arm.”

“You couldn't afford it.”

Duy lowered his eyes. Khang laughed, a real laugh this time, and pushed the envelope farther across the desk.

“Good. Then we understand each other.”

They did not. Minh took the money anyway.

For the next eighteen months, he earned more of it. He stood at doors. He recovered vehicles. He entered rooms in which men had prepared themselves to be brave and watched their preparations become insufficient. Sometimes the claims were real. Sometimes the men who owed money had injured people weaker than themselves. Minh enjoyed those jobs. He enjoyed the sudden correction that entered a room when he did.

He enjoyed other jobs too, and that was harder to examine.

In April 2000, Khang sent him to the cold store. Minh stood outside for most of an evening while voices carried through the door. He heard a chair scrape, a cry, a radio playing from a passing vehicle. He turned up his own radio. At midnight, Duy came out with signatures on seven sheets of paper and blood along the side of one shoe.

“Finished?” Minh asked.

“Finished.”

Minh took his envelope and went home.

Mai was asleep at the table with an account book beneath her forearm. He put a folded towel under her head before waking her. She opened her eyes and looked at the envelope.

“How much?”

He told her.

“What did you do?”

“Stood outside.”

“Then what did someone else do?”

He had no answer he wanted to give her.

The next morning he counted all the money they had saved. He calculated what it would take to rent a room, buy used equipment, and survive six months of work arriving more slowly than bills. Mai sat opposite him and checked his figures, not because they needed checking but because she would not allow a future to be presented to her as a finished calculation.

“A repair shop,” she said.

“Yes.”

“With customers who can leave.”

“Yes.”

She moved one number from his list to hers. “I'll cover this.”

“You don't have to.”

“We're married. Occasionally you should allow that to inconvenience your pride.”

He looked at her. She looked back without smiling until he did.

In May, he went to Khang's office and returned the next envelope unopened.

Khang left it on the desk.

“A holiday?”

“I'm opening a shop.”

“Take a week.”

“I'm leaving.”

Khang's secretary was still in the room. The man nodded toward the door. She gathered her notebook and went out.

“Duy says your wife wants you out.”

“My wife wants a husband who eats dinner before it gets cold. This is my decision.”

“You think repairing old motorbikes will hold your attention?”

“I think I'd like to find out.”

Khang leaned back. For a moment he looked genuinely disappointed. Minh had never doubted that the affection existed. He doubted what it permitted.

“You have an unusual gift,” Khang said. “You can make men obey you. A man who can do that doesn't have to spend his life waiting for money.”

“Then you should understand why I'm leaving.”

“I could make your shop part of the company.”

“You could.”

“There would be security in that.”

“For whom?”

The question remained between them. Outside, someone laughed in the corridor and then stopped too abruptly. Khang looked toward the sound.

“I've treated you well,” he said.

“I've done what you paid me to do.”

“Everything?”

Minh thought of the cold-store door. “Everything I agreed to.”

“Then I won't stand in your way.”

He said it generously. Minh would remember that afterward, the care Khang took to let his departure feel like permission.

Duy waited downstairs. He had two bottles of beer in a plastic bag.

“You're really doing it.”

“Yes.”

“What if you get bored?”

“I'll come repair your car.”

“What if I don't pay?”

Minh looked at him.

Duy held out a bottle. “All right. Stupid question.”

They drank beside the river, sitting on a low wall with the evening traffic behind them. For a while neither mentioned Khang. Duy asked about the machines Minh intended to buy. Minh described them. The larger lathe had a worn bearing, but he knew how to make the replacement. The compressor needed a new tank. He could restore the motor himself.

“You make everything sound possible,” Duy said.

“Most things are.”

“And the rest?”

Minh flexed the wrist the crane cable had opened. The scar had turned white.

“You find out while you're holding them.”

Duy drank the last of his beer. He looked out across the water for a long time before dropping the empty bottle into the bag.

“I'll come see the shop.”

“Bring work.”

“You sound like my wife.”

Minh stood. “Then pay her too.”

They parted at the road. Minh went home with the smell of the river on his shirt and enough money in his pocket to buy the first machine.

A month later, the warehouse burned.

## 3. Paper and Ink

Khang kept Minh waiting until nine forty.

At nine forty-one, Minh stood up.

The secretary looked over her desk. “He'll see you shortly.”

“He can see me leave.”

The office door opened before Minh reached the corridor. Khang stood inside it, wearing a pale blue shirt and the tolerant expression of a man preparing to forgive someone for noticing an insult.

“Come in.”

“I have customers.”

“Then we'll be brief.”

Minh entered. Three men sat around a side table covered in maps. Two wore work shirts. The third had a heavy gold ring and an unlit cigar between his teeth. Minh had met him once, during an argument over a warehouse entrance. His name was Hải. At the time, he had claimed to own the entrance. When asked about the rest of the building, he had said that was somebody else's problem.

Hải took the cigar out of his mouth.

“The mechanic.”

Minh set the red folder on Khang's desk. “The man with the receipt.”

He opened it and placed the original beside the altered copy. The secretary remained in the doorway. Khang gestured for her to stay.

“An error,” he said after looking at the sheets.

“An expensive one.”

“We'll correct it.”

“Now.”

One of the men at the side table gave a quiet laugh. Minh looked at him. The laugh stopped. Khang watched the exchange with something close to pleasure.

“You see?” he said to Hải. “Even when he's collecting nothing, people listen.”

Minh pushed the false notice closer to him.

“Write that the account is settled.”

“You have your receipt.”

“Yesterday I had it too.”

The secretary crossed the room with a blank sheet. Khang took the pen she offered, wrote a short acknowledgment, and signed it. He handed it to Minh without looking away from his face.

“Satisfied?”

“With the debt.”

“Sit. There is something I wanted you to hear.”

“I know.”

“Then spare me the performance of discovering it.”

Minh stayed standing. Khang indicated the maps.

“Hải's drivers are being stopped near the south depot. Mine are being stopped at the same place. Cargo sits in the heat while boys with notebooks ask for fees nobody has agreed to pay.”

Hải put his cigar back between his teeth. “They broke one of my mirrors.”

“Buy a mirror,” Minh said.

“They hit the driver.”

That changed the room for him. Khang saw it change and continued quickly.

“An arrangement has become unclear. There are men who would understand a conversation with you more readily than one with anyone else.”

“Then send anyone else twice.”

Hải stood up. He was thick through the neck and had learned to use that thickness as an introduction. “You think you're too good to work now?”

Minh turned toward him. He did not straighten or broaden his shoulders. He let Hải see the distance between them and decide whether to close it.

“I'm working today,” he said. “At my shop.”

The man with the ring looked at Khang. Khang held up one hand.

“He made his choice. We respect that.”

“Then respect it,” Minh said.

He folded the acknowledgment and put it in his pocket. At the door, Khang called his name.

“Your wife's business is growing.”

Minh stopped.

“People say her work is excellent. I thought we might place an order.”

“Then place an order. Pay her deposit. Collect the work when she tells you.”

“You sound protective.”

“I'm saving you from asking her for a discount.”

The secretary looked down at her notebook. Minh saw the corner of her mouth move. Khang did too.

He left the office with the sound of Hải resuming the discussion behind him, louder now that he could afford it.

Mai's shop occupied the lower floor of a building with a green balcony. Above it, someone grew plants in paint tins and occasionally watered the street by mistake. When Minh arrived, a customer in a cream shirt stood beside the press, holding a sample invitation as if the paper had transmitted an illness to his hand.

“It is too blue,” he said.

Mai stood opposite him. “It's the blue you chose.”

“I chose it from the book.”

“This is the book.”

She opened a sample binder and placed the invitation beside the approved sheet. They matched. The customer stared at them for several seconds.

“It looks different in quantity.”

“You ordered two hundred. I can put one hundred and ninety-nine in a cupboard while you look at the first.”

Minh went behind the counter before the customer could see his face.

“This is an important dinner,” the man said.

“Then I hope you enjoy it. The invitations are ready.”

He noticed Minh. “Are you the owner?”

“No.”

“Her husband?”

“Yes.”

“Perhaps you can explain.”

Minh looked at the matching papers. “It appears to be blue.”

The man took a breath. Mai set the invoice down beside the samples.

“You approved the proof on Tuesday. Your deposit covered the paper and setup. This is the remaining amount.”

For a moment Minh thought the customer might try to leave without the invitations. Then the man looked at his watch, took out his wallet, and paid with the wounded dignity of someone who had lost a trial he expected to win by appearing in person.

Mai counted the money before handing over the wrapped order.

When he had gone, Minh leaned against the counter.

“Very important dinner.”

“He changed the list four times.”

“Perhaps the guests became different in quantity.”

She threw a clean rag at him. He caught it.

“You were gone longer than you said.”

The humor left the space between them without leaving either face entirely. They had learned how to let a conversation turn without pretending the earlier part had not occurred.

Minh showed her Khang's acknowledgment.

She read it, compared it with the receipt, and put both in a separate envelope.

“What did he really want?”

“Men near a depot are taking money from his drivers.”

“And he wanted you to ask them nicely.”

“He didn't specify nicely.”

Mai looked up. “Did you agree?”

“No.”

“Did he hear you?”

“He heard.”

“Those aren't always the same thing with him.”

Minh watched the press behind her. A sheet had caught slightly out of alignment. He pointed. She turned, stopped the machine, and pulled the sheet clear before it could wrinkle against the next one.

“Lan accepted three months,” she said.

“When do we move the table?”

“Now.”

“You could have warned me.”

“I put a drawing in your pocket.”

He took out the drawing. She saw the note beneath hers and smiled.

“So you do read.”

They walked to Lan's office carrying a toolbox and a small bundle of shelf brackets. The room behind it had a tiled floor, a high window, and a damp patch that Lan described as historical. Mai asked whether historical damp cost less than ordinary damp. Lan told her the room could be rented to someone less exhausting by the afternoon.

Lan was in her forties, narrow-faced, with gold spectacles she wore on a cord. Minh had seen her speak gently to a frightened shop assistant for twenty minutes and reduce a supplier to silence with three sentences. She examined him now as if deciding which version he required.

“Don't break anything.”

“I brought tools.”

“That doesn't reassure me.”

The table waited in the adjoining storeroom. It had once belonged to a furniture finisher and weighed enough that Lan had invited two men to help. They arrived to find Minh already crouched beneath it, removing the last leg while Mai held the screws in an envelope.

“We were told there was lifting,” one said.

“There is,” Lan said. “Find something else heavy.”

Minh carried the top through the doorway on edge. It was longer than he was tall. He moved slowly, not because it strained him, but because the passage contained a hanging light, two glass frames, and Mai, who had opinions about every possible mark on the wall.

“Left,” she said.

“Your left or mine?”

“The wall's left.”

“The wall doesn't have a left.”

“Then stop trying to introduce the table to it.”

He adjusted half an inch. The top passed through. One of the men watched his arms, then looked at the tool kit, as if strength might be another attachment Minh had forgotten to put away.

By noon the table stood level. Minh tested each joint, then pressed down on the nearest corner. The floor tile beneath it cracked.

Everyone heard.

Lan removed her spectacles.

Minh looked at the tile. “I'll replace that.”

“Astonishingly, I had already reached the same conclusion.”

Mai covered her mouth with the envelope of screws.

They ate lunch on the newly assembled table. Lan brought pickled vegetables from upstairs. One of the men went for rice. The other remained to ask Minh about an engine that stalled whenever it became warm. Within five minutes, he was drawing its fuel system on the back of a torn carton.

Minh listened until the description reached the part that did not fit. Then he asked whether the problem disappeared after the cap was opened.

The man stopped drawing.

“Yes.”

“Bring it by.”

“You know what's wrong?”

“I know what to look at.”

“How?”

Minh pointed at the drawing. “You told me.”

Mai, across the table, met his eyes. She knew he had done more than listen. She had watched him multiply numbers nobody had finished saying, seen him identify an engine by the intervals between its exhaust strokes from inside their bedroom. She had never asked him to make a performance of it. Sometimes she asked him to stop calculating long enough to notice that she was speaking about a feeling.

After lunch, a messenger arrived with an order from Đông Lợi.

He was young, polite, and visibly relieved to discover that no one intended to shout at him. He carried a packet of old forms and a list of quantities. Mai read the order while Minh fitted the last shelf bracket.

“These are numbered,” she said.

“They want the same numbers.”

“For a reprint?”

The messenger consulted his sheet. “Yes.”

She laid two forms side by side. “Different amounts. Same receipt number.”

“I just bring the orders.”

“Then bring this question back.”

He looked toward Minh. “Mr. Khang said your husband knew about it.”

Minh set the screwdriver down.

“My husband knows where I keep the paper,” Mai said. “The order is mine. Who approved the replacement figures?”

The messenger's ears had begun to redden. He did not know. He had been given a delivery and found a problem inside it.

Mai took a separate sheet and wrote three questions. She clipped it to the front of the order, handed everything back, and told him what deposit she required if the questions were answered properly.

“If they can't answer?” he asked.

“Then I can't print it.”

He left quickly.

Minh returned to the shelf. He drove the screw too far and stopped before the head disappeared into the wood.

Mai watched him.

“He sent that because he saw you this morning.”

“I know.”

“He wants me to ask you whether I should accept it.”

“I know.”

“Good. Then don't answer a question I haven't asked.”

He put the screwdriver down and turned toward her. “I told him he doesn't own us.”

“He's a man who buys things that aren't for sale. Telling him doesn't change his profession.”

Minh looked at the room they had made. There was work in it now: orders waiting to be bound, shelves holding paper, a small box beside the door for deposits. It had taken less than a day for an empty room to acquire reasons that its occupants could not simply walk away.

“I'll speak to him.”

“About what he sent. Not about whether I'm allowed to refuse it.”

“Mai.”

“I know what you mean. I also know what he'll hear.”

He went to her and rested his hand lightly against the back of her neck. She leaned into it for a moment, then reached past him for the invoice book.

“Finish the shelf,” she said. “We have customers.”

That evening they ate at home with the window open. A neighbor's radio drifted between songs and static. Mai read out the expenses for the new room while Minh cut fruit with a knife she had forbidden him to use as a screwdriver.

“I haven't used it as a screwdriver,” he said when she looked at his hand.

“You were thinking about it.”

“You can't prove that.”

“The tip is shaped like your thoughts.”

He put the fruit in a bowl between them. She closed the account book and moved her chair closer. For a while they talked about people who had nothing to do with Khang: Lan's sister, who had taken another night shift; the landlord's nephew, who had failed his driving test and blamed the examiner's handwriting; Hòa, who wanted them to visit on Sunday and had asked whether Minh could bring a small replacement hinge.

“Your grandfather will have repaired it before we arrive,” Minh said.

“Probably.”

“Then why am I bringing one?”

“Because my father asked you.”

He nodded. There were calculations worth performing and others that had already been settled by marriage.

Later, while Mai washed the bowls, he went downstairs to check the shop's shutter. A motorbike stood across the lane. Duy sat on it with his helmet hanging from one handlebar.

“I heard you called Hải an idiot,” he said.

“I didn't.”

“He heard it anyway.”

Minh locked the shutter. “What do you want?”

Duy looked at the window above them. Warm light lay across the lane.

“A favor.”

“Come back with work.”

“It's a driver. A man I know. They're holding his truck.”

Minh remained beside the door.

“Who is?”

“The men at the south depot.”

He laughed once, without amusement. “Khang doesn't waste time.”

“It's not his truck.”

“It's his request.”

Duy swung one leg over the motorbike and stood. “The driver sleeps in the fucking cab because he can't afford a room near his route. He has vegetables going bad behind him. You can tell me you don't want to work for Khang. I know that. I'm asking whether you'll come look at the man.”

Above them, Mai turned off the kitchen light.

Minh looked at Duy's hands. They were empty. He looked at his face, which he had known through good work and bad work and several evenings neither of them discussed.

“Tomorrow morning,” he said. “You bring the driver here first.”

Duy nodded too quickly.

“And Duy?”

“Yes?”

“If you've left something out, tell me before I find it.”

Duy put his helmet on. The strap caught beneath his chin. He took too long fastening it.

“I'll bring him,” he said.

## 4. Driver's Night

The driver arrived carrying the steering wheel.

He set it on Minh's counter with both hands. The horn button was missing. The retaining nut remained threaded onto the center, as if whoever removed the wheel had taken care not to lose anything valuable while making the vehicle useless.

“They gave you this?” Minh asked.

“I took it.”

Duy stood behind him, looking tired.

“He thought they might move the truck.”

“They can't drive it without a wheel,” the driver said.

“They can put another one on.”

The man looked down. “I didn't have a better idea.”

His name was Phú. He was forty-one, married, and four payments from owning the truck outright. He carried onions and leafy vegetables to market, occasionally machinery on the return journey when someone paid cash. He had been awake for almost thirty hours. His shirt smelled of diesel, stale coffee, and the particular sourness of clothes worn through a night that would not permit their owner to rest.

Minh pulled out a stool.

“Sit.”

Phú sat immediately. Duy looked toward the doorway.

“Tell me what they want,” Minh said.

The amount was large enough to consume the driver's remaining profit on the load and part of the money owed to the growers. The collectors called it a storage fee. Phú called it a fee for being prevented from leaving. Both descriptions referred to the same night.

“Why did you enter their yard?”

“The dispatcher told me to wait there.”

“Whose dispatcher?”

“The buyer's. I thought.”

Minh looked at Duy.

“I found him at a coffee stall,” Duy said. “He asked whether I knew someone at the depot.”

“You do.”

“I know several people. None of them wanted to know him.”

Phú took a folded delivery slip from his pocket. Minh read it. The directions had been added in a different hand beneath the buyer's address. There was no signature beside them.

“Did you call the buyer?”

“They say they never gave that order.”

“Do you believe them?”

“I believe everyone has decided the vegetables are mine until they're worthless.”

Minh went upstairs and returned with the bread Mai had bought for breakfast. He put it beside the driver.

“Eat that. I'll tell my wife where we're going.”

Mai listened from the landing. She had heard enough of the conversation to ask only two questions: whether the driver could prove the load belonged in his care, and whether Minh intended to bring Duy home for lunch as well.

“He can buy his own,” Minh said.

“Then make sure he understands that before you become responsible for him.”

Duy looked up. “I can hear you.”

“Good,” Mai said. “It saves Minh repeating me badly.”

They left in Duy's car. Phú sat in the back with the steering wheel against his chest. Minh took the front seat and watched the route. Twice, Duy chose streets that added distance.

“Traffic?” Minh asked the second time.

“Roadworks.”

“Not there.”

Duy kept his eyes ahead.

“Khang's yard is on the direct road. I didn't want you thinking I was taking you to see him.”

“That requires me to notice the road.”

“You notice everything.”

Minh turned toward the window. “No. I notice engines and men lying badly.”

The yard lay behind a low wall painted with the name of a transport business. Its gate stood open just wide enough for a person. Inside, six trucks waited in uneven rows. A man beneath an umbrella wrote numbers in a notebook. Two others sat on plastic chairs beside a table holding a kettle and three glasses.

One of the seated men had Hải's narrow eyes and none of his confidence. He stood when he saw Duy's car.

“Didn't know you were coming.”

“I'm here for his truck,” Minh said, indicating Phú.

The man looked past him at Duy. “You brought him?”

“He asked a question,” Minh said. “Answer me.”

“There's an account.”

“Show it.”

The man went to the table and took out a sheet. He held it with his thumb over the company name until Minh removed the thumb.

Hải owned the company.

Minh looked at the seated men, then back at Duy.

“These are the boys breaking his mirrors?”

Duy swore under his breath.

The man with the notebook closed it.

“I don't know anything about mirrors,” the collector said.

“That makes two of us. Open the gate.”

“He owes storage.”

“He was directed here under someone else's name. You held the truck when he asked to leave. Show me what he agreed to pay.”

The collector looked at the wheel in Phú's hands.

“He damaged the truck.”

“His truck.”

“Not while it's in our yard.”

Minh waited. The man could hear the weakness in his own sentence and was searching for enough anger to cover it.

“Do you think you can just walk in here?”

“I did walk in. We're discussing leaving.”

The collector put two fingers in his mouth and whistled.

Men came out of the rear shed. Five, then three more. Most carried lengths of wood or metal. One held a long knife low beside his leg. A ninth man stayed in the doorway with a hand beneath his shirt.

Duy moved close to Phú.

Minh looked at the trucks. Three drivers watched through their windscreens. The nearest sat with both hands on the dashboard, as if presenting them to an officer. On a flatbed at the back, a young man had been sleeping beneath a tarp. He emerged now, saw the gathering, and stopped with one bare foot on the ground.

“Stay in your cabs,” Minh told them.

The collector began to smile.

“You recognize the situation.”

“Yes.”

Minh walked toward the man with the knife.

He chose him because the blade gave him the most reach, and because the man in the doorway would have to move to find a clear line around him. He had already measured the distance to the trucks, the space behind Duy, the open patch of yard where bodies could fall without striking anyone who had not volunteered.

The knife came up.

Minh's hand closed around the wrist. He turned it just far enough that the man dropped the blade. Then he lifted him by the same arm and carried him across the yard.

The man screamed before Minh reached the gatepost.

“I'm putting you down,” Minh said. “Decide what you're doing afterward.”

He lowered him outside the gate. The man ran, clutching his arm.

Minh came back in.

The other eight men had not moved.

“Next,” he said.

Two came together, because each believed the other had made the decision. Minh struck the first man's stick with the heel of his hand. Wood shattered. He drove the broken end back against the man's shoulder and used the falling body to obstruct the second. A pipe glanced off his upper arm. He took it, bent it across his knee, and threw it beneath the table.

The kettle fell. Hot water spread over the ground.

The man in the doorway drew his pistol.

Minh ran directly at him.

He saw the muzzle begin to rise. He saw the man's index finger tighten before the arm settled. He knew the shot would come high because the wrist had not finished lifting. The calculation occupied no words and almost no time.

He moved his head.

The bullet struck the gate behind him. A driver shouted inside one of the trucks.

Minh reached the gunman, closed his hand around the weapon, and drove him backward into the shed. The wall stopped the man's body. It did not stop Minh's arm. Brick cracked behind the shoulders. The pistol's slide folded, trapping the gunman's fingers beneath it.

Minh let go.

The gunman slid to the floor, holding the ruined weapon and crying through clenched teeth.

“Anybody else?” Minh asked.

No one answered.

He looked toward the collector. The man had backed against Phú's truck. The steering column showed through the windscreen, bare and accusing.

“Open the gate.”

This time the man did it.

Phú climbed into the cab. Minh followed him up and fitted the wheel while the driver held the nut. Neither spoke until it was secure.

Then Phú put a hand on Minh's shoulder.

“I can't pay you what that was worth.”

“You can get the load to the buyer.”

“They'll refuse it now.”

“Call them.”

Phú looked at him.

“I'll speak.”

The conversation took place at the yard's telephone. The collector gave up the receiver without argument. Minh told the buyer where the driver had been held, how the false instruction had reached him, and how long the vegetables had waited. The buyer argued about quality. Minh agreed that quality mattered. He asked whether the buyer was prepared to examine the load before announcing that it no longer existed as food.

By the end, Phú had a destination and a price that would keep him from owing money for the privilege of being robbed.

Minh handed back the receiver.

“The other drivers leave too,” he told the collector.

“Different accounts.”

“Then call the owners and discuss them. Until you have agreements, open the fucking gate.”

Duy stood beside the car, watching the trucks pull out. Phú stopped briefly and raised one hand through the window. Minh raised his in return.

When the last vehicle had gone, he turned to Duy.

“Did you know?”

“No.”

“Look at me.”

Duy did. “I knew Khang wanted someone to come. I didn't know Hải owned the yard.”

“How did Phú find you?”

“The coffee stall owner sent him.”

“Who told the owner?”

Duy's face changed.

They stood in the quiet left by the departed engines. In the shed, the injured gunman was asking someone to cut the metal away from his fingers. Minh heard the collector begin dialing another number.

“Don't bother hiding it,” he told the man. “Tell Hải exactly what happened.”

On the drive back, Duy said nothing for almost ten minutes. Then he struck the steering wheel with the flat of his hand.

“He used me.”

“Yes.”

“I brought you there like a fucking delivery.”

“Yes.”

“You could disagree once.”

Minh looked at him. “Would it improve the driving?”

Duy gave an angry laugh and changed gear. His hand remained tight on the wheel.

“What happens now?”

“Hải calls Khang. Khang says he didn't send me. Hải thinks I came because Khang wanted him to think that. They spend the afternoon trying to decide which insult they can afford.”

“And you?”

“I go back to work.”

“They won't leave it.”

“Neither will I.”

They stopped at a fuel station. Duy went to pay while Minh stood beside the car. Across the road, a truck from Đông Lợi waited at a light. The driver leaned out of his window and called Duy's name.

Minh recognized Trọng, Khang's operations manager. He had a square face and an old burn scar along one side of his neck. He used to make a point of counting men at the beginning and end of every job, even when the work consisted of loading boxes.

Duy crossed the road. The conversation lasted less than a minute. Trọng looked past him at Minh twice.

When Duy returned, he carried an envelope.

“Expenses,” he said before Minh asked.

“For what?”

“Fuel. The morning.”

“Give it back.”

“I bought fuel.”

“Then buy it. If you take that, this morning becomes a job.”

Duy looked at the envelope. The light changed. Trọng's truck pulled away, leaving a wash of hot air and diesel smell behind it.

For a moment Minh thought Duy would put the envelope in his pocket. Then he tore it open, took out the money, and laid it on the passenger seat without counting.

“I'll return it.”

“Today.”

“Yes.”

Minh got into the car. The money remained between them for the rest of the journey. Neither touched it.

At the shop, Duy turned off the engine and sat with his hands in his lap.

“Do you remember Tấn?” he asked.

Minh looked at him.

“From the crane. Of course I remember him.”

“His wife came to the office last week.”

Minh waited.

“She wanted his final wages. They told her the contractor hadn't paid.”

“Did he?”

“I don't know.”

Minh opened the car door.

“Duy.”

“What?”

“You keep bringing me things you don't know.”

He went inside before his former partner could answer.

## 5. Fire Names

Liên answered the door holding a man's work shirt and a pair of scissors.

She recognized Minh before he introduced himself.

“The crane,” she said.

“Yes.”

She looked past him at the lane. “Are you alone?”

“Yes.”

“Then come in.”

Her room contained a bed, a table, a cabinet, and a fan whose guard had been repaired with wire. Shirts lay in two piles on the bed. Some had buttons missing. On the table, beside a cup of cold tea, a row of salvaged buttons had been arranged by size.

Minh remained near the door until she told him where to sit.

“Duy said you went to the office about Tấn's wages.”

“Duy said something useful. I should write down the date.”

She put the shirt on the bed. It had been cut into rectangles, leaving only the collar and one cuff recognizably human in shape.

“Cleaning cloths,” she said when she saw his eyes move. “The men at the machinery stalls buy them. He left enough shirts to keep me busy.”

“I can use some.”

“I know. I wasn't asking you to buy them.”

He nodded.

She sat opposite him, scissors still in her hand. Her hair had been tied back carelessly. The woman who brought tea to the hospital two years earlier had looked direct and watchful. Grief had not made her less so. It had reduced the amount of time she intended to spend on other people's comfort.

“How much do they owe?” he asked.

“Six days at the warehouse. Two repairs the week before. A replacement part he paid for because the purchasing man said he'd be reimbursed.”

She gave the amounts without reaching for a paper. Minh added them automatically.

“That's more than Duy said.”

“Duy asked me what would make me go away. I told him the smallest number first.”

Minh looked at the cup between them.

“Did they pay any of it?”

“They gave me money after the funeral. They called it help. Then they called it wages when I came back.”

“Do you have the envelope?”

“I have the envelope. I have the card. I have the names of the two men who told everyone how sorry they were. What I don't have is the money Tấn earned before they became sorry.”

She set the scissors down.

“Why are you here?”

“Because I can ask.”

“Anyone can ask. They prefer people asking. It gives them someone to refuse.”

Minh met her eyes. “They'll have more difficulty refusing me.”

“I remember.”

That answer contained the engine, the hospital, and the man in the repair office holding a handkerchief to his mouth. Minh let it contain them. He had not come to explain his life in a form she could admire.

Liên stood and opened the cabinet. She took out a flat tin containing documents wrapped in a shopping bag. At the top lay the notice listing the dead. Minh read the names again: Tấn; Thúy; Hạnh. The sisters had packed fragile goods for transport. Tấn had been repairing a freight lift that stopped between floors.

“They said he should have left earlier,” Liên said.

“Who?”

“The contractor. The purchasing man. Someone from the building. Everyone who paid him to stay.”

She placed a note in front of Minh. It was in Tấn's handwriting, hurried but legible. Parts to buy. A measurement. A reminder to call a cousin. At the bottom, beneath the cost of a bearing, he had written: Bring red box home Friday.

“His tools,” she said. “They told me everything burned.”

“You don't believe them.”

“A man at the market offered me one of his socket sets last week. He didn't know whose wife I was.”

Minh leaned forward.

“You're certain?”

“Tấn marked every third piece because his hands shook if he worked late. Small white lines. He could count them without lifting his head.”

She showed Minh her fingers, three raised, then folded them back into her palm.

“I bought it. The seller charged me more because I cried.”

He looked at her for a long moment.

“Show me where.”

They went first to the warehouse.

It stood behind a fence with sheets of metal covering the lower half. Through the gaps Minh could see the fire's direction: blackened beams, a roof fallen inward, a stair that ended in open air. Rain had washed the ground clean in some places and gathered ash into ridges in others. A guard sat beside the gate reading a newspaper.

Liên had been refused entry twice. She did not expect a different answer now.

Minh gave the guard his name.

The man folded his paper.

“I can't let people wander.”

“Then walk with us.”

“The building is unsafe.”

“We won't enter it.”

The guard looked at Liên. “You're back.”

“I haven't finished,” she said.

He took a ring of keys from his belt and let them into the yard.

Minh stayed outside the collapsed structure. He did not need to climb into it to see that the lift machinery had been partly dismantled before the fire. The mounting bed remained against one wall. Three bolts were missing. A fourth had been cut cleanly. Fire could warp metal. It could not unscrew three bolts and decide the last required a saw.

“Was equipment removed during salvage?” he asked the guard.

“Some.”

“That motor?”

The guard followed his finger.

“I wasn't here then.”

“Who was?”

“A different company had the contract.”

Liên looked at the empty mounting bed.

“He was told to fix it.”

“The lift could have failed for more than one reason,” Minh said. “I can't tell you from here.”

She heard the limitation and nodded. He saw that she preferred it to a confident answer he could not support.

They walked along the fence toward the office entrance. A strip of unburned wall remained beneath the eaves. Against it stood several metal lockers. The doors had been removed. Nothing inside suggested who once kept clothes there.

Liên stopped at the second locker.

“He called from here.”

“That night?”

“Just before six. He said he would be late. I told him I wasn't keeping dinner warm forever.”

The guard moved away a few paces and pretended to examine his keys.

Minh waited.

“I still think about the rice,” Liên said. “What I said. As if the fire heard me.”

“It didn't.”

“I know that.”

“Then I'll say it again when you don't.”

She looked at him, surprised by the simplicity of the offer. Then she turned toward the gate.

“Let's find the man selling his tools.”

The machinery stalls occupied a long, hot aisle crowded with things that had once belonged inside other things. Fans without guards hung from rafters. Shafts, gears, chains, and brass fittings filled bins beneath handwritten prices. Every third seller claimed his stock was nearly new. Much of it had outlived the first person to make that claim.

Liên led Minh to a stall stacked with welding equipment. Its owner sat behind a desk eating noodles from a bowl balanced on an invoice book.

He recognized Liên and immediately looked elsewhere.

“You had a socket set,” she said.

“Many.”

“With white marks.”

“You bought it.”

Minh lifted a red toolbox from a shelf above the desk. The seller put his bowl down.

“That's expensive.”

“Whose is it?”

“Mine.”

Minh opened it. The hinges moved smoothly. Inside, tools lay in fitted compartments, each outlined in old paint. Three spaces were empty. A small screwdriver had a handle wrapped in green thread.

Liên took the screwdriver out and closed her hand around it.

The seller watched her face.

“I buy from people who sell,” he said. “I don't ask for their family histories.”

“I'm asking who sold it,” Minh said.

“A mechanic.”

“Name.”

“I don't know.”

“When?”

“Months ago.”

Minh set the box on the desk, moving the bowl aside before it could spill. The desk creaked beneath the careful pressure of his hands.

“Look at the woman standing in front of you.”

The seller did.

“You charged her for her husband's tools. Now you're going to help her understand why you had them.”

Two neighboring sellers had stopped talking. The stall owner noticed their attention and tried to recover his authority.

“You can't come here and make accusations.”

“I asked questions. You're the one who recognized an accusation.”

The man looked at Minh's hands on the desk. One of the boards had begun to bend.

“A truck brought several boxes. Parts too. I bought the lot.”

“When?”

“Before the fire.”

Liên's fingers tightened around the screwdriver.

“How long before?” Minh asked.

“A day. Two days. I don't remember.”

“Then remember the truck.”

The seller described a gray flatbed with one green side panel and no matching paint on the rear gate. Minh knew it. Khang had bought it cheaply after an accident and used it for jobs that did not need the company's name painted on both doors.

Trọng usually drove it.

“Did the driver have a scar?”

The man touched the side of his neck before he could stop himself.

Minh let go of the desk.

“What did you pay for the box?”

The seller named a number. Liên looked at him sharply.

“You charged me half that for six sockets.”

“Retail—”

“Don't finish,” Minh said.

He took out his wallet and paid the purchase price, less what Liên had already spent. The seller opened his mouth, looked at the desk again, and counted the money instead.

Outside, Liên took the box from Minh. It was heavy. He offered to carry it. She refused.

“I want to feel it,” she said.

They walked toward the road. A vendor passed with cold drinks. Liên bought two and insisted on paying before Minh could reach his pocket.

“I don't know what this means,” she said, looking down at the red box.

“It means someone removed his tools before the building burned.”

“Could he have sold them himself?”

“Would he?”

She shook her head.

Minh drank. Traffic moved past in bursts, close enough that he could feel engine heat against his legs.

“I know the truck,” he said.

“Khang's?”

He looked at her.

“You didn't have to tell me,” she said. “You're very careful with your face when you don't want someone to know what you know.”

He considered that. Mai had never found him particularly difficult to read either.

“Don't go back to the office alone.”

“I need the money.”

“I know.”

“Don't say you'll give it to me. I want them to pay.”

“Then let me find out what they took first.”

She set the box between her feet. “You worked for him.”

“Yes.”

“Do you still?”

“No.”

“Does he know?”

Minh looked at the traffic. Farther down the road, a truck changed lanes without warning. Three riders adjusted around it in a movement too intricate for any single driver to have planned. For a moment the whole street seemed balanced on everyone doing just enough of what the others expected.

“He's learning,” he said.

He took Liên home and returned to his shop after dark. Duy was waiting across the lane, sitting on the same motorbike in the same place.

“Where have you been?” Duy asked.

“Buying tools.”

“Khang wants to speak to you.”

“He has a telephone.”

“Hải's angry.”

“Then he can call too. We'll make an afternoon of it.”

Duy did not smile.

Minh stopped beside him.

“Trọng moved equipment from the warehouse before the fire.”

Duy looked at the shop's shutter.

“They moved equipment all the time.”

“Tấn's tools.”

The motorbike's engine clicked as it cooled. Minh watched his former partner take a breath and decide what to do with it.

“I don't know anything about that,” Duy said.

Minh stepped closer. Duy did not retreat, but the muscles beneath his jaw tightened.

“Then ask someone who does.”

“You shouldn't get into this.”

“I was underneath an engine with that man. Your advice is late.”

He left Duy in the lane and went upstairs carrying the green-thread screwdriver Liên had pressed into his hand before he left.

## 6. Floor Above Water

Trọng agreed to meet at a loading wharf and failed to mention which side of the river.

Minh called him back.

“The side I'm on,” Trọng said.

“Then stay there until I arrive.”

“You make it sound like an order.”

“You can decide what to call it when you see me.”

The wharf served several small warehouses whose owners shared a strip of hard ground between the road and the water. Men were unloading sacks from one boat and lengths of steel from another. A woman in a wide cloth hat stood beside a scale, recording weights and refusing to let anyone begin a second argument before she finished the first.

Minh parked his motorbike beside her table.

“It can't stay there,” she said without looking up.

“Where can it stay?”

She pointed with her pencil. He moved it.

Only then did she look at him.

“You're the one looking for Trọng.”

“Yes.”

“He said to wait.”

“Where is he?”

She pointed across the yard, toward a covered launch tied beyond the steel boat.

Minh thanked her and walked past the scale. Trọng stood beneath the launch's canopy speaking to two men in sleeveless shirts. He saw Minh and remained where he was. A motor idled somewhere inside the hull.

Between them lay a narrow floating walkway made from boards on steel drums. Workers crossed it carrying tools. Beyond it, the water moved swiftly enough to draw long seams around the moored boats.

Minh waited for two men carrying a pipe to clear the walkway before stepping onto it.

Trọng watched his feet.

“You came alone.”

“You asked me to.”

“Most men bring someone anyway.”

“Most men need to.”

The launch's canopy cast Trọng's face in shade. The burn scar along his neck remained visible, pale against the sweat-darkened collar.

“Duy says you're asking about equipment.”

“Tấn's tools. A lift motor. Things removed from a building before it burned.”

“You think that makes the removal a crime?”

“I think it makes the men saying everything burned liars.”

Trọng stepped onto the walkway. The two men stayed aboard the launch.

“The warehouse was changing its machinery. We move machinery. You used to move it for us.”

“I remember the weight.”

“Then remember the business. The owner sells something. We carry it. A contractor asks us to clear a floor. We clear it. I'm not responsible for every man who forgets his lunch tin.”

Minh took the green-thread screwdriver from his pocket. He held it where Trọng could see.

“Does that look like someone forgot it?”

“It looks like a screwdriver.”

“Tấn's wife bought his tools back from a stall. You sold them with the machinery.”

Trọng looked toward the woman at the scale. She was still arguing about weights. He lowered his voice.

“Don't say things you can't prove.”

“I'm giving you a chance to correct them.”

A chain clattered behind Minh.

He turned his head slightly. On the hard ground, a forklift had stopped beside the stack of steel. Three men stood around it. They were not dressed for unloading. One carried the same bent pipe Minh had left in Hải's yard, as if someone had decided the object deserved a second opinion.

Trọng saw them and swore.

“Your friends?” Minh asked.

“Hải's.”

“Everyone keeps saying that as if it explains why they don't have to clean up.”

The man holding the pipe came to the head of the floating walkway.

“You left something.”

Minh looked at it.

“It was broken.”

“So was my brother's hand.”

“He pointed a gun at me.”

“He won't do that again.”

The man raised the pipe with both hands. Minh heard the two men beside the forklift move apart. One went toward the ropes securing the steel boat. The other remained beside the machine.

This was not an argument arriving spontaneously. Someone had chosen a narrow walkway, moving water, and a work site full of people who could not get clear quickly.

Minh looked at Trọng.

“Did you arrange this?”

“No.”

For once he believed the man. Trọng's attention was on the launch, measuring whether he could get back aboard before the first blow. He had come expecting to control a conversation and found someone else had brought a decision.

“Move your boat away from the workers,” Minh told him.

Trọng did not ask why. He went back aboard and shouted to the man at the tiller.

The pipe struck.

Minh caught it against his forearm. Pain ran from his wrist to his elbow, sharp and brief. He stepped into the attacker, put one hand against his chest, and pushed him backward along the walkway.

The man tried to brace. His shoes skidded. Minh kept walking.

At the shoreward end he lifted the man over the last gap and threw him onto a stack of empty sacks. The pipe followed, landing beside his head with enough force to bury one end in the ground.

“Stay there.”

Behind him, the steel boat lurched.

The man at its mooring had cut a line. The loaded hull swung outward into the current. Two workers on deck grabbed for the rail. The bundle they had been guiding struck the floating walkway and split it in the middle.

Boards rose like teeth. A drum tore free. One worker fell between the boat and the broken walkway.

Minh jumped after him.

He crossed the open water before the man surfaced. His hand closed on the worker's belt beneath the brown water. He pulled him upward, caught the edge of the boat with his other hand, and lifted them both clear as the two surfaces struck together.

The worker landed on deck coughing. Minh hung from the rail for an instant, legs still over the water.

The forklift accelerated toward the shoreward end.

Its driver meant to push the remaining walkway out into the river. Minh could see that from the angle of the forks and the way the man had lowered his head. He could also see the woman from the scale standing in the machine's path, shouting because the driver had not noticed her table.

“Get away!” Minh shouted.

She turned.

The forklift hit the table. Weights and paper flew. The woman stumbled backward into a coil of rope and fell.

Minh let go of the rail.

For a fraction of a second he was falling away from everything he could stand on. A broken board rose beneath him with the swell. He struck it with one foot. The board shattered under the force, but the force had already gone through it.

He crossed the gap in a low, violent arc.

His shoulder struck the forklift's upright frame. The whole machine twisted sideways. Its rear wheel lifted from the ground. Minh caught the frame with both hands and continued turning until the forks faced empty water instead of the woman.

The driver fell against his harness. His foot remained on the pedal.

“Take it off,” Minh said.

The man stared at him.

Minh struck the instrument panel with his fist. Plastic broke. The engine died.

“Now.”

The driver's foot moved.

Minh hauled him out of the seat and threw him away from the controls. The man rolled across the hard ground, tried to stand, and collapsed again. Minh went to the woman and helped her sit up.

“Anything broken?”

She tested one ankle, then the other. “My table.”

“I'll pay.”

“You can start by getting those bastards away from my scale.”

He stood.

The man who had cut the mooring line was running. Minh took three steps and caught him by the back of his shirt. The cloth tore, leaving him with a fistful of cotton. The man kept running until Minh overtook him again.

This time he caught the body beneath the cloth.

He drove him against the warehouse wall hard enough that dust fell from the roof. The man lost all interest in fleeing. Minh pulled the knife from his belt and threw it into the river.

“You cut a boat loose with men aboard.”

“I didn't—”

Minh lifted him a foot higher.

“Try again.”

“Hải said to scare you.”

“Did I look scared?”

The man's eyes moved toward the wrecked forklift.

“No.”

Minh put him down. “Then tell him he's spending his money badly.”

He turned toward the water.

Trọng's launch was leaving.

It had cleared the steel boat and turned into the current. Trọng stood beneath the canopy, one hand gripping its frame. When he saw Minh looking at him, he shouted something to the man at the tiller.

The engine's note rose.

Minh ran to the end of the remaining walkway.

The launch was moving faster than he could reach it along any solid path. He knew the current from the direction of the drifting drums. He knew the boat's acceleration from the stern settling lower, the propeller note shifting through the water. He had less than a second in which the next position remained reachable.

He jumped.

The walkway broke behind him. He passed over the first drifting drum, the second, the widening wake between them. Trọng looked up. There was time for the man's mouth to open, but not for him to say anything useful.

Minh landed on the launch's stern.

The deck went through beneath one heel. The bow rose. The man at the tiller fell sideways as water came across the rear boards. Minh caught the canopy frame and held on while the boat settled.

Then he walked forward.

Trọng backed against the bow rail. “You fucking lunatic.”

“Turn around.”

“You could have sunk us.”

“You'd have had company.”

The driver looked at Trọng. Minh answered the question before it was asked.

“Back to the wharf.”

The boat turned.

Trọng kept one hand on the rail and the other raised between them. For the first time since Minh had known him, the operations manager seemed unable to arrange his face into an expression suitable for business.

“What do you want from me?”

“An answer.”

“I moved the machinery. Before the fire. It was an ordinary job.”

“Tấn's tools weren't machinery.”

“They were in the load.”

“Who put them there?”

Trọng's eyes went to the driver.

Minh moved close enough that the man could no longer look past him.

“You have told me three different things about a box that belonged to a dead man. The next thing had better be one you can live with.”

“Duy supervised the loading.”

The engine continued beneath them. On the shore, workers were pulling the steel boat back with a replacement line. The woman from the scale had found her notebook and was shouting at someone to collect the loose weights.

Minh looked at Trọng for a long time.

“Was Duy there that night?”

“Ask him.”

“I'm asking you.”

“He worked late. We all worked late. That's what people do when there are deadlines.”

It was an answer constructed to survive another question. Minh heard the joints in it. He also knew that breaking the man's jaw would not improve his memory in any direction he could trust.

He stepped back.

“You're paying for the damaged walkway.”

“Hải's men did that.”

“Then argue with Hải. You brought me here.”

Trọng looked at the hole in the launch's stern. Minh followed his gaze.

“I'll pay for the board.”

“That's generous.”

“I can make another hole while you reconsider.”

Trọng stopped talking.

At the wharf, Minh helped the workers secure the last line. He did not try to manage them. The oldest man aboard the steel boat knew what the current was doing and told him where to pull. Minh pulled there. Within minutes the vessel rested safely against its fenders.

The worker he had dragged from the water approached him with a dry shirt.

“Keep it,” Minh said.

“You're soaked.”

“I'll dry.”

The worker looked at the broken walkway, then at the launch, where Trọng remained seated beneath the canopy.

“Do you do this often?”

“I'm trying to do it less.”

The man handed him the shirt anyway.

Minh changed beside his motorbike. His left forearm had swollen where the pipe struck it. The scar across his wrist stood out white against the reddened skin. He rolled the borrowed sleeve down and went to the woman's scale.

She had set the weights on a crate.

“Name,” she said.

He gave it.

“Address.”

He gave the shop's address.

“For the table.”

“I said I'd pay.”

“Men say many things while they're standing over broken furniture.”

He took out enough money to replace it and set the notes beneath her notebook. She counted them, wrote a receipt, and handed it over.

“Come back when you want something weighed,” she said. “Nothing else.”

Minh put the receipt in his pocket beside the screwdriver.

When he reached his shop, Khang was waiting inside.

The shutter stood open. Mai sat on the low stool beside the counter, arms folded. Khang occupied Minh's chair as if the room had been built to accommodate the visit.

He looked at the borrowed shirt, the wet trousers, the swelling beneath the sleeve.

“You have been busy.”

Minh stopped in the doorway.

“Get out of my chair.”

## 7. The Offer

Khang stood slowly, making the movement look like courtesy.

“Your wife offered me a seat.”

“I offered you that one,” Mai said, pointing to the low stool beside the shutter. “You preferred his.”

Khang glanced at her. Minh saw him revise whatever description of Mai he had brought into the room.

“Then I apologize.”

“For the chair?” Minh asked.

“Let's begin there.”

Minh went to the tap. Water ran brown from the borrowed shirt's cuffs. He washed his hands, examined the swollen forearm, and flexed the fingers until he was satisfied they still moved properly. Khang watched without speaking. He had brought no visible guards. That was either confidence in their history or confidence in how much Minh disliked making Mai watch him work.

“Hải sent men to the wharf,” Minh said.

“I heard.”

“Did you hear before or after?”

“After.”

“Then your arrangement needs improving.”

Khang took the low stool. It made his knees rise awkwardly. Mai remained by the counter.

“He believes you went to his yard on my behalf.”

“You arranged the introduction.”

“Duy brought you a driver.”

“And someone brought the driver to Duy.”

Khang spread his hands. “A man was being robbed. You helped him. Are we going to argue over who deserves thanks?”

Minh leaned against the workbench. He could feel Mai watching him. She knew the expression that arrived when he was enjoying an argument more than he intended to admit.

“You wanted Hải to think you could still send me,” he said.

“Could I?”

“No.”

“Yet his men are hurt, his yard is empty, and his drivers are no longer stopping mine.”

“Your drivers?”

“Some of the trucks are leased through companies I finance.”

“Of course they are.”

Khang looked around the shop. He studied the clean tools, the shelves Minh had built, the repaired engines awaiting collection. The scrutiny was genuine. He understood businesses, including businesses too small to interest him as investments. He could look at a cluttered room and see what money entered, where it slowed, and whose patience kept it alive.

“How many vehicles can you service in a week?”

“Depends what's wrong with them.”

“Twenty?”

“Depends what's wrong with them.”

“You always make a man pay for asking the wrong question.”

“You keep asking them.”

Khang smiled. “Eight trucks. A maintenance contract for two years. You choose the work. You choose the people who help you. My office guarantees payment on the first of each month.”

Minh did not answer immediately.

Mai shifted on her stool but stayed silent.

Eight trucks could support a second mechanic and the larger premises. Reliable monthly payments would remove the period in which every part he bought remained money he could not spend on rent. He calculated the wear from likely routes, separated scheduled work from failures, and saw the price Khang would offer before the man named it.

“Too low,” Minh said when he did.

Khang's smile widened. “So there is a number.”

“There is always a number. It doesn't mean I'm selling.”

“Tell me yours.”

Minh named it.

Mai looked at him, then back at Khang.

“That's excessive,” Khang said.

“Your trucks idle for hours at yards where the drivers are paid to wait. The cooling systems are neglected. You buy cheap replacement bearings and blame the mechanics when they fail. At your price I'd spend the first six months repairing the last two years of decisions.”

Khang's eyes sharpened.

“How do you know what parts we buy?”

“I can hear your fleet coming down a street.”

The man sat back as far as the low stool permitted. “Your price includes that?”

“My price includes not being blamed when you ignore me.”

For a moment they were simply two men discussing a contract. Minh felt how easy it would be to remain in that moment. Khang was good at making the next step appear smaller than the step already taken. First a truck. Then a fleet. Then a driver who needed persuading. Then a door someone had to keep closed until a signature was finished.

Mai put the disputed printing order on the counter.

“Are these connected?” she asked.

Khang glanced at the forms. “A clerical matter.”

“Then the clerk should answer my questions.”

“I can have someone call.”

“Someone who knows why the same receipt number requires two different amounts.”

Minh watched Khang read the clipped questions. He took longer than the text required. The delay gave him time to decide whether indignation would work in this room. He seemed to conclude that it would not.

“Cancel the order,” he said.

Mai picked up her pen. “I'll note that.”

“It was only printing.”

“Then canceling it should be simple.”

She drew a line through the order and wrote the date beside it. Minh felt an unreasonable pleasure at the small violence of the pen.

Khang turned back to him.

“There are men who would fight for what I'm offering.”

“Then offer it to them.”

“They can't do the work.”

“You should have led with that.”

The room became quiet. Outside, a rider stopped to ask whether the shop was still open. Mai went to the shutter, took the man's description of the problem, and wrote his name on a slip. She gave him a time the next morning. Khang watched her conduct business around him and understood the answer had become larger than Minh's refusal.

When she returned, he stood.

“Walk with me.”

“Say it here,” Minh said.

Khang looked at Mai.

She met his eyes. “If it concerns the business, I can listen. If it concerns something else, he can tell me afterward.”

“You're fortunate,” Khang told Minh.

“I know.”

They went as far as the open shutter. Mai remained inside, close enough to hear if neither man lowered his voice. Khang did.

“Hải thinks you embarrassed him for pleasure.”

“He isn't completely wrong.”

“That is exactly the sort of thing that costs money.”

“He can send me an invoice. I'll file it with the first one.”

Khang looked down the lane. Children had begun playing with a ball near the corner. A man carried a stack of empty crates on the back of a bicycle. Ordinary traffic made it impossible for either man to occupy the whole street, however much they might have preferred it.

“You have talent,” Khang said. “You have more than talent. I've watched a room change because you put a hand on a door. You think I don't know the difference between you and men who need guns?”

Minh waited.

“But you enjoy being able to walk out. You enjoy making a man like me ask twice. At some point you must decide whether you want independence or an audience.”

It was a better accusation than Minh expected. He disliked how readily he could find evidence for it.

“And you must decide whether you want a mechanic or someone to frighten Hải.”

“Why should I choose?”

“Because I'm choosing.”

Khang rubbed one thumb along the edge of his watch.

“Stop asking about the warehouse.”

There it was. No folder, no contract, no courtesy disguised as a chair.

Minh let the silence lengthen.

“Why?”

“Because people died. Families are trying to recover. Men who had nothing to do with the fire are being accused because somebody recognizes a truck.”

“You heard about that quickly.”

“I run a company. People speak to me.”

“Then tell them to pay Tấn's wages.”

“We helped his wife.”

“Pay his wages.”

“I'll have it reviewed.”

“You can say yes. Try it.”

Khang's face hardened. “Be careful how you speak to me.”

Minh moved half a step closer. The distance between them had been conversational. Now it required an answer from Khang's body before his mouth could provide one.

“You came to my shop,” Minh said. “You sat with my wife. You offered money and then told me which questions I could ask. If you want careful, start with yourself.”

Khang held his gaze. To his credit, he did not retreat.

“The wages will be paid.”

“To her. With a statement of what they cover. No paper saying she has finished asking questions.”

“You think I need to cheat a widow?”

“You keep making me explain the job you've already done.”

Khang took a breath through his nose. Somewhere beyond the lane his driver started the car.

“Duy is worried about you.”

“He should worry about himself.”

“He does. That's one of his difficulties.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means he's always looking for someone stronger to stand beside.”

Minh looked back into the shop. Mai was sorting the next day's appointments, placing the smallest jobs first so the waiting area would not fill. She looked up as soon as his attention reached her.

“People can stand beside me,” he said. “You were the one who charged them for it.”

Khang gave him one final, level look. Then he turned and walked to the car without offering his hand.

Minh watched until the vehicle left the lane.

When he went inside, Mai put the appointments down.

“You wanted the contract.”

“Yes.”

“You were already counting the equipment.”

“Yes.”

“Would you have told me before accepting?”

“I didn't accept.”

“That's not the question.”

He sat on the stool Khang had used. It was still warm.

“I would have told you.”

“Before or after you decided?”

Minh looked at the workbench. The used machines had taken months to restore. He knew their limitations as intimately as he knew their strengths. He had begun dreaming of better ones before the first had paid for itself.

“I don't know.”

Mai nodded. She preferred the answer he had to the answer he knew she would like.

“I want you to have a larger shop,” she said. “I want a house where the roof belongs to us. I want us to buy things without calculating whether the next customer will come back on Friday. I'm not asking you to become a man with no appetite.”

He looked at her.

“I'm asking you to notice when someone is feeding it.”

“I noticed.”

“At the end.”

“Before I said yes.”

She came to stand between his knees. He rested his forehead against her stomach for a moment. Her fingers moved through his hair.

“You smell like the river,” she said.

“There was a boat.”

“One or two?”

He laughed against her shirt.

“Several.”

“Of course there were.”

She lifted his chin. “Wash. Then tell me which of them we're paying for.”

That night, after they had eaten and after Mai had fallen asleep, Minh sat beside the window with a blank sheet in front of him.

He wrote the names of the customers who had returned more than once. Beside each he wrote the work they were likely to need. He calculated what a second mechanic would cost, what the larger room would save in waiting time, how many contracts he could accept without owing his mornings to a single man.

At the bottom, he wrote the number Khang had offered.

Then he crossed it out and kept working.

## 8. Where He Stood

Hòa had asked for a hinge small enough to fit a cupboard door. Minh brought three, because none of the descriptions over the telephone had included a measurement.

“Your father teaches arithmetic,” he told Mai as they left the shop. “He knows numbers exist.”

“He knows you enjoy arriving with more than anyone asked for.”

She sat behind him on the motorbike with a bag of fruit between them. At each bad patch of road she tightened her hands around his waist before the wheels reached it. He adjusted his speed in response. After several years together, the journey had become a conversation they rarely needed to speak aloud.

Hòa lived in a modest house on a quieter street, with a tiled courtyard and a gate that had acquired three different shades of paint. His father, Tín, had come to stay for several weeks. That was the official description. According to Mai, the old man had announced he was visiting, repaired two things nobody had asked him to repair, and begun growing herbs in a cracked basin. No one knew when he intended to leave.

Hòa met them at the gate holding a piece of paper covered in sums.

“You're late.”

“Ten minutes,” Mai said.

“Twelve.”

Minh took the hinges from his pocket. “Now you count.”

Hòa examined them and smiled reluctantly. He was fifty-one, with a teacher's habit of waiting after a question to discover whether a person intended to think. He had never mistaken Minh's silence for stupidity. He had occasionally mistaken Minh's impatience for a correct answer arriving too soon.

“Your students?” Minh asked, nodding at the paper.

“One student. Twelve explanations of the same error.”

“Let me see.”

Hòa held it out. Minh read the page in a glance.

“He carries the number into the wrong column.”

“Yes.”

“Show him with—”

“I've shown him with beans, coins, marks, boxes, fingers, and an apple he ate halfway through the explanation.”

Mai took the fruit inside.

Minh looked again at the paper. The answers were wrong, but the wrongness had a pattern. Each time the child changed the spacing, the error moved with it. He was remembering where the teacher's hand had been rather than what the number meant.

“Turn the page sideways,” Minh said. “Make the columns wider. He's following the shape.”

Hòa studied him for a moment, then folded the sheet and put it away.

“I'll try that.”

“You don't sound convinced.”

“Teaching has cured me of being convinced before someone else understands.”

From inside, Tín called for them to stop discussing children who were not present and come eat.

The old man sat beside the open window. At seventy-eight, he had grown spare through the face without becoming small through the body. His hands rested on his knees, broad and dry, the veins raised beneath skin that had spent most of its life outdoors. A cup of tea stood within reach. Beside it lay a radio with its cover removed.

Minh greeted him and set the fruit on the table.

Tín looked at the swelling beneath his sleeve.

“Pipe?”

Minh hesitated. “Yes.”

“You let it land.”

“There were people behind me.”

The old man nodded. He neither praised the choice nor asked Minh to demonstrate the injury. He picked up a small screw from beside the radio and held it toward him.

“This one doesn't belong.”

Minh took it. The thread was wrong for the case. He looked at the dismantled radio and saw three other screws arranged neatly in a row.

“Someone replaced it.”

“My son.”

Hòa came in behind them. “It held.”

“It split the mounting post.”

“The radio worked.”

“A chair works while you're falling off it.”

Mai put bowls on the table. “Please finish insulting each other's craftsmanship before the food cools.”

They ate with the window open and a fan moving warm air from one side of the room to the other. Hòa asked about the printing business. Mai described the new room, the difficult customer, the deposit she had negotiated from Lan. Tín asked whether the damp patch remained damp. She admitted it did after heavy rain. He said he would look at the gutter when he was next nearby.

“You are not climbing on the roof,” Hòa said.

Tín looked at him.

Hòa continued eating. “I am allowed to say it.”

Minh kept his eyes on his bowl.

“You can laugh,” Tín told him. “He'll be telling you the same thing in thirty years.”

Mai laughed openly. Hòa pointed his spoon at her, then at Minh, and returned to his food with the dignity of a man outnumbered in his own house.

The meal remained ordinary for almost half an hour. They discussed a cousin's wedding, a neighbor's new dog, a roof tile Hòa believed had been dislodged by the people next door. Minh offered to inspect it. Hòa accepted immediately and avoided looking at his father.

Afterward, Mai helped clear the bowls while Minh took the cupboard door off its hinges. Tín sat near him with the radio open across his lap.

“You're busy,” the old man said.

“The shop is improving.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

Minh rested the door against the wall. “You didn't ask anything.”

Tín smiled. “Then answer whatever you prefer.”

For a while Minh worked in silence. He fitted the new hinge, tested the screw holes, and added a small hardwood plug where the old fastening had pulled loose. The old man watched without correcting him.

“Khang came to the shop,” Minh said finally.

“Did you invite him?”

“No.”

“Did he leave?”

“Yes.”

“Then he understands at least one word.”

Minh looked at him. Tín's expression had not changed.

“He wants me working for him again.”

“Do you want that?”

“I want what the money would buy.”

“Good. You know the difference.”

Minh drove the screw into the plug. “Knowing hasn't made him go away.”

“Men who want something rarely disappear because you've identified it.”

The old man set the radio aside. “Bring that chair.”

It was the chair with the loose joint, the one Hòa had been meaning to repair for weeks. Minh brought it over. Tín tested the leg and asked him to hold the frame while he examined the joint.

Minh placed both hands on the wood.

The old man pulled.

The chair did not break. It moved, and Minh moved with it, dragged an inch across the floor despite having braced himself before Tín touched it.

He tightened his grip.

Tín's hand remained loose around the rail.

“Don't fight the chair,” he said. “You'll owe my son another one.”

Minh looked at the old fingers resting against the wood. He could feel the immense reserve behind them, a force so far beyond the small movement being made that it seemed wrong to call both by the same name.

He had felt it before.

Four years earlier, when he was twenty-three, Tín had visited the small room Minh and Mai shared before her business had a proper storefront. Minh had been carrying a press component home because the repairman refused to collect it. The component required three men and a cart. Minh had brought it upstairs alone.

Tín watched him set it down and asked to feel his grip.

At the time, Minh had heard stories about the old man. Most came from people who lowered their voices at the useful parts. He distrusted stories that required a lowered voice. Usually they belonged to men whose greatest achievement was controlling how other people repeated their names.

He took Tín's hand carefully.

“You can hold it,” the old man said.

Minh increased the pressure.

Nothing happened.

He increased it again. The knuckles beneath his fingers might have been cut from the same piece of stone as the floor. Tín remained seated, looking up at him with mild attention. Minh felt heat move into his face.

He put more of himself into the grip.

The old man turned his wrist.

Minh went to his knees.

He did not decide to kneel. One moment his feet held the floor and his shoulder stood above the joined hands. The next, every force in his body had been redirected into a position he could neither resist nor complete without kneeling.

Tín had not moved his chair.

Mai looked in from the small washing area. She saw them and stopped.

“Grandfather.”

“He's all right.”

Minh tried to stand. The old man's fingers closed slightly. The attempt ended before his knees left the floor.

“Let go,” Minh said.

Tín released him immediately.

He stayed down for another second, staring at his own hand. Nothing was broken. Nothing even hurt in a way that would explain the outcome. That offended him more than an injury would have.

Mai brought tea. She did not smile until he looked at her.

“Don't,” he said.

“I haven't said anything.”

“You're preparing.”

She set his cup down. “Then you have time to prepare too.”

Later, after the room had cooled and Mai returned to washing, Tín stood beside him and showed him how he placed his feet when lifting something heavy. Minh copied the stance. He believed he copied it precisely.

Tín touched his heel with one slipper.

Minh fell sideways onto the sleeping mat.

He lay there looking at the ceiling. From the washing area came the sound of a bowl being set down with extreme care.

“You can come laugh in here,” he called.

Mai did.

The old man offered him a hand. Minh took it after a moment's hesitation.

“Again,” he said.

“Tomorrow.”

“I can do it now.”

“Yes. That's why you'll do it badly.”

He asked for a second lesson before Tín left the next morning. The old man told him to walk across the room without making the floor announce each step. Minh spent the next week noticing how often his strength reached a surface before his judgment did.

Now, four years later, he held Hòa's chair while the same old man examined a loose joint.

Tín let go. Minh released the frame slowly.

“You remember,” the old man said.

“I remember the floor.”

“Useful. It's usually there when you need it.”

Minh laughed despite himself.

From the doorway, Hòa asked whether the chair had become an argument. Tín told him the joint needed glue. Hòa brought the glue and stayed to watch, taking ownership of the repair by the simple act of refusing to leave his own furniture alone with them.

“Hold it square,” Tín said.

Minh caught the two sides of the frame. The old man took the opposite rail. This time Minh felt the pull before he saw any movement. He adjusted his feet and let his hips settle. A tile beneath his left heel made a small, dry sound.

Hòa put down the glue.

He placed his right hand on the chair back. He did not grasp it. His fingers lay over the top as they might have rested on the shoulder of a student who needed to remain seated.

Everything stopped.

Minh had been moving the chair toward himself. Tín had been moving it the other way. Now both efforts ended in the hand between them. The old wood gave one complaint and fell silent. Hòa stood with his feet close together, weight apparently on one leg, his other hand still holding the glue cap.

Minh increased the pressure before he could stop himself.

The chair remained exactly where it was.

He looked at Hòa's wrist. The cuff of the teacher's shirt had slipped back. No tendon stood out. His father-in-law looked down at the cracked tile.

“That one matched the others,” Hòa said.

“I can replace it,” Minh said.

“You can try finding the same color after twelve years.”

Across the chair, Tín studied his son. The amusement had left his face, though he did not look angry. He looked attentive in a way Minh had never seen when anyone else touched something he was holding.

Hòa turned his head.

“Both of you. Enough.”

Tín released the rail.

Minh did too. Hòa lifted the chair with the same hand and set it on the newspaper. He poured glue into the joint, turned the leg, and asked Minh to bring the cord from the cupboard.

The request sounded ordinary. That made it take Minh a moment to answer.

When he returned, Hòa was kneeling beside the repair. Mai leaned in to ask about tea. Her father told her where the good leaves were, then resumed tying the chair as if nothing interesting had happened.

Minh went with Tín to wash their hands.

“How much was that?” he asked quietly.

“Enough to protect a chair.”

“I'm asking about him.”

“I noticed.”

Tín ran water over his fingers. Minh waited beside the basin.

“Could he do that if you wanted him not to?”

The old man turned off the tap.

“My son has spent fifty-one years making his own decisions. I've found it useful to remember that.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“No.”

Minh lowered his voice further. “Is he stronger than you?”

Tín looked at him for long enough that the question began to feel badly chosen. Then he dried his hands on a towel.

“He hasn't asked me to settle that. Neither have I.”

From the other room, Hòa called for somebody to stop the kettle boiling dry. Tín went immediately. Minh remained by the basin, thinking about a man who asked for cupboard hinges without supplying a measurement, who knew exactly where a child had gone wrong on a page, and whose right hand had just ended a contest Minh had not known he was losing twice.

In the courtyard, Mai was watering the herbs in the cracked basin. Minh joined her after the cupboard door had been rehung.

“He still does that,” she said.

“Moves things?”

“Makes you quiet.”

Minh looked back toward the house. Tín and Hòa were arguing about how long the glue needed to set.

“He doesn't make me.”

“No. You volunteer after the first attempt.”

He took the watering can from her when it became heavy. She let him finish the row.

“Your grandfather knows about Khang.”

“I told him you left. I didn't give him a report.”

“He asked.”

“Of course he did.”

Minh set the can down. “I don't want him dragged into it.”

Mai looked at him for a moment, then toward the open door.

“I don't think you've understood what would happen if somebody tried dragging him anywhere.”

He smiled. “I mean the trouble.”

“I know what you mean.”

She took his hand, turning the swollen forearm into the light.

“You can ask him something without making him responsible for fixing it.”

“I asked him enough four years ago.”

“And you learned to walk.”

He shook his head. “You're enjoying this.”

“I've earned it.”

They left before evening. Hòa packed fruit back into the bag they had brought, because visits involved returning at least part of what had been carried in. Tín walked with them to the gate.

He put one hand on Minh's shoulder. The weight was ordinary. Minh knew how much choice went into making it so.

“Come back next Sunday,” the old man said.

“We will.”

“Bring the right screw for the radio.”

“I can make one.”

“I know.”

On the ride home, Mai rested her forehead against his back. The road had cooled. Streetlights came on one by one, their reflections breaking in puddles left by a brief afternoon shower.

For almost an hour, nobody asked Minh to frighten anyone.

When they reached the shop, a note waited beneath the shutter.

It held an address, a time, and seven names written in Duy's hand.

## 9. Empty Addresses

The address on Duy's note belonged to a laundry that no longer existed.

Its sign remained above the door, sun-faded and missing one corner. Inside, a man sold repaired fans from shelves that had once held folded clothes. He looked at the seven names and said he knew none of them.

“A man left this address,” Minh said.

“Then ask the man.”

“I intend to. I'm asking you first.”

The seller wiped a fan blade with a cloth. “You look like someone I'd prefer to disappoint quickly.”

Minh put the paper away. “That's useful honesty.”

He stepped back into the lane. A woman across the street was cutting vegetables into a basin. She had watched the conversation through the open door. When Minh looked toward her, she nodded at the narrow passage beside the former laundry.

“The back room,” she said. “He should have told you the back room.”

“Who?”

“Whoever wanted you to find it.”

The passage led to a courtyard containing a rusted boiler, a clothesline, and a table beneath a sheet of corrugated roofing. Three men sat at the table. One had a hand wrapped in a support made from cloth and thin bamboo. The second wore a driver's jacket despite the heat. The third was asleep with his cheek against his forearm.

The man with the injured hand saw Minh and stood so violently that his chair fell.

“No.”

Minh stopped at the passage entrance.

“Duy sent me.”

“That bastard.”

The driver in the jacket stood as well. The sleeping man woke and looked from one face to another, already reaching for something beneath the table.

Minh recognized the first man by his voice.

He had heard it through the cold-store door in April.

The recognition changed the courtyard. The old boiler, the basin by the drain, the broken chair leg beneath the table became fixed points around a memory Minh had allowed to remain mostly sound.

“Your name is Lộc,” he said.

“You learned it now?”

Minh looked at the note.

Lộc gave a short, hard laugh. “He sent you a list. How thoughtful.”

The third man brought a knife above the table. It was a kitchen knife, broad-bladed, with a wooden handle darkened by use. Minh watched it without moving.

“I didn't come to collect anything.”

“You came last time to stand outside,” Lộc said. “Apparently that was different too.”

Minh let the words land. He had nothing to gain by ducking them.

“It wasn't.”

The man with the knife looked uncertain. He had expected an argument that would justify keeping the blade between them. Minh's answer had not made him safer, only less certain where the next moment would go.

“Duy said someone could help,” the driver in the jacket said.

“He should have given you my name.”

“Would you have come if he told you ours?” Lộc asked.

“Yes.”

“You answer quickly.”

“You can ask again later.”

Minh remained in the passage. He did not enter the courtyard until the man with the knife lowered it.

Lộc picked up his chair and sat. “Stand there. That's what you're good at.”

Minh stood.

They had been brought to the cold store because seven drivers disputed deductions against cargo they said had arrived intact. The company accused them of selling part of the loads. Some had. Two admitted it at once, with the exhausted defiance of men whose small thefts had become the justification for a much larger one. Others had not. All seven were told to sign the same admission and accept balances calculated before they entered the building.

“I refused,” Lộc said, lifting his wrapped hand. “For a while.”

Minh watched the fingers move badly beneath the support.

“Who broke it?”

“You want a name to give your friend?”

“I want a name.”

“Trọng held me. Duy brought the paper. A man I didn't know used a hammer.”

The driver in the jacket looked away. The third man kept the knife beside his glass.

“Did Khang come?” Minh asked.

“After. Clean shirt. Very unhappy with what had happened. You'd have thought we owed him an apology for making a mess.”

Lộc reached under the table and produced a folded account statement. Minh took it only when the man held it out.

He read the figures.

The deductions had been calculated against the full retail value of missing cargo, then repeated against the transport contract, then added to a loan balance that already included a penalty for the same alleged loss. Three different descriptions for the same money. The driver had continued working because each payment seemed to reduce something until the next page restored it under another name.

“This was designed never to end,” Minh said.

“Congratulations. You read faster than I did.”

He turned the page and looked at the route entries. A warehouse code appeared beside cargo delivered on a day the building had been closed for repairs. Another entry charged Lộc for a refrigerated load carried in a truck without refrigeration.

“These journeys aren't yours.”

Lộc stared at him.

“That truck couldn't have carried this load. And you couldn't have completed both of these routes in the time listed.”

“I told them that.”

“I believe you.”

The man looked down at his hand. “That would have been useful in April.”

Minh put the account back on the table. “Yes.”

The courtyard remained quiet except for the fans being tested in the room at the front. Their motors rose and fell as the seller moved from one machine to the next.

“What can you do?” the driver in the jacket asked.

Minh looked at all three men before answering.

He could walk into Khang's office and break the desk. He could carry Trọng into the courtyard and make him kneel beside Lộc's chair. He could take money, hand it over, and leave three men waiting for the consequence of his departure. Every option arrived complete in his head, force finding the shortest path through a problem that would outlast force.

“I can get these read by someone who doesn't work for him,” he said. “I can help you give statements. I can make it difficult for him to visit you afterward.”

“Difficult isn't impossible.”

“No.”

Lộc studied him. “You're the man people say lifted the engine.”

“Yes.”

“And that's the best you can offer?”

Minh met his eyes. “It's the part I can promise.”

The answer disappointed them. He saw it. They had been brought a man who looked like the end of an argument, and he was discussing copies and statements as if he belonged to the same slow world that had failed them before.

Then the gate at the far side of the courtyard opened.

A fourth man entered, carrying a bag of bread. He saw Minh and nearly dropped it.

“He's with us,” the driver in the jacket said, not quite believing it himself.

The newcomer shut the gate quickly.

“Two men at the corner,” he said. “Same motorbike as yesterday.”

Lộc's face changed. “You brought them.”

Minh looked toward the passage.

“Maybe.”

“Maybe?”

“Stay here.”

He went through the former laundry and into the street. The fan seller glanced up as he passed.

“Find what you needed?”

“More than I expected.”

The motorbike stood beneath a tree at the corner. Its rider watched a newspaper without turning a page. The passenger had positioned himself to see both the laundry and the side passage. Minh crossed the street and approached from behind the tree.

The passenger noticed first. He touched the rider's shoulder. The engine started.

Minh caught the rear frame.

The rider opened the throttle. The rear wheel spun against the road, smoked, and rose as Minh lifted it clear. The engine screamed uselessly. Both men froze, the rider gripping the handlebars while the front wheel remained on the ground.

“Switch it off,” Minh said.

The engine stopped.

“Get off.”

They dismounted carefully. Minh kept the rear of the motorbike suspended in one hand.

“Who sent you?”

“We were waiting for someone.”

“You've found him. Who sent you?”

The passenger looked at the wheel hanging beside Minh's hip.

“Vũ.”

“Did he know I'd come?”

“He told us to watch the laundry.”

Minh set the motorbike down. The suspension compressed beneath it with a hard little bounce.

“Then you can tell him what you saw.”

“That's all?” the rider asked before he could stop himself.

Minh stepped closer. “Would you like something else to report?”

The man shook his head.

“Don't come back to this street.”

They left without putting their helmets on until they had reached the next corner.

The woman cutting vegetables watched Minh return.

“Are they gone?”

“For now.”

She nodded toward the laundry. “Those men have enough trouble.”

“I know.”

“Then don't become another piece of it.”

Inside the courtyard, the newcomer had distributed the bread. The knife lay on the far side of the table now, beyond easy reach. Minh took that as permission to sit, but waited until Lộc pointed at an empty chair.

“They were watching before I arrived,” he said.

“Who?”

“Vũ's men.”

Lộc closed his eyes. “Of course.”

“How many of the seven still drive?”

“Four. One went home. One sells spare parts now. I do whatever this hand lets me.”

Minh looked at the names. “Can you reach the others?”

“Some.”

“Ask whether they'll speak together. No one gives away their only paper. No one meets a company man alone because he promises to settle quietly.”

The driver in the jacket leaned back. “You sound as if you've heard the promise before.”

“I used to stand outside while they made it.”

Lộc's mouth tightened, but he did not look away this time.

They talked for another hour. The men disagreed about how much to say, whether the two who had actually stolen cargo would make everyone less believable, whether accepting money for medical treatment meant they had surrendered the right to ask for anything else. Minh did not settle those questions by announcing himself the strongest person in the courtyard. He listened, asked for dates, and wrote down which claims belonged to which men.

When he left, Lộc followed him to the passage.

“Why did Duy send you?”

“I don't know yet.”

“He brought the paper,” Lộc said. “He didn't use the hammer. That's what he'll tell you.”

Minh waited.

“He knew when to turn the page so the blood wouldn't get on the signature.”

The fan in the front room began turning, its repaired bearings almost silent.

Minh folded the note and put it in his pocket.

“I'll ask him about that too.”

## 10. The Shirt He Wore

Nhi had seven men's shirts hanging from a line across the room and a pistol on the table beneath them.

She opened the door to Minh, looked at his face, and stepped aside.

“If you're here to break something, choose the gun. I need the machine.”

The sewing machine stood by the window, its foot pedal worn smooth. Half a hotel uniform lay beneath the needle. Nhi made alterations for people whose employers considered one size an acceptable description of several bodies. Minh had known her almost as long as he had known Duy. She had once repaired a split seam in his wedding trousers while telling him that being large did not excuse refusing to stand still.

“Where is he?” Minh asked.

“That's a popular question today.”

She closed the door. The pistol remained on the table, untouched.

“Who else asked?”

“A man who wouldn't give his name. Then another man who said he was a friend. I told both of them they should know where their friend lives.”

“Did they come inside?”

“No.”

“Did they insist?”

Nhi looked at him. “I'm still here, aren't I?”

She was thirty, with a narrow face and a quick way of speaking that made most interruptions arrive too late. Her hair was pinned up with a pencil. On the bed lay an open travel bag containing folded clothes, a small tin of jewelry, and a stack of paper patterns tied with string.

Minh noticed the bag. Nhi noticed him noticing.

“I'm going to my sister's.”

“For how long?”

“Until my husband can answer a question without beginning three other stories.”

He pulled out a chair, keeping clear of the pistol.

“Do you know why those men came?”

“Duy owes money. Or someone owes him money. Or there is money nobody can admit exists. The details change. The visits don't.”

“Has he been gambling?”

“He says no.”

“What do you think?”

“I think he's learned to use people instead of cards.”

She went to the stove and took a kettle off the heat. Two cups already stood beside it. Minh wondered how long she had been expecting someone whose arrival would explain the gun.

“Did he leave that here?”

“This morning. He put it under a shirt as if I don't move shirts for a living.”

Minh waited while she poured tea.

“He said it was for protection.”

“Do you know how to use it?”

“I know how to telephone you.”

The answer was dry, but he heard the fear beneath it.

“Call me if anyone returns.”

“You didn't answer whether he has been gambling.”

“You asked what I know about it. I don't know.”

“Then you're becoming difficult company too.”

He accepted the cup. A shirt sleeve moved in the draft from the window, brushing the table every few seconds. Nhi caught it and pinned it higher.

“How is Mai?”

“Busy. She rented another room.”

“Good. Tell her I can help with cloth covers if she starts doing more albums.”

“I will.”

“And tell her I didn't ask you for a discount.”

Minh smiled. “She'll appreciate the distinction.”

They drank for a moment without discussing Duy. The room had the crowded order of work conducted where people also slept. Everything belonged to two parts of a life at once. A cupboard held bowls on one shelf and spare bobbins on another. The bed served as cutting table when no one was in it. Minh understood how much care it took to prevent a business from swallowing the place meant to survive it.

“He thinks the money makes this temporary,” Nhi said suddenly.

“What?”

She indicated the room. “The sewing. The rented place. Me working while he comes home with envelopes. He keeps promising the day I won't have to do it.”

“Do you want to stop?”

“I want a better machine. I want clients who pay when they collect. I want him to understand those are ambitions, not symptoms of being poor.”

Minh looked at the travel bag again. “Have you told him?”

“In sentences even he could afford to remember.”

Footsteps came up the stairs outside. Nhi put her cup down. Minh stood before the key entered the lock.

Duy opened the door and stopped.

“You found him,” Nhi said.

Duy looked at the pistol, then at Minh.

“Why are you here?”

“You left me seven names.”

“You met them?”

“Yes.”

Nhi folded her arms. “Now there are seven.”

“This isn't your concern,” Duy said, and regretted it before she answered.

“It's on my table. That makes it easy to mistake for my concern.”

She pointed at the pistol. Duy went toward it. Minh put a hand on the chair back between them.

“Leave it.”

“It's mine.”

“We know. She'd like the table back.”

Duy stopped moving. He smelled of cigarettes and the sweet edge of liquor, but his eyes were clear. He had not drunk enough to excuse himself, only enough to make the excuse available if he needed it.

“You should have called,” he told Minh.

“You should have told Lộc who was coming.”

Nhi looked from one man to the other. “What happened to Lộc?”

Duy's jaw tightened.

“An old work matter.”

“He can't close his hand,” Minh said.

The room became very quiet.

Nhi sat on the bed beside the open bag. “What did you do?”

“I didn't break it.”

“What did you do?”

Duy turned toward Minh. Anger came into his face, welcome because it gave him somewhere else to look.

“You stood outside. You think that makes you better?”

“I haven't said it does.”

“You walked away with money. You opened a shop. Now you come here looking at me like—”

“Like the man who sent me to hear what happened.”

Duy stopped.

“You wrote the names,” Minh said. “You gave me the time. If you wanted me to stay ignorant, you chose an unusual way to ask.”

Nhi watched her husband. She was no longer looking at the gun.

Duy sat down. He rubbed both hands over his face, then let them fall between his knees.

“I thought you could help them.”

“Why didn't you tell them yourself?”

“They wouldn't trust me.”

“They didn't trust me either.”

“But they listened.”

“After they recognized who I'd been.”

Duy nodded slowly.

Minh pulled the other chair closer. He sat, leaving the pistol untouched at the far end of the table.

“Did you load Tấn's tools?”

Duy looked at Nhi before answering.

“Yes.”

“Who told you to?”

“Trọng.”

“Why?”

“Equipment was being sold. The owner wanted things cleared.”

“Tấn didn't sell his tools.”

“They were in the box.”

“You knew whose box it was.”

Duy looked down at his hands. “Yes.”

“Were you at the warehouse when it burned?”

The question remained in the room for several breaths.

“I was there earlier.”

“How much earlier?”

“During the day.”

Minh watched his face. Duy had always been good at lying to men he did not care about. He became less accomplished when the lie had to preserve something he wanted to keep.

“You're choosing the edges of your answer,” Minh said.

“Because you keep asking questions that can kill people.”

“Three people are already dead.”

Duy stood so abruptly that the chair hit the wall. Nhi did not flinch. That, more than the movement itself, told Minh how often she had waited through this part.

“You think I don't know?” Duy said.

“Then tell me what you know.”

“Not here.”

Nhi laughed once, without warmth. “My house is suitable for a gun, but not a sentence.”

“I'm trying to keep you out of it.”

“You brought it upstairs.”

She rose and closed the travel bag. The sound of the fastening was small and final.

“I'm going to my sister's,” she said.

“For how long?”

“Until I decide to come back.”

Duy looked at Minh as if his presence had produced the bag. Minh stayed seated.

“You can take a taxi,” Duy told her. “I'll give you money.”

“I have money.”

“Nhi—”

“I have money because people pay me for something I do. You should try explaining yours in one sentence.”

She lifted the bag. Minh stood and took it from her. Duy moved toward the door, then stopped when she shook her head.

“Stay,” she said. “Your friend came to speak to you.”

Minh carried the bag downstairs. Nhi followed with the paper patterns under one arm. At the foot of the stairs she paused.

“Is he in danger?”

Minh looked up toward the apartment.

“Yes.”

She closed her eyes briefly. “I thought so.”

“Give Mai your sister's number.”

“I will.”

“And don't tell anyone from his work where you're going.”

Nhi looked at him. “He knows.”

“Then tell Mai if you decide to go somewhere else.”

She understood the distinction. He disliked having made her understand it, but she would have disliked being kept comfortable at the expense of knowing more.

They found a taxi at the larger road. Minh put the bag inside. Nhi took his hand for a moment before getting in.

“Don't let him turn you into an excuse,” she said.

“He'll have trouble.”

“He's practiced.”

She closed the door and gave the driver the address herself.

Upstairs, Duy had put the pistol away. The shirts still hung across the room. He stood beneath them, holding the cup Nhi had left.

“You helped her leave.”

“She was leaving.”

“You could have said something.”

“What would you like me to say that she hasn't already heard?”

Duy looked down into the tea.

“I didn't ask you to come here for this.”

“You didn't ask me to come here at all.”

The younger man sat. For the first time that evening he looked simply tired, with no anger arranged over it.

“I don't know how to stop,” he said.

Minh took the chair opposite him.

“Start with the next thing you're being asked to do.”

“It doesn't work like that.”

“Sometimes it does.”

“For you. People look at you and decide the trouble isn't worth it.”

“You were there when I started. They didn't decide that immediately.”

Duy gave a faint smile. “No. They were usually on the floor first.”

Minh let the smile remain for a moment.

“Why send the names?”

Duy rubbed his thumb around the rim of Nhi's cup. “Because someone has to say what happened before the men who remember it disappear.”

“Including you?”

He did not answer.

Outside, a motorbike passed slowly and continued without stopping. Both men listened until it had gone.

“When you're ready,” Minh said, “you tell me. You don't send another address and hope it says the difficult part for you.”

Duy nodded.

Minh stood. At the door he looked back at the room, the unfinished uniform, the small indentation Nhi's sewing machine had worn in the floor.

“And put that gun somewhere it won't make a decision for you.”

Duy kept both hands around the empty cup until Minh had closed the door.

## 11. The Inspection Pit

Bình's problem made a sound every seventh revolution.

Minh heard it before he entered the yard. A rear-engined coach stood beneath the roof with its access panels open. Two mechanics watched the engine while a third sat in the driver's seat, maintaining a steady idle. Bình stood at the rear with a screwdriver held against the casing and its handle pressed to his ear.

“Switch it off,” Minh said.

Bình looked up. “You haven't heard it properly.”

“I've heard enough.”

The engine stopped. For a moment, the fans on the wall seemed enormously loud.

“It disappears when it's cold,” Bình said.

“I know.”

“You know everything today?”

“Only the expensive part.”

The fleet manager removed the screwdriver from his ear. He had broad shoulders, a receding hairline, and the habit of folding invoices into small squares that became impossible to flatten. Minh had repaired a pump for him in August. Since then, Bình had sent jobs he did not have time or equipment to finish, always with a note explaining what had already been tried.

“I ordered a replacement,” he said. “It's wrong.”

“Wrong model?”

“Wrong in a more creative way.”

He showed Minh the part on the bench. It looked right until Minh turned it over. The mounting surfaces had been machined unevenly, then dressed to conceal the difference. Someone had made a cheaper component resemble the one named on the box.

“Where did you buy it?”

Bình pointed at the invoice.

Minh recognized the supplier's stamp. The company supplied Đông Lợi as well.

“How many came in the batch?”

“Four.”

“Don't use the others.”

“I've already put one in another bus.”

“Where is it?”

Bình looked toward the gate.

Minh followed his gaze. A coach had just started moving toward the road. Its passengers were not aboard yet; it was leaving to collect them at another yard. The engine note rose as the driver changed gear.

The false rhythm appeared beneath it.

Minh ran.

He reached the coach before its rear cleared the gate. With one hand on the frame, he matched its speed for three steps, then pulled himself onto the rear service ledge. The access panel latch resisted. He tore it free.

Inside, the engine shuddered on its mounts.

“Stop!” Bình shouted from the yard.

The driver did not hear over the engine and traffic. Minh saw the damaged assembly begin to move out of alignment. A rotating component touched its housing. The sound sharpened into a scream.

He drove his hand between the moving parts.

The engine stopped as if it had struck a wall.

The coach lurched. The driver braked hard. Minh kept his feet beneath him while the ruined assembly tried to twist his arm inside the casing. He turned against it, feeling the last stored force pass through his shoulder and into the frame.

Then everything became still.

Smoke rose from between his fingers.

Bình reached the back of the bus and looked inside. For several seconds he said nothing.

“Are you burned?”

“A little.”

“Your hand was in the engine.”

“I noticed.”

The driver came around the side. He saw Minh on the rear ledge and swore with the bewildered relief of a man discovering that the impossible noise had belonged to someone else's decision.

“What the fuck happened?”

“Bad part,” Bình said.

Minh pulled his hand free. The fingers were red and streaked with grease. He flexed them one by one. Nothing had torn. The skin would blister later, but not enough to prevent him working.

They pushed the coach back into the yard. Minh did most of the pushing. The others contributed partly to steer and partly because standing idle while one man moved a bus offended everyone who had spent his life moving things in groups.

“You can tell the driver to stop,” Bình said when they had it over the inspection pit.

“I did.”

“You can wait for him to hear.”

“The engine didn't intend to.”

Bình looked at the broken assembly in Minh's hand. “You're going to charge me for arguing?”

“Only if it continues.”

They worked through the afternoon. Minh laid out the failed parts and compared them with the unused ones. He made no performance of the calculations. He watched the wear patterns, measured the surfaces, and reconstructed the order in which the forces had moved through the housing. By the time Bình returned from telephoning the supplier, he had drawn the fault on a sheet of wrapping paper.

“They say we fitted it badly,” Bình said.

Minh pointed at the drawing.

“Then ask how we fitted the unused one badly too.”

Bình looked at the bench. “They're sending a man.”

“Good.”

“You won't say that when you meet him.”

The man arrived in a car with Vũ beside him.

Minh had not seen Vũ since leaving Khang's employment. He looked much the same: pale shirt, tidy cuffs, cigarette held away from the body. He smiled when he found Minh beneath the rear of the coach.

“You're difficult to keep track of.”

Minh slid out of the inspection pit and stood.

“You should take up a different hobby.”

The supplier's representative held a folder against his chest. He was a small man with a careful comb-over and a tie loosened in the heat. He looked at Vũ before introducing himself to Bình.

“We've reviewed the complaint.”

“You've seen nothing,” Bình said.

“The part is standard stock.”

Minh put the unused component in his hands.

“Then you'll recognize what's wrong.”

The representative turned it over. His expression changed before he controlled it.

“This could have been altered after delivery.”

“By whom?” Bình asked.

“I'm not making an accusation.”

“You're building a little house around one.”

Vũ put his hand on the representative's shoulder.

“Let's discuss a replacement. No one wants a quarrel.”

“I want four replacements,” Bình said. “And the cost of the damage.”

“That may be difficult.”

“Then discuss it carefully.”

Minh stood beside the bench, hands loose at his sides. He could see Vũ evaluating the yard: the number of workers, the open gate, the customers waiting near the office. The man preferred to conduct threats where the listener had more to lose than witnesses had to gain.

“You have a contract coming up for renewal,” Vũ told Bình.

The fleet manager's face hardened.

“We're talking about parts.”

“We're talking about relationships. A supplier has a problem. A customer has a problem. Sometimes the sensible thing is to share the cost and continue doing business.”

Minh took the component from the representative and placed it beside the ruined assembly.

“Look at them.”

Vũ did not move his eyes.

“A bus full of people could have been on the road when this failed.”

“It wasn't.”

“Because we stopped it.”

“Then everyone has been fortunate.”

Minh stepped closer. “Don't call my hand your good fortune.”

The representative backed half a pace. Vũ stayed where he was.

“You don't work here,” he said.

“He does today,” Bình answered.

Vũ looked at him. “You want to be careful about making someone else's disagreement your own.”

Bình pointed at the bus. “I was under that before he arrived. It was already mine.”

The yard had become quiet. The two younger mechanics stood near the office, watching. Minh saw pride enter Bình's face alongside the fear. It was an uncomfortable mixture, and one Khang's men had learned to exploit. A frightened man could retreat. A proud frightened man often needed to be pushed hard enough that retreat looked like defeat.

Minh refused to provide the push.

“Replace the parts,” he told the representative. “Take the damaged ones. Write down what you're taking. Bình keeps one of the unused pieces until the matter is settled.”

“You can't dictate—”

Minh picked up the failed housing and closed his hand around it.

The metal compressed with a grinding crack.

He set it down again. His hand had left four deep impressions along the side.

“I can be less precise,” he said. “Would that improve the conversation?”

The representative stared at the housing. Vũ glanced toward the open gate.

“We'll replace the parts,” he said.

Bình took a clean sheet from the office. The representative wrote the acknowledgment while Vũ smoked outside. When the paper was signed, Minh went to stand beside him.

“The men at the laundry,” Minh said.

“What men?”

“The ones who became less comfortable on their motorbike.”

Vũ exhaled slowly. “They were watching an account.”

“Watch it somewhere else.”

“You're protecting a great many people. You should keep a list.”

“Duy gave me one.”

The cigarette paused on its way to Vũ's mouth.

There. Not surprise exactly. A piece of information being placed beside something already known.

“Did he?”

“Ask him.”

“I will.”

Minh moved close enough that Vũ lowered the cigarette rather than risk having it pressed into his shirt.

“Ask him with your mouth.”

Vũ looked up at him.

“You always think the dangerous part is the man standing in front of you.”

“No. I think he's the part I can reach.”

For the first time, Vũ looked away first.

The representative came out with the signed paper. They got into the car and left. Bình remained in the gate until it turned out of sight.

“That cost something,” he said.

“The parts were already costing you.”

“You know what I mean.”

Minh looked at the yard. Three workers had returned to the bus. One was sweeping the broken bits from beneath it. They were already trying to restore the day to something they could finish.

“Yes.”

Bình folded the acknowledgment, reconsidered, and carried it flat into the office.

When he came back, he brought a clean shirt and two cups of coffee. Minh accepted both. They sat on the low wall beside the inspection pit, legs hanging over the edge.

“You could make a fortune doing nothing but what you did to that housing,” Bình said.

“There isn't much demand for crushed housings.”

“Don't be a clever bastard. You know what I mean.”

Minh drank. “I want a larger shop.”

“Then take the work I can't fit in. I'll send it.”

“You already do.”

“I'll send more. On paper. A real arrangement.”

“You saw who was here.”

Bình looked at the bus. “I saw who kept it from going onto the road.”

They discussed rates until the coffee cooled. Neither offered a friendship as a substitute for payment. Minh respected that. When they reached an agreement, Bình wrote it down, made a second copy, and handed one to him.

It was less money than Khang had offered. It belonged entirely to the work.

As Minh prepared to leave, one of the younger mechanics approached him.

“If they come back,” the man said, “should we call you?”

Minh looked at Bình, then at the open gate.

“Call for help. Tell people what is happening. You don't have to keep quiet while someone decides how much you owe for being afraid.”

The mechanic nodded.

Minh put the signed agreement in his shirt pocket and rode home with grease beneath his nails and a new, legitimate reason to be tired.

## 12. A Room With Two Exits

Mai read Bình's agreement twice and set it beside the account book without saying anything.

Minh waited.

“Well?”

“I'm enjoying the part where nobody owns you at the end.”

“It's less money.”

“I can read numbers too.”

He stood beside the binding table with his hands in his pockets. The room had acquired shelves, a wall clock, and a stack of albums awaiting covers. A customer had brought a cake in thanks for an order completed early. Lan had cut it into pieces of such precise equality that Mai accused her of auditing the icing.

“There will be more work,” Minh said.

“Then you'll need help.”

“I can manage it.”

“You can carry a bus. You still can't be at two counters when people arrive at both.”

He considered the figures again. “An apprentice first.”

“Someone you intend to teach, or someone you intend to move out of your way?”

“Do you want to write the advertisement?”

“Desperately.”

She took his wrist and looked at the red marks from the engine. The gesture had become familiar over the previous week. Minh was beginning to dislike how often he provided a reason for it.

“I didn't need stitches,” he said.

“An ambitious standard.”

Lan came in carrying a stack of clean envelopes. Behind her walked a woman with graying hair, broad hands, and the brisk expression of someone who had spent years arriving after other people made mistakes.

“My sister,” Lan said. “Hồng.”

Minh greeted her. Hồng looked at his hand before looking at his face.

“What did you put it into?”

“An engine.”

“Was the engine invited to put something into you?”

Mai laughed. Lan put the envelopes down with evident satisfaction.

“I thought you'd get along.”

Hồng took Minh's hand, turned it, and examined the skin. She did not ask permission in a way that suggested refusal would change the inspection. Minh allowed it.

“Keep it clean,” she said. “If it worsens, have someone properly examine it. And stop using it as a machine part.”

“I'll try.”

“That sounded beautifully insincere.”

She let go and accepted the piece of cake Lan offered.

Lộc arrived half an hour later with two of the other drivers. He remained in the doorway when he saw Minh, then looked at Mai as if asking which part of the room belonged to whom.

She introduced herself, told him where to sit, and asked whether he wanted tea before discussing anything else. She made the choice ordinary. Lộc accepted tea.

The men had brought their accounts, folded contracts, and slips recording payments made in cash. One carried everything in a tin that had once held biscuits. Another brought only a shirt-pocket notebook because he had surrendered the larger papers when Duy promised the balances would be corrected.

Mai spread a clean cloth over the binding table before they laid anything down.

“These are yours,” she said. “We'll make copies of what you want copied. You take the originals when you leave.”

The man with the notebook visibly relaxed.

“What will it cost?”

“For this, nothing.”

He looked at Minh.

“She's speaking for her business,” Minh said.

Mai nodded without looking up. “He does learn.”

They worked through the papers in uneven order. The drivers remembered events more clearly than dates. An argument at a gate had happened after a cousin's wedding. A payment had been made on the day a tire burst. A missing trip belonged to the week one man's mother went to stay with his sister. Mai wrote those details separately rather than forcing them into dates they did not know.

Lan asked who had actually witnessed each payment. Hồng examined Lộc's hand when he finally allowed her to. She listened to his description of the injury and told him she wanted a doctor to look at it again.

“I've had it looked at,” he said.

“Then have it looked at by someone with time to hear the whole story.”

“That costs.”

“So does leaving it as it is.”

Minh leaned against the wall. He could make most calculations before Mai finished reading the amounts aloud, but he did not interrupt. She understood the people speaking. He understood the figures. On the occasions those were different problems, hers mattered first.

After an hour, Lộc looked at him.

“You could have paid the balances yourself.”

“Yes.”

“You have that much?”

“Some. I could find the rest.”

“Why not?”

Minh pushed away from the wall. “Because they would discover another balance as soon as the money arrived. You'd still need someone to explain why this one exists.”

Lộc rubbed his good hand over his face.

“I'm tired of explaining.”

“Then let them answer something.”

Mai placed two copies beside the original statement. On a separate sheet she had listed the duplicated charges without telling anyone what legal word they should use for them.

Lộc read slowly. When he reached the final line, his mouth moved as if he were testing a phrase aloud without wanting the others to hear.

“I can take this?”

“It's yours,” Mai said again.

The courier arrived while the men were gathering their papers.

He carried an envelope addressed to Liên and a smaller one containing delivery instructions. Minh recognized him from Khang's office. The young man stopped at the sight of the drivers, then recovered his professional expression.

“Mr. Khang asked for this to be given to the widow.”

“She has a name,” Mai said.

The courier looked at the envelope. “Mrs. Liên.”

“Why bring it here?” Minh asked.

“He said you'd know where to find her.”

“He knows too.”

The courier held out the second envelope. “She needs to sign for receipt.”

Mai opened it. She read the attached sheet and put it on the table in front of Minh.

The acknowledgment did more than record money. It described the payment as settlement of all outstanding matters associated with Tấn's employment and death. Near the bottom, a sentence stated that the recipient understood the fire to have resulted from equipment failure.

Minh looked at the courier.

“Who wrote this?”

“The office.”

“Which person?”

“I don't know.”

Mai picked up the telephone. She called Liên and asked whether she could come to the room. She did not tell the courier to leave. She gave him a chair and a glass of water, which somehow made him look more trapped than a threat would have.

Lộc and the other drivers watched silently. Their own papers remained on the table, making the arrival of another document feel less like an exception than the next example in a lesson everyone had attended unwillingly.

Liên arrived wearing the same sandals Minh remembered from the warehouse. She read the acknowledgment while standing.

“No,” she said.

The courier looked relieved to have an answer simple enough to carry back.

“You don't want the payment?”

“I want the wages. I don't know what caused the fire. I won't sign a sentence saying I do.”

Minh took a blank sheet from beside Mai's pen and began to speak. Liên stopped him with one raised hand.

“Let me.”

He stepped back.

She wrote a plain acknowledgment of the amount owed for Tấn's work, listing the days and expenses. Her handwriting was slower than Mai's and pressed harder into the paper. When she finished, she signed her name and held the sheet out to the courier.

“This is what I can say. Take it to whoever wants a different sentence.”

The young man looked at Minh. Minh looked back until he turned toward the woman who had actually spoken.

“I'll need to telephone,” he said.

Mai indicated the instrument. The whole room listened to his half of the conversation. He repeated the amount. He said the original acknowledgment had been refused. He listened for a long time, shoulders gradually rising toward his ears.

Then he put the receiver down.

“Mr. Khang says to pay it.”

Liên accepted the envelope and counted the money in front of him. The amount was correct. She signed her own acknowledgment, gave him one copy, and kept the other.

Only when he had gone did she sit down.

“That's what he should have done the first time,” Lộc said.

“Yes,” Liên answered. “It only took a room full of people.”

Minh looked around the room. He had begun the week thinking in terms of men he could reach, doors he could open, accounts he could force someone to settle. Here were people who had made a small event difficult to conceal simply by being present for it.

He found the result satisfying in a way he had not expected.

When the visitors left, Mai stayed at the table sorting the duplicate sheets. Lan took the originals that belonged to her customers back to the office. Hồng washed the cups and complained that nobody in the building understood how long a proper lunch required.

Minh tested the rear door.

“It sticks,” Mai said.

“At the bottom.”

“You can fix it tomorrow.”

He crouched and examined the swollen timber. “I'll do it now.”

She watched him take the hinge pins out.

“You're worried.”

“Vũ had men watching the laundry.”

“I assumed someone was watching something.”

“That isn't a reason to accept it.”

“I haven't accepted it. I told Lan. Her neighbor knows who belongs at the rear gate. The storeroom owner will open his courtyard if we need it.”

Minh looked through the doorway. The rear passage connected several properties and narrowed near a locked metal gate. Beyond it, another door led toward the adjoining storeroom.

“Keep this clear,” he said, moving a bundle of discarded cardboard.

“It is clear.”

“Clear enough for someone carrying a person.”

Mai stood beside him. They looked at the passage together.

“Then show me what needs moving,” she said.

He did. They shifted boxes, rehung the sticking door, and marked which neighbor held each key. Hồng watched for a while, then fetched a small first-aid box from upstairs and placed it on a shelf where everyone could see it.

“I would prefer not to use that,” she said.

“So would I,” Minh answered.

“You say that like a man who considers preference a minor detail.”

Mai took his hand. “Sometimes he needs help ranking them.”

Later, after the others had gone, they sat on the binding table with their feet resting on a crate. Minh told her about Nhi's departure and the gun on the table. Mai listened without asking whether he had tried to persuade her to stay.

“She called,” she said when he finished. “I have the number.”

“Duy knows the address.”

“Nhi knows he knows. She's deciding what to do.”

Minh nodded.

“You can't carry everyone,” Mai said.

“I can carry more than most.”

She looked at him for a moment, then leaned her shoulder against his.

“I know. That's why I want you to think before they climb on.”

Outside, somebody pushed a folded invitation through the front door. It landed on the floor with a soft, expensive sound.

Khang was holding a dinner for transport contractors. Minh and his wife were invited.

Mai picked it up and examined the printing.

“They used someone else,” she said.

“Are you offended?”

“By the spacing.”

Then she read the names beneath the invitation and stopped smiling.

## 13. Guests of the Company

Mai decided to attend before Minh finished explaining why he disliked the invitation.

“Then you heard none of that,” he said.

“I heard all of it. He wants people to see us at his table. He wants a photograph. He wants you to feel rude about leaving.”

“And?”

“And I want to see who thinks that makes us his.”

He looked at her across the bedroom. She was fastening an earring, her face half turned toward the small mirror. On the bed lay a dark dress she had worn to a cousin's wedding. Minh wore the trousers she said made him look less like he had been called out to repair the building.

“You don't have to do this,” he said.

“Neither do you. Yet you're already checking whether your shirt catches at the shoulder.”

He stopped moving his arm.

She smiled at his reflection. “Wear the other one. If you're going to frighten anyone, I'd like you to remain properly dressed.”

The dinner occupied the upper floor of a restaurant with a painted river scene across one wall. Guests stood between round tables, talking over music from a cassette player near the bar. Most were transport contractors, warehouse operators, suppliers, and people who had learned to appear at these dinners without asking which part of their business required it. Their wives and husbands stood beside them, carrying separate conversations through the gaps.

Minh saw three men with guns before he saw Khang.

The weapons remained concealed, but the men had chosen jackets too heavy for the room and kept one side of each body clear of passing trays. Minh noted them and continued beside Mai.

Khang's wife greeted them near the stairs.

Hương owned a clothing shop and had a more direct manner than her husband. She looked at Mai's dress, asked who made it, and listened to the answer with the professional attention of a woman already considering whether the maker could handle larger orders.

“Nhi does alterations for me,” Mai said. “I can give you her number.”

“Please do.”

Hương turned to Minh. “My husband says you don't visit.”

“I visited twice this week.”

“Then he means you don't visit pleasantly.”

Mai laughed. Minh inclined his head in acknowledgment of the more accurate charge.

Khang came through the crowd carrying a drink.

“You came.”

“You sent an invitation,” Mai said. “We understood the mechanism.”

He smiled and guided them toward the main table. Place cards had been arranged so that Minh sat beside Khang while Mai sat across the room with several women she did not know.

Mai examined the cards.

“I'll sit with my husband.”

“The tables are balanced,” Khang said.

“Then we'll be an interesting weight problem.”

She picked up her card and took the seat on Minh's other side. Hương watched, then called for another chair to be moved. The rearrangement took less time than the discussion of whether it could occur.

Hải arrived late.

He wore a dark shirt open at the throat and the same heavy ring Minh remembered from Khang's office. Two men came behind him, neither of the ones Minh had injured. Their replacement suggested either a large staff or a useful capacity to avoid learning from another man's experience.

Hải saw Minh at the main table and stopped.

Khang rose to greet him. He put a hand on the man's shoulder, spoke close to his ear, and brought him to a seat directly opposite Minh.

“Now we can all eat,” he said.

The first dishes arrived. Conversations resumed with the exaggerated relief of people who had noticed an awkwardness and were grateful to be permitted to pretend they had not.

Minh ate. He had spent enough years in rooms like this to know that refusing food made a man appear nervous or offended, and he intended to give the table neither impression. Mai asked a supplier beside her about paper storage. Within minutes they were discussing humidity, delivery delays, and a price neither expected the other to accept at once.

Across the table, Hải watched Minh's hands.

“They say you broke a forklift.”

“It needed stopping.”

“Machines are expensive.”

“You should tell the people you hire to drive them.”

One of the contractors coughed into his glass. Khang continued serving fish as if the conversation concerned routine maintenance.

“There has been confusion,” he said.

“Apparently,” Minh answered.

Hải leaned forward. “You think I sent those men?”

“They used your name. You should be upset with them if it was borrowed.”

The man with the ring held his gaze. Minh held it back until the arrival of another dish gave Hải somewhere else to look.

Khang lifted his glass.

He spoke about business improving, about cooperation between companies, about the need for reliable people in a city that never stopped moving. His voice carried easily. He knew when to pause for agreement and when to proceed before someone could consider the statement too closely.

Then he rested his free hand on Minh's shoulder.

“Some of our best people have begun businesses of their own. We take pride in that.”

Minh set down his glass.

Khang felt the movement and removed his hand before Minh had to remove it for him.

“Independence is good,” Minh said. “I recommend it.”

Several men laughed. A few looked at Khang first.

“Within friendship,” Khang added.

“Friendship can manage it.”

Mai picked up her glass. “To businesses that pay their invoices.”

That produced louder laughter, because everyone could recognize the virtue without admitting which side of it they occupied. Khang drank. The toast ended with less ownership in it than he had intended.

Later, a contractor asked Minh whether the stories about his strength were true. The man had drunk enough to confuse fascination with a right to entertainment.

“Which stories?” Minh asked.

“The truck. The boat. Someone said an engine.”

“I repair vehicles. People find that difficult to believe.”

“Come on. Lift the table.”

Minh looked at the food, the glasses, the dozen people seated around it.

“Eat faster.”

The contractor laughed and tried again. He placed a thick roll of banknotes beside his plate.

“For one hand.”

Mai's expression cooled. Minh remained looking at the man.

“Put your money away.”

“I'm only asking.”

“And I'm giving you an inexpensive answer.”

The man saw that the people nearest him had stopped laughing. He gathered the money and returned it to his pocket.

Khang watched with an expression Minh could not quite read. Part admiration, part calculation, part irritation that the performance had occurred without his permission and ended without his profit.

A young waiter came to remove an empty dish. His sleeve caught Hải's glass. Beer spilled across the table and onto the man's lap.

Hải caught the waiter's wrist.

The room changed before the waiter understood how.

“Look what you did,” Hải said.

“I'm sorry.”

The grip tightened. The waiter bent with it, trying to keep the tray level in his other hand.

Minh stood.

Hải looked up and smiled without releasing the wrist. “Sit down. We're talking.”

Minh reached across the table. His hand closed around Hải's forearm just above the heavy ring.

For one second nothing visible happened.

Then Hải let go.

His face went gray. Minh kept holding him until the waiter had stepped back from the table.

“He apologized,” Minh said.

Hải tried to pull his arm away. The attempt moved the tablecloth but not Minh's hand. On either side of the room, the men in jackets shifted their weight.

Minh looked at the nearest one.

“Don't.”

The man stopped.

Khang rose. “Enough. No one touches the staff.”

It was a useful sentence, delivered just late enough that the work of enforcing it had already been done.

Minh released Hải.

The man drew his arm beneath the table. His other hand covered it. He had not been injured in a way that required treatment, but everyone had seen the exact moment his choices became smaller.

Mai gave the waiter a clean napkin. “Are you all right?”

He nodded, eyes lowered.

“Tell the manager you need a moment,” she said.

The waiter left. Conversations restarted in small, separate bursts. Minh sat and finished the food on his plate.

Across the table, Hải did not touch his glass again.

Duy found Minh near the balcony after the meal.

He wore a shirt Nhi had altered for him. Minh recognized the neat work at the cuffs. Without his wife beside him, Duy looked more dressed and less assembled than usual.

“You're enjoying yourself,” he said.

“The fish was good.”

“Hải wants you dead.”

“He should order more fish. It might improve his evening.”

Duy leaned against the rail. Below them, motorbikes moved through the restaurant's lights and vanished into the darker road beyond.

“Nhi called Mai?”

“Yes.”

“Is she all right?”

“You can ask her.”

“She isn't answering me.”

“That is an answer.”

Duy looked down at his cuffs. “I know.”

For a while they stood without speaking. Music from inside covered the traffic, then a waiter opened the balcony door and both sounds entered together.

“Vũ asked about the names,” Duy said.

Minh turned toward him.

“I told him you gave them to me.”

“I know.”

“You should have heard it from me.”

Duy shook his head. “He would have found out. That's what he does. He counts who is missing from a room, then asks why.”

“What did you tell him?”

“That I wanted the accounts settled before the drivers went somewhere else.”

“Did he believe you?”

“He said it was sensible. That's worse.”

Minh watched the doorway. Vũ stood inside speaking to the supplier from Bình's yard. Neither looked toward the balcony. Both had positioned themselves to see it in the glass.

“You need to decide what you're going to say,” Minh told Duy.

“You keep saying that as if the sentence ends when I stop speaking.”

“It doesn't. I'll be there for the rest.”

Duy looked at him, and Minh saw the desire to believe arrive before the reasons not to.

“You can't stand beside everyone.”

“You're standing beside me now. Start there.”

The balcony door opened. Mai came out with her bag over one shoulder.

“We're leaving,” she said.

Duy straightened. “Good night.”

She looked at his face and softened slightly.

“Nhi is safe. Give her time to decide what she wants to tell you.”

He nodded, unable to answer immediately.

At the stairs, Khang intercepted them with the courtesy of a host whose evening had escaped several of its intended shapes.

“I hope there are no hard feelings.”

Mai smiled. “The printing was poor, but the food was excellent.”

Khang laughed despite himself. Minh shook the hand he offered. He kept his grip ordinary.

“Pay the restaurant properly,” he said quietly.

“You think I wouldn't?”

“I think the waiter earned it.”

They went downstairs into the warm night. At the motorbike, Mai paused while Minh handed her the helmet.

“You wanted to break his arm.”

“Yes.”

“Why didn't you?”

“The waiter had enough people looking at him.”

She fastened the strap and touched his sleeve.

“You looked good in this shirt.”

He glanced toward the restaurant. “It survived.”

“For once, both things can be true.”

She climbed on behind him. As they pulled away, Minh saw Vũ come down the stairs and stop beneath the light. He did not wave. He watched until the motorbike turned the corner.

## 14. Witness

The investigator had a bowl of noodles going cold beside his notebook.

He moved it to the windowsill when Liên entered, apologized for the lack of chairs, and found another one in the corridor before asking why she had come back.

His name was Định. Minh recognized him from the newspaper photograph Mai had kept after the fire. He looked younger in person and more tired. A dark mark on one cuff suggested he had leaned his arm against wet ink without noticing.

Liên placed the acknowledgment she had refused on the desk.

“They wanted me to say the fire was an equipment failure.”

Định read the sentence. “You didn't sign.”

“I don't know what happened.”

“Neither does this sentence.”

He set the sheet aside and asked her to begin earlier.

She described the missing wages, the tools, the red box at the market. Minh listened while she gave each part in her own order. He corrected nothing unless she asked him about a date. Định wrote steadily. When Liên reached the gray flatbed, he stopped her and asked for the description again.

“One green side panel,” she said. “The rear didn't match.”

He looked at Minh.

“You recognize the vehicle?”

“Đông Lợi uses it. Trọng often drives.”

“You saw him remove the equipment?”

“No. The seller described him. Trọng admitted moving it when I asked.”

“Admitted is your word.”

Minh considered that. “He said he moved it.”

Định wrote the correction.

“You worked for the company?”

“Until May.”

“In what capacity?”

“Collections. Recoveries. Mechanical work when they needed it.”

The investigator looked up at him for a moment longer than before.

“And now?”

“I run a repair shop.”

“So I've heard.”

Minh waited. Định returned to the notebook without explaining what else he had heard.

“Removing equipment before a fire doesn't tell me who caused it,” he said. “Selling tools that belonged to someone else may tell me something different. I'll speak to the seller. I'll need the address.”

Liên gave it.

“They told everyone the tools burned,” she said.

“That matters. It doesn't yet tell us why they lied.”

“How many things have to matter before something happens?”

The question came out harder than she intended. Minh saw her prepare to apologize, then decide not to.

Định put his pen down.

“More than I would like. Fewer than the people who wrote this hope.”

He tapped the false acknowledgment.

Liên looked at him. “Are you going to keep it?”

“I'd like a copy. You should keep yours.”

For the first time since entering, her shoulders lowered slightly.

The conversation lasted almost an hour. Định asked about Tấn's work, the phone call before six, the names of the men who delivered money after the funeral. He did not turn each answer into a conclusion. When Minh explained the removed motor, he asked him to draw what he had seen rather than interpret it as evidence of something he had not seen.

Minh drew the mounting bed and the four points where the motor had been attached. His sketch was quick and exact. Định examined it, then looked at Minh's hands.

“You do that often?”

“Repair things?”

“See what's missing.”

“Not often enough.”

The investigator accepted the answer without improving it into a speech.

Before they left, he asked whether anyone had threatened Liên directly.

She hesitated.

“They asked me to stop coming.”

“How?”

“Politely. Several times.”

“What words?”

She remembered them one at a time. People were trying to move on. Her husband would not want her distressed. Further visits would make it difficult for the company to continue helping. The phrases sounded weaker when separated from the men who had spoken them. Định wrote the men down too.

At the door, he gave her a number.

“If someone comes again, tell me what happens. Don't improve the story because you think the plain version isn't enough.”

Liên looked at the paper. “The plain version is plenty.”

Outside, noon heat had gathered against the buildings. Minh and Liên walked toward the larger road, where he had left his motorbike. She carried her documents in a canvas bag. He kept his left hand free.

“Do you believe him?” she asked.

“I believe he was listening.”

“That's another careful answer.”

“It's the one I have.”

She nodded and adjusted the bag on her shoulder.

At the corner, a man leaned beside a bicycle loaded with empty crates. He looked at Liên's bag, then at Minh, then away. Across the road, another man had stopped beside a street vendor without buying anything.

Minh felt the pattern before he could name it. Two people waiting in positions that did not match what they appeared to be doing. A third reflected briefly in the window of a parked car, moving behind them at the same pace.

He touched Liên's elbow.

“Keep walking toward the shop with the blue door.”

“What?”

“Don't stop.”

The man behind them accelerated.

Minh turned and caught his wrist before the knife reached Liên's back.

The blade stopped so close to her shirt that its point lifted a fold of cloth. Minh twisted the wrist. The knife fell. He struck the man's chest with his open palm and drove him into the parked car. The door collapsed around his body with a sound like a metal cabinet being dropped down stairs.

Liên turned despite the instruction.

The man across the road drew a pistol.

Minh moved between them. He could not reach the gunman before the arm came level. Traffic crossed the gap: two motorbikes, a bicycle, a small cart piled with baskets. He saw the path the bullet would take if the man fired where he was already aiming.

He pushed Liên down behind the parked car and threw the fallen knife.

It crossed the road below the riders' sight line and struck the gunman's forearm. The shot went into the awning above the vendor's stall. Fabric snapped. The vendor fell backward, dragging a pan from the table.

Minh ran across the road.

One rider braked hard. Minh cleared the front wheel in a stride too long to belong to the movement that began it. His next footfall landed beside the wounded gunman. He drove the pistol against the wall until the grip opened, then caught the man under the jaw and lifted him clear of the ground.

The man's heels struck the wall twice.

“Who sent you?”

Blood ran down the arm with the knife still in it. The man tried to speak and could not. Minh lowered him enough to breathe.

“Vũ?”

The eyes answered before the mouth did.

Minh let him drop against the wall. The man remained seated, one hand clamped around his bleeding forearm.

The third attacker had abandoned his bicycle and run into traffic. Minh could have caught him. He heard Liên call his name from behind the car and turned instead.

She was standing now, the canvas bag pressed against her chest. Her shirt had a small tear where the knife touched it. She looked at the damaged car, the man beneath it, the empty road where the bicycle had been left.

“Did they come for the papers?” she asked.

Minh crossed back to her.

“No.”

She understood. Her face changed, but she did not sit down.

People had begun gathering. Someone shouted for the police. A shopkeeper brought cloth for the injured men. The vendor whose awning had been struck was already furious about the pan, and his fury gave the scene something ordinary to hold onto.

Minh stayed beside Liên until uniformed officers arrived. He gave his name, pointed out the weapons, and asked someone to contact Định.

The investigator came out himself.

He looked at the crushed car door and the wounded men, then at Minh.

“You see what's missing,” he said quietly, “and you rearrange what's left.”

Minh said nothing.

Định spoke to the first officer, then took Liên back inside. Minh followed after giving his account of the attack. The knife throw, the struck arm, the gun against the wall: he described each movement plainly. There were enough people who had seen parts of it that exaggeration would only make the truth harder to use.

In the office, the noodles had gone completely cold.

Liên sat with both hands around a glass of water.

“They knew we came here,” she said.

Định nodded. “They may have followed you. They may have been watching your room. We'll need to find out.”

He did not offer the convenient certainty that the danger began somewhere else. Minh respected him for that and disliked how little it changed the afternoon.

“I can't go home,” Liên said.

“Not alone today,” Minh answered.

She looked at him sharply. “I didn't ask to move into your life.”

“I know.”

“I asked for wages.”

“I know.”

Định pulled his chair closer.

“Let's discuss where you'll be tonight before we discuss everything after it.”

They made calls. Liên had a cousin on the other side of the city who could take her for a few days. Mai arranged for someone to collect the things she needed from her room. Nhi, hearing about it through Mai, offered spare clothes without asking Liên's size first. Lan said she would bring food. Each offer was small enough to be possible and specific enough not to become another promise waiting for a stronger person.

Minh remained until Liên's cousin arrived. The cousin looked at him with the wary anger of a man told that danger had acquired a family connection.

“She'll call you?” he asked.

“Or Mai. Both numbers are there.”

Liên stood. At the door, she turned back to Định.

“Now is it enough for something to happen?”

He met her eyes.

“Something is happening.”

When they had gone, Minh remained beside the empty chair.

“The man with the gun recognized Vũ's name,” he said.

“Recognition isn't a statement.”

“You'll ask him.”

“Yes.”

“He'll be told to say someone else paid him.”

“Possibly.”

Minh looked toward the window. Beyond it, the street had resumed moving around the damaged car.

Định closed his notebook.

“If you know someone who can tell me what happened inside that company, bring me the person while he's still willing to speak.”

Minh took out Duy's folded note. He did not hand it over. He looked at the seven names, the address that had led to the courtyard, the familiar pressure of the handwriting.

“I'm trying,” he said.

The investigator looked at the paper, then at his face.

“Try quickly.”

## 15. The Men Who Pay

Hải agreed to meet Minh only after being told he would not have to shake hands.

“He thinks that's funny?” Hải asked the messenger.

“He said you'd understand.”

The meeting took place in a billiard room above one of Hải's offices. At that hour it was almost empty. Two men played at the far table, slowly enough that their interest appeared to lie in the conversation occurring elsewhere. A ceiling fan turned above the green felt. Someone had chalked a score on a board and abandoned the game before either number reached respectability.

Hải stood beside the nearest table with a cue in his good hand.

“You come to apologize?”

“No.”

“Then you've saved us time.”

Minh remained near the door until Hải pointed at a chair. He sat, turning it slightly so he could see the staircase without making the adjustment look accidental.

Hải noticed. “My men aren't going to jump on you.”

“They keep discovering that doesn't work.”

The man struck a ball too hard. It missed the pocket and returned along the cushion.

“Khang wants you frightened,” Minh said.

Hải looked at him.

“He sent Phú to Duy. He expected me to empty your yard. Then he could tell you he never asked.”

“You think I don't know how he operates?”

“I think you sent men after me instead of asking why he wanted you to.”

Hải set the cue down. “You embarrassed me in front of people.”

“You put a knife in a yard full of drivers. Then a forklift beside a woman who was weighing cargo. I don't owe you a better audience.”

The two men at the far table stopped playing. Hải looked toward them. They resumed with exaggerated care.

“What do you want?” he asked.

“An end to your part of it.”

“You break my men's bones, then ask for peace.”

“I'm asking whether you intend to buy more broken bones. It isn't the same offer.”

Hải sat opposite him. The bruising around his wrist showed when he moved the cuff. Minh looked at it once, enough to acknowledge what had happened, and returned his attention to the man's face.

“You could work with me,” Hải said.

“You watched Khang fail at that three times.”

“He wanted an employee.”

“What do you want?”

“A man people will listen to. You choose the work. I provide the money.”

Minh laughed quietly.

Hải's face reddened. “What's funny?”

“How many different men have offered to let me choose what they need.”

“You're being offered a share.”

“Of what?”

“Yards. Storage. Transport. We can discuss numbers.”

Minh looked past him at the billiard table. Every ball occupied a position produced by earlier decisions, most of them badly judged. Hải had enough force in his arm to move all of them and no evident interest in where the second collision would end.

“The men who attacked Liên,” Minh said. “Were they yours?”

Hải's expression changed.

“No.”

“Look at me.”

“I am looking at you. No.”

Minh believed the anger. He did not yet know what it protected.

“One recognized Vũ's name.”

“Most people in this business recognize Vũ's name. Half of them have been paid by him. The other half wish they hadn't.”

“Would Khang send men outside a police office?”

Hải leaned back. “Khang would send a man to collect a debt from the moon if he thought the man would take the blame for landing badly.”

“That's an answer about distance.”

“It's an answer about Khang.”

For a while they watched the game at the far table. One of the players missed an easy shot and swore softly. The other looked toward Hải before laughing.

“You know something about the warehouse,” Minh said.

“Everyone knows it burned.”

“You know why it was being emptied first.”

Hải studied him. Minh could see the calculation entering the room: how much of another man's danger could be sold without accepting delivery of it yourself.

“The owner wanted out of a contract,” Hải said at last. “Khang wanted the site. Equipment had liens against it. Cargo belonged to several different people. A fire made every argument slower and every person poorer.”

“Except the people who had already moved what mattered.”

“That's your sentence.”

“Is it wrong?”

Hải did not answer.

“Did you speak to him about it before the fire?”

“I spoke about warehouses. That's what I own.”

“Did you know people would be there?”

The man slapped the table between them. “I didn't burn the fucking place.”

Minh stayed still.

“Then stop answering as if I asked whether you held the match.”

Hải breathed through his nose. He had come to the meeting expecting a contest of pride. Minh could see his irritation at finding questions inside it that required more complicated defenses.

“I kept two trucks available that evening,” he said. “Khang asked. He paid whether he used them or not.”

“Did he use them?”

“One went to a yard. One didn't move.”

“Which yard?”

“Ask Trọng.”

Minh leaned forward. “You keep a business. You know where your trucks go.”

Hải looked toward the staircase. His men were still listening, though neither pretended the game mattered anymore.

“The north storage lot. They brought in boxed equipment. That's all I'm saying.”

“Will you say it to an investigator?”

He laughed openly this time. “There he is. The new mechanic, collecting statements between oil changes.”

“You have a better offer?”

“Yes. Stop asking. Keep your wife close. Fix buses. Khang has a great deal of money and a great many people who need it more than they need to be right.”

“So do you.”

“That's why I'm giving useful advice.”

Minh stood. Hải rose with him, sudden caution replacing the laughter.

“We're finished?”

“Your men stay away from my shop and from the drivers who left your yard. You want money from someone, explain what it's for in a room where he can leave.”

“And if I don't?”

Minh picked up the cue. He balanced it between two fingers, examined the slight bend near its tip, and placed it gently across the table.

“You already know the part where I arrive.”

Hải looked at the cue as if expecting it to break by delayed instruction.

“You enjoy this,” he said.

“Sometimes.”

“At least admit what kind of man you are.”

Minh met his eyes.

“I'm the man telling you the price before you decide.”

He left Hải standing beside the undamaged table.

At the shop, a young man waited with a canvas bag on his knees. He stood when Minh entered and nearly knocked over the stool.

“I came about the work.”

“What work?”

The man held out an advertisement. Mai had printed it that morning.

Minh read it. The position required punctuality, willingness to learn, basic mechanical experience, and the ability to ask a question before dismantling something expensive. At the bottom she had written that strength was not a qualification.

He looked toward the stairs. Mai appeared at the landing before he called.

“You said apprentice.”

“I said we should discuss one.”

“We're discussing him.”

The young man looked between them, uncertain whether he had entered an interview or a marriage already in progress.

“Name?” Minh asked.

“Phước.”

“Age?”

“Twenty.”

“What have you repaired?”

Phước listed motorbikes, a water pump, a generator that belonged to his uncle, and a fan he seemed less proud of. Minh asked what happened to the fan.

“It worked.”

“Why do you sound unhappy about it?”

“It turned the wrong way.”

Mai went back upstairs before either man could see her smile.

Minh put an old carburetor on the bench. It was clean, complete, and deliberately left with one small fault he had not yet corrected.

“Tell me what you'd check.”

Phước reached toward it, stopped, and asked whether he could pick it up.

“Good beginning,” Minh said.

The young man worked slowly. He named two possible causes that did not apply, then found the sticking movement and showed Minh without announcing that he had solved every engine ever made. Minh asked why it stuck. Phước admitted he did not know.

“Better than inventing something,” Minh said.

They discussed pay. Phước named less than Minh intended to offer. Minh gave him the larger amount and explained the hours. The young man looked suspiciously pleased, as if waiting for the hidden part of the arrangement to appear.

“You'll sweep,” Minh said. “You'll clean parts. You'll watch more than you touch at first. When you don't understand, say so. If you damage something, tell me before the customer discovers it.”

Phước nodded at each point.

“And don't call me master.”

“What should I call you?”

“Minh.”

The young man looked uncomfortable.

“Then call me Mr. Minh until you get bored with it.”

They shook hands. Minh kept his grip light. Phước went out carrying the advertisement as though it had become proof of something more than a job.

Mai came downstairs.

“You hired him.”

“For a trial.”

“You gave him more than he asked.”

“He asked badly.”

She leaned against the counter. “That has never bothered you when a customer does it.”

Minh took the agreement from Bình out of his pocket and set it beside the apprentice advertisement. There was a shape forming now: work coming in, someone learning, another pair of hands that could remain at the shop when he had to collect parts. A business becoming more than the hours he could stay awake.

He felt pleased with it. He intended to remain pleased even though men elsewhere were discussing how much of his life could be used against him.

The telephone rang.

Mai answered, listened, and held out the receiver.

“Duy.”

Minh took it.

His former partner sounded sober and far away.

“Can we meet?”

“Where?”

“The place we used to eat after work. Tomorrow. Before it gets crowded.”

Minh looked at the advertisement on the counter.

“No more addresses doing the talking,” he said.

“I know.”

“You tell me.”

Duy breathed into the line. “I'll tell you what I can.”

“Tell me what happened.”

The silence lasted until Minh thought the call had ended.

Then Duy said, “All right.”

## 16. What the Order Said

Duy had ordered enough food for four men and touched none of it.

Minh sat opposite him in the restaurant where they used to eat after collecting vehicles. The owner recognized them, brought tea without asking, and asked whether their wives had finally thrown them out.

“Only one of us,” Duy said.

The owner looked at his face and stopped smiling.

“Eat,” she told him. “Being miserable doesn't make the rice less expensive.”

She left them beneath a fan with one loose blade. Minh listened to it turn while Duy arranged his chopsticks, moved them, then set them down again.

“Speak,” Minh said.

“You haven't eaten.”

“I can listen hungry.”

Duy looked toward the doorway. There were only three other customers, all occupied with lunch. Outside, someone was repairing a bicycle puncture beneath a striped umbrella. Nothing in the room justified the way Duy held his shoulders.

“Khang wanted the warehouse emptied,” he said. “The owner wouldn't agree to the price. There were debts against the machinery. Claims against the stock. He said the longer it stood there, the more people would discover they owned pieces of it.”

Minh waited.

“Trọng said a fire would make the claims different.”

“You heard him?”

“I was in the office.”

“Who else?”

“Khang. Trọng. Me. Vũ came in later.”

The fan clicked above them. Minh noticed the rhythm without intending to.

“What did Khang say?”

Duy swallowed.

“He said to make sure the valuable equipment was gone first.”

“Did he say fire?”

“Yes.”

“Say the sentence.”

Duy looked at him, anger rising briefly through the fear.

“You want me to perform it?”

“I want to know what you heard and what you decided it meant.”

The anger subsided. Duy rubbed one hand against his trouser leg.

“He said, 'If it burns, it burns after the good equipment leaves.' Then Trọng asked about the night staff. Khang said the contractor had been told to clear them.”

“Had he?”

“I don't know.”

“What did you do?”

“I supervised the loading.”

“After that?”

“I drove.”

“Where?”

“Between the office and the yard.”

Minh leaned back. The table between them held fish, rice, vegetables, two bowls of soup, and a meal's worth of reasons to delay the next question.

“You're leaving something out.”

Duy's eyes went to the food.

“I told you what he said.”

“You told me what everyone said except you.”

“I didn't give the order.”

“That wasn't the question.”

Duy picked up his cup and found it empty. Minh refilled it. The small courtesy seemed to disturb him more than the questioning.

“They were supposed to be gone,” he said.

“Who?”

“The people working there.”

Minh thought of Tấn beneath the engine, the wedding ring between the greasy fingers, Liên counting the wages on Mai's table. He kept his hands away from the table's edge.

“Did you see them that evening?”

“I saw the building.”

“Duy.”

“I'm telling you what I can.”

“That's not enough anymore.”

The owner came by with another dish. She looked at their untouched food, looked at their faces, and took the dish away without placing it down.

Duy watched her leave.

“Do you remember when we used to come here with money in our pockets and nothing else?”

“We brought plenty else. We didn't put it on the table.”

“You make everything sound ugly after you're finished enjoying it.”

The accusation struck because it had found something real.

Minh let a breath pass before answering.

“I enjoyed the work when men thought they could beat me. I enjoyed taking things from men who had already taken them from someone else. I enjoyed Khang counting money in front of people who wished they'd been sent instead.”

Duy looked up.

“You want me to say it? I liked being the man who could do it. I still like it. That doesn't explain three people burning inside a warehouse.”

Duy's mouth tightened. “No.”

“Then stop asking it to.”

They sat silently until one of the other customers paid and left. The restaurant felt larger with one less conversation inside it.

“There's an investigator,” Minh said. “His name is Định. He spoke to Liên. He wants someone who heard the order.”

“He'll arrest me.”

“He'll ask what you did.”

“You keep making those sound different.”

“I can't promise they will be.”

Duy laughed bitterly. “Excellent offer.”

“You prefer the one Khang is making?”

The laugh stopped.

“What does he want now?” Minh asked.

“He wants me to go with Trọng tomorrow. An equipment transfer. Outside the usual route.”

“Don't go.”

“If I refuse, he'll know.”

“He already knows you gave me the names.”

“Knowing isn't the same as hearing me refuse.”

“For once, make him manage the difference.”

Duy looked toward the door again.

“There are papers. Transport sheets. Payments entered as repairs. They show who was there and who got paid.”

“Bring what you have. We can go now.”

“I don't have them here.”

“Then go without them.”

“You think they'll take my word against his? He'll say I was stealing. He'll say I made it up because he found out. He'll show them something with my signature on it.”

“The papers won't speak if you don't.”

“They'll keep him from making every part of it mine.”

Minh watched his friend. Duy had reached the point where every possible future looked like a punishment assigned by someone else. He was searching for the version that allowed him to choose the order in which it arrived.

“What are you planning?”

“I get the sheets. I put Nhi somewhere they can't reach easily. I come to you.”

“Nhi has already left.”

“They know her sister.”

“Then tell Mai what she needs to know. Nhi can decide where to go.”

“She's my wife.”

“She's a person who answered you. Listen to her.”

Duy put both hands flat on the table. For a moment Minh thought he might overturn it, not to hurt him but because the violence would give the room a simpler problem.

Instead, he picked up his chopsticks and took a mouthful of rice. He swallowed with difficulty.

“Tomorrow night,” he said.

“Now.”

“I can't.”

Minh leaned forward. “I can carry you through the door.”

Duy met his eyes. “Can you make me tell them the right thing after you put me down?”

The question stopped him.

He could force Duy into a car. He could keep Trọng away from him. He could break the hand holding a weapon. He could not make a man stop preparing a lie simply by removing the reasons that made sense to someone watching.

“Nine,” Minh said finally. “At the shop. If you're late, call.”

Duy nodded.

“And if someone asks you to go somewhere before then, you call me first.”

“Yes.”

“Don't say yes because you're tired of this table.”

Duy looked at the food. “I'm tired of every table.”

They ate after that, slowly at first. The owner came back and pretended not to notice the change. Duy asked her whether the fish had become smaller. She said his appetite had become arrogant. Minh paid before the old argument over whose turn it was could begin.

Outside, the afternoon light was hard enough to flatten the buildings across the road.

Duy stood beside his car with one hand on the door.

“If I tell him no,” he said, “there won't be a way back.”

“You've been trying to leave for weeks.”

“Trying is different.”

“Yes.”

Duy looked at him and gave a small, exhausted smile.

“There. A question you don't have a clever answer for.”

“I have several. None will do the work.”

They stood in the heat. Then Duy held out his hand.

Minh took it.

He remembered a shirt wrapped around his opened wrist, a man on his knees dragging Tấn out from beneath an engine. He remembered the white marks on tools that the same man later loaded into a truck. Both memories belonged to Duy. Neither canceled the other.

“Nine,” Minh said.

“Nine.”

Duy got into the car and drove away.

Minh remained until the vehicle disappeared beyond the intersection. Then he went to the nearest telephone and called Mai.

“I need you to speak to Nhi.”

“What happened?”

He told her enough to make the request intelligible. He did not tell her Duy had promised everything would be simple after tomorrow. Duy had not promised it, and neither of them would have believed him.

Mai listened, asked which number Nhi should avoid answering, and said she would call back when they had a plan.

“Come home,” she said before ending the call.

“I have to see Định first.”

“Then come home afterward.”

Minh looked at the coins remaining in his palm.

“I will.”

He put the receiver down and headed toward the office where a man with cold noodles had asked him to bring a living witness.

## 17. No Safe Appointment

Định listened without writing until Minh finished.

Then he asked Duy's full name, wrote it carefully, and turned the notebook so Minh could check the spelling.

“Tomorrow night,” he said.

“Nine at my shop. I'll bring him here.”

“Does he understand what he'll be asked?”

“He understands enough to be afraid.”

The investigator tapped the pen against the page once.

“I can't offer him a version in which he gets to decide which parts count.”

“I haven't offered that.”

“You may not have intended to.”

Minh looked toward the window. The afternoon light had begun to soften, though the room remained hot. Someone in the corridor was trying to make a photocopier accept a sheet it plainly disliked.

“He heard Khang approve the fire,” he said.

“That's what he told you.”

“Yes.”

“And you believe him?”

Minh considered the restaurant, the careful edges of Duy's answers, the sentence about workers who should have gone home.

“I believe he's told me less than he knows.”

Định nodded. “Then bring the rest of the man.”

They discussed what would happen if Duy changed his mind, if he arrived late, if Khang's people appeared at the shop before the appointment. Minh wanted certainty and knew the man across from him did not own enough of it to distribute. Định gave him numbers, names of colleagues who would be present if he was called away, and instructions simple enough to remember while frightened. Minh listened to all of them.

“You expect trouble,” the investigator said.

“I've had enough to recognize the schedule.”

“Then don't turn your shop into a private station.”

“I intend to leave it.”

“With Duy.”

“Yes.”

Định closed the notebook. “If he is hurt, take him where he can be treated. We can ask questions beside a hospital bed. We can't ask them afterward if there isn't anyone in it.”

Minh nodded.

At the door, the investigator stopped him with one last question.

“Why you?”

“He knows me.”

“He knows many people.”

Minh looked down at the scar across his wrist.

“He was there once when I needed another pair of hands.”

“Is that enough?”

“It was enough to answer the telephone.”

Định accepted that. Minh went out into the evening with the appointment written on a small sheet that felt too light for the number of lives being balanced on it.

Mai had closed the shop by the time he returned. She was upstairs speaking on the telephone, her voice low and firm. Minh could hear Nhi's name, an address repeated back, a question about whether someone had already left work.

He waited until she hung up.

“She's moved again,” Mai said. “A friend from the hotel has a room. Her sister will tell Duy she's away if he calls.”

“Does Nhi want that?”

Mai looked at him. “Nhi arranged it. I found someone to carry the sewing machine.”

“I could have carried it.”

“Yes. And then every man watching you would know where it went.”

He set Định's paper on the table.

“You've been busy.”

“So have you. Tell me whether we're eating before or after the bad news.”

“The news is the same. Duy says he'll come tomorrow.”

“What did he finally say?”

Minh told her the words about the fire. He gave them as Duy had given them, separating what his friend claimed to hear from what he had refused to explain. Mai listened with one hand resting on the back of a chair.

When he finished, she sat down.

“He wants you to get him out.”

“Yes.”

“Has he decided to leave?”

“I think so.”

“That's not the same answer.”

“I know.”

They ate while the radio played quietly on the windowsill. For several minutes neither spoke. Then Mai asked about Phước, and Minh described the young man's careful examination of the carburetor and the fan that had turned backward.

She laughed.

“You'll frighten him if you repair everything before he sees it break.”

“I can leave him a few things.”

“Don't invent faults. Teach him what customers actually bring.”

“You have a great many opinions about a job you didn't apply for.”

“I'm the one who wrote the advertisement. I consider that seniority.”

Minh smiled and took more rice. The small argument relieved something in the room. He had spent the afternoon discussing whether a man might live long enough to tell a story. Mai wanted to know whether the apprentice would receive a proper lunch break. Both mattered. He was grateful she would not let the larger problem erase the smaller one simply by being louder.

After dinner, she brought out the month's accounts. They reviewed the rent, the binding-room deposit, the first expected payment from Bình. Minh added Phước's wages. There would be less spare cash than he had planned and more possibility than there had been a month earlier.

“We can manage it,” Mai said.

“I know.”

“Then stop staring at the total as if you can intimidate it.”

He put the book down.

“If I have to leave the shop tomorrow, don't stay there waiting.”

“I have an order to finish. I'll be at the printing shop.”

“Mai.”

She looked at him steadily. “People still expect the work they've paid for.”

“They can wait.”

“Some can. Some have a dinner arranged, invitations due, deposits they can't get back. I can close if there's danger. I'm not closing because Khang likes the idea of us sitting in a dark room thinking about him.”

Minh leaned back. He wanted to object. He also knew exactly which part of him admired the answer.

“Tell Lan what's happening.”

“I have.”

“And Hồng.”

“She's working tomorrow, then coming by after her shift.”

“If you see anyone—”

“I will call. I will leave. I will use the rear passage if the front is watched. You helped make the plan. Let me be someone who can remember it.”

He looked at her hands on the account book, ink-stained and steady.

“I don't like how much of this you've had to learn.”

“Neither do I.”

She closed the book and came around the table.

“But I learned it. That's the part you need to keep up with.”

He took her in his arms. She rested her cheek against his shoulder. For a while they remained standing beside the table, the room quiet around the place their bodies occupied together.

“You are very difficult to hold when you decide to be furniture,” she said.

He loosened his arms. “Better?”

“I still have ribs. I'd like to keep the arrangement.”

He kissed her forehead, then her mouth. There was nothing tentative in either movement. She caught the front of his shirt and held him there until the radio announcer began another item neither of them heard.

Later, while Mai slept, Minh checked the room below.

The shutter was secure. The rear gate opened without sticking. His tools were where he had left them. He put the telephone numbers beside the receiver and wrote them again on a card small enough to fit his pocket.

Then he went upstairs to speak to the landlord.

She answered in a dressing gown, suspicious of any knock that arrived after she had finished washing the breakfast bowls.

“Something leaking?”

“No.”

“Then it can usually wait.”

He told her enough. An old work dispute. Men who had threatened him. A friend expected tomorrow. If strangers came, she should not argue at the shutter or let anyone upstairs.

She listened without interrupting.

“You should have told me sooner.”

“Yes.”

“I don't rent rooms for fighting.”

“I know.”

“Does your wife know?”

“Yes.”

The woman looked past him toward the stairs.

“My sister's niece lives two streets over. If anything looks wrong, we'll go there.”

“Thank you.”

“Don't thank me. Make sure we can come back.”

Minh went downstairs and stood in the shop for another minute. He could hear the landlord moving a chair above him, then a cupboard opening, then the soft exchange of voices as she woke her sister to explain why tomorrow might require different arrangements.

He had wanted to warn them. The warning had become a piece of their night.

He turned off the lamp.

In bed, Mai moved closer without fully waking. He lay on his back and listened to the city beyond the window. Engines passed. Somewhere a dog barked at a gate that remained closed. A couple argued softly enough that only the rhythm of disagreement reached the room.

Minh could account for the building's weight, the engine sounds, the small shifts of Mai's breathing. He could calculate how far the nearest streetlight threw its reflection across the ceiling.

He could not make nine o'clock arrive more honestly by staying awake.

At last he slept.

## 18. Before the Knock

Phước arrived early enough to find Minh lifting the shutter.

The young man wore clean work clothes and carried his lunch in a metal container. He had brought a notebook too. Minh approved of the notebook until he saw that the first page contained a careful drawing of a motorcycle surrounded by no actual notes.

“You'll need words as well,” he said.

“I wasn't sure what to write.”

“Start with what the customer says. Then write what you find. They won't always resemble each other.”

The first customer claimed his engine had stopped without warning. The warning, after further discussion, consisted of three days of smoke, a new knocking sound, and oil appearing beneath the vehicle whenever it was parked. Phước wrote each item down while trying to keep his face respectful.

Minh saw the effort and assigned him the customer conversation while he examined the engine.

“You can laugh afterward,” he told him quietly.

“I wasn't going to.”

“Then you'll need more practice lying too.”

By ten, the shop had acquired the unfamiliar rhythm of two people working. Minh reached for tools that Phước had moved, corrected the placement once, then began saying their names before reaching. The apprentice took longer than necessary to clean a component but missed nothing obvious. Minh had to stop himself from finishing the last stubborn patch with his own thumb.

Mai came down with tea and watched them for a moment.

“How is he?” she asked Phước, nodding toward Minh.

The apprentice looked alarmed.

“He hasn't broken me yet.”

Mai laughed. Minh looked up from the bench.

“Good. Keep that answer. You'll need it.”

She left for the printing shop carrying the revised order she had collected the previous evening. The client had changed a seating arrangement and discovered that important relatives became more important after the invitations were printed. Mai described the revisions with enough precision that Minh suspected she could have redrawn the man's family without needing his assistance.

“Late tonight?” he asked.

“Probably. I'll call before I close.”

He nodded. She touched his shoulder as she passed, then went out into the lane.

At noon, Hải telephoned.

Minh took the call with one hand while holding a bent bracket against the bench with the other. Phước watched the bracket straighten under his fingers and forgot to keep sweeping.

“Your friends at the wharf have their replacement walkway,” Hải said.

“Good.”

“And the woman has a table.”

“I paid for that.”

“Now she has a better table.”

Minh set the bracket down. “What do you want?”

“Nothing from you.”

“That sounds expensive.”

Hải exhaled into the receiver. “Trọng is taking Duy out tonight. A transfer. That's what they're calling it.”

“I heard.”

“Then hear this too. The truck was serviced yesterday. There was no cargo listed when my dispatcher asked whether we'd be providing another vehicle.”

Minh looked toward the open shutter.

“Why tell me?”

“Because Khang keeps counting me as a man who will discover what happened after he's already decided where I stood.”

“Will you tell Định?”

“You ask that again and this becomes a much shorter call.”

“Thank you for the information.”

The politeness seemed to irritate Hải more than the earlier question.

“Don't mistake it for friendship.”

“I won't invite you to move furniture.”

Hải gave an unwilling snort and hung up.

Minh called Duy's apartment. There was no answer. He called the office and asked for him. Vũ came on the line instead.

“He's working.”

“Tell him I need to speak to him.”

“You need him often.”

“Then the message won't surprise him.”

“What's it about?”

Minh looked at Phước, who had resumed sweeping with suspicious energy.

“A repair.”

Vũ was silent for a moment. “We'll see whether he has time.”

The line went dead.

Minh remained by the telephone until Phước asked whether he should stop work.

“No,” he said. “Keep cleaning that. I'll be outside.”

He walked to the larger road and looked both ways. There was no white van, no familiar motorcycle waiting in the shade. The absence reassured him less than it should have. Vũ had been told he noticed men who watched badly.

He returned to the shop and called Định's office. The investigator was out, but the colleague whose name he had given Minh confirmed that the message had been received. Someone would be available that evening. If plans changed, he should call.

Minh wrote the colleague's name beside the first number.

The afternoon brought ordinary failures. A cable frayed inside its housing. A customer wanted a repair he could not afford and accepted a used part after Minh explained the difference. A man came to complain about work done by another shop and left after being told that buying tea for Minh did not transfer the quarrel with it.

Phước learned quickly when given one thing at a time. He learned less quickly when Minh forgot that a glance at a mechanism did not provide everyone with the same invisible diagram. By four, Minh had begun explaining before his apprentice had to ask.

That pleased him more than he expected.

At five, Duy called.

His voice was low, with other men speaking in the room behind him.

“I got your message.”

“Don't go with Trọng.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I changed the arrangement.”

“To what?”

“I'll tell you when I come.”

Minh closed his eyes briefly.

“Nine.”

“Yes.”

“If you're bringing someone, tell me now.”

“I'm alone.”

He said it too quickly. Minh heard a door close at the far end, and Duy spoke again, quieter.

“Nhi?”

“Safe. Mai knows where she is.”

“Don't tell me.”

The request settled in Minh's chest.

“Duy—”

“Just don't. I'll see you tonight.”

The call ended before Minh could answer.

He stood with the receiver in his hand until Phước looked up. Then he put it down and told the apprentice to finish early.

“Did I do something wrong?”

“No.”

“I can stay.”

“You did a full day. Go eat with your family.”

Phước gathered his things reluctantly. Minh paid him for the day and gave him enough extra to buy the small tools they had discussed. The apprentice tried to refuse the extra until Minh said it was an advance on equipment, not a reward for looking worried.

At the shutter, Phước turned back.

“Tomorrow?”

“Call first.”

The young man looked toward the telephone, then at the two customers' motorbikes still waiting under sheets.

“All right.”

He went home carrying the notebook, which now contained three pages of writing beneath the drawing.

Minh ate at the counter after the lane began to quiet. The landlord brought down a bowl of soup and asked whether his friend had arrived. He said not yet. She reminded him that she and her sister were upstairs if he needed anything.

“If someone comes to the front, don't answer for me,” he said.

She looked at him carefully.

“You told us.”

“I'm telling you again.”

She took the empty tray and went upstairs without another question.

At seven thirty, Mai called from the printing shop. The press was audible behind her voice.

“Still waiting?”

“Yes.”

“We've nearly finished the first run. The client changed two names again.”

“Tell him to change relatives.”

“I suggested it. He thought I was joking.”

Minh smiled despite the tension in his shoulders.

“Lan?”

“At her office. Hồng is on her way home from the hospital. Nhi called. Liên is with her cousin.”

Each name arrived with a place attached, briefly making the people he worried about more solid than the possibilities surrounding them.

“Thank you,” he said.

Mai was quiet for a moment.

“You don't have to say it as if I'm reporting to you.”

“That's not how I meant it.”

“I know. I'm reminding you how to hear it.”

He looked around the shop. “Come home when you're done.”

“I will. Call if anything changes.”

After the call, he cleaned the workbench and put the finished jobs on their shelves. He left a cloth, a bowl of water, and the first-aid supplies within reach. Then he took out the carburetor Phước had examined that morning and worked on the fault the young man had correctly found.

At eight fifty, he checked the rear door.

At nine, he stopped looking at the clock.

The city continued around the shop. A television sounded above the next building. Someone pushed a cart down the lane. The fan swung from one wall to the other, making the same tired click at the end of each turn.

Minh placed the repaired carburetor on a clean cloth and reached for the pencil to mark it finished.

Then someone knocked on the shutter.

## 19. The Address

At eleven minutes past nine, someone knocked on the repair shop's shutter with Minh's old knock.

He had stopped using it when he left Đông Lợi. Two blows, a pause, one more. At the time he had thought that leaving the knock behind was the same as leaving the work.

He put down the carburetor. Under the shutter he could see a pair of bare feet and water spreading across the concrete. It had not rained for three hours.

“I'm alone,” Duy said.

Minh lifted the shutter with one hand. His former partner stood barefoot in a torn office shirt, holding his abdomen. The water came from his trousers. Below his hand, something darker was soaking through.

“Who followed you?”

Duy looked away.

Minh took him inside and lowered the shutter.

His shop occupied the ground floor of a narrow rented house. The owner lived upstairs with her sister and sold breakfast from a cart. They had gone to bed. A fan swung above the workbench, showing each corner of the room the same brief attention. Two customers' motorbikes waited beneath clean sheets.

Minh eased Duy onto the floor and opened his shirt. A bullet had passed through his side. Another wound, high on the shoulder, was still bleeding freely.

“How many?”

“At the office, four.”

“Following you.”

“I don't know.”

Minh folded a clean cloth against the shoulder. Duy watched his hands. At twenty-seven, Minh had thickened through the chest without becoming slow. A white seam crossed his left wrist where a winch cable had snapped two years earlier. The man beneath the cloth had been there when it happened. He had seen Minh hold the falling engine while three others got clear.

“They're coming here,” Duy said.

“You told them where you'd go.”

“Yes.”

The fan reached the wall and started back.

Duy removed a packet from inside his waistband. Office paper wrapped in a plastic bag. Minh recognized the cargo stamps before he read the first page.

Phạm Quốc Khang owned Đông Lợi Freight, whose respectable trucks carried fabric, machine parts, and produce. His other employees charged small haulers for permission to use roads Khang did not own. When drivers refused, cargo disappeared. When merchants complained, their debts were bought and collected with hammers.

Minh had once been the man holding the hammer.

He spread the pages on the bench. Truck movements. Warehouse leases. Cash payments entered twice, once as wages, again beneath names of people who had never worked there. He read all fourteen pages before Duy finished swallowing a mouthful of water.

Three columns belonged to the warehouse fire in June. There were the transfers Hải had mentioned, the equipment Trọng had moved, the payments Duy had described as repairs. The sheets would not tell anyone who struck a match. The man on Minh's floor would have to do that himself.

Minh looked at him.

“They were supposed to have gone home,” Duy said.

“Who?”

“The packers.”

Three adults had died there: Thúy and Hạnh, who wrapped ceramic goods, and Tấn, whose weight Minh still remembered dragging against his arm beneath the fallen engine. Minh had bought the man's tools back from a stall and returned them to Liên. Duy had helped put them on the truck.

“You lit it.”

Duy nodded.

“Say it.”

“I lit the warehouse.”

The words were smaller than everything Duy had used to avoid them.

Minh looked at the wound beneath his hand. Blood moved through the cloth in a slow, widening circle. He could have removed the cloth. He could have let Duy hold the pressure himself while explaining the difference between the man who gave an order and the man who carried it out.

He pressed harder.

Duy made a sound through his teeth.

“Stay with me,” Minh said.

“I thought they were gone.”

“You'll tell that part after.”

“You have to understand—”

“I have to keep you from bleeding through this. You have to keep speaking when someone asks you what you did. For once, we both have a clear job.”

Duy shut his mouth. His eyes had become wet, though Minh could not tell how much of that belonged to pain.

“Who shot you?”

“Trọng.”

“Where's Khang?”

“At the office. He watched.”

“What happened to the transfer?”

“There wasn't one.”

Minh already knew that. He let Duy say it anyway.

“They asked why I'd copied the sheets. I said I wanted the money accounted for. Khang asked whether I had become an accountant too.”

The laugh that followed was mostly breath.

“I told him I was leaving. He said I could leave after one more thing.”

“What thing?”

Duy looked at the rear door.

“He wanted to know how you'd take someone out of here if the front was blocked. Who lived upstairs. Whether Mai would be home.”

Minh's face did not move.

“What did you tell him?”

“I told him.”

“All of it.”

“He already knew the businesses. I thought he was checking what I would say. I thought if I answered, he'd believe I hadn't gone to you.”

“You drew him a way into my house.”

Duy nodded.

The fan reached the wall and began to turn back. Minh heard the landlord move upstairs, a familiar scrape of a chair leg against the floor. His friend had placed that sound inside someone else's plan.

“Then he asked where Nhi was,” Duy said. “I couldn't tell him. I didn't know. He said I was lying, and Trọng raised the gun.”

“Did you shoot?”

“At the man by the door. I don't know if I hit him. I went through the rear office, into the water by the landing.”

That explained the soaked clothes, the bare feet. Minh lifted the wounded shoulder slightly and replaced the cloth beneath it. Duy's whole body tensed.

“You came here knowing they would.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Duy looked at him with nothing left in his face that resembled negotiation.

“Because you said nine.”

Minh's fingers closed around the edge of the workbench. The hardwood compressed beneath them. He let go before it broke.

Outside, a motorbike passed the shop and returned. No change of gear. Two men looking at addresses.

“Khang won't leave me alive,” Duy said.

“Then he'll have to get through me.”

Duy shut his eyes as if the answer hurt more than the pressure on his wound.

Minh crossed to the telephone. Mai answered at her printing shop, where an anniversary banquet had become two hundred revised invitations after the client changed the seating arrangements.

“Duy is here,” he said. “He's shot. Men are coming. They know the rear passage.”

Mai was silent long enough for him to hear the press in the background.

“They know I'm working?”

“Assume they know everything Duy could tell them.”

“And you called from the shop.”

“I needed to tell you.”

“Then tell me everything.”

He did. He did not use the words old business.

When he finished, she said, “Get the women upstairs out through the back. I'll call them. Don't come here until I tell you.”

Someone stopped outside the shutter.

Minh put the receiver down without hanging up. He lifted Duy onto his back. At the rear door, the landlord stood in her nightclothes with a cash box against her chest. Her sister carried their documents in a biscuit tin.

“Your wife said to leave,” the landlord whispered.

“Go to your niece. I'll pay for what happens.”

“You can't know what will happen.”

He had no answer.

The first bullets punched bright holes through the shutter as they entered the rear passage.



Minh ran with Duy's blood sliding between his shoulder blades.

Behind them a door burst open. A voice shouted their direction. Minh turned into a passage too narrow for the pursuing motorbikes and found a locked gate at its far end.

He kicked through the gate without slowing. The hinges tore out of the wall. He caught the falling metal before it struck a sleeping dog and set it aside. Beyond it, a woman washing bowls stared up from a low stool.

“Stay inside,” he said.

The next passage opened onto a wider street. He could have crossed it in a single leap. He waited instead for a bicycle with two baskets of bread to pass, then crossed behind it. A boy working at a drinks stall looked at the blood and dropped a glass.

Minh heard the pursuers reach the first gate. They were spreading out now, confident that a wounded man could not travel far.

He climbed an outside staircase and gained the roof of a closed furniture workshop. There was a long gap to the next building, with telephone wires sagging across it. Duy felt him stop.

“Leave me here.”

“Hold your shoulder.”

“Minh.”

“Do it.”

He stepped onto the parapet.

He saw the far roof as loads and distances: tiles resting on battens, a patched section, the pale edge of a concrete beam. He selected the only place that would carry them both. Below, a pursuer came around the corner with a pistol raised.

Minh jumped from standing.

They crossed more than nine meters. Duy's grip broke midway. Minh caught his wrist in the air and brought him back against his shoulder before they struck the beam. Concrete cracked beneath Minh's feet. The roof tiles on either side remained intact.

The gunshot arrived after them.

He lowered Duy behind a water tank. The wounded man's mouth hung open. The dressing on his shoulder had slipped.

“You pulled it loose,” Minh said.

He pressed it back into place and bound it with a strip torn from his own shirt.

“I didn't know you could do that.”

“Neither did I.”

Duy started shaking. Minh put a hand against his neck. The pulse was too fast and becoming difficult to feel.

They needed a hospital. Khang knew it as well as they did. He would not have to control every entrance, only put someone at a telephone and wait for a familiar face.

Minh took the packet from Duy. The paper smelled of river water and blood. At the bottom of a transport sheet he found the number that had troubled him on first reading: the same repair charge entered against six different trucks.

He closed his eyes and reconstructed their routes from memory. Six vehicles traveling distances that did not match their fuel purchases. On the nights listed, each had spent hours stationary. The expense was being used to pay men, not mechanics.

Two of those nights Minh had been paid to stand outside a building and keep people away.

Before Lộc put the accounts on the courtyard table, Minh had told himself it was an argument over a lease. Now the same nights had amounts beside them. His own payments stood among the others, precise and unsurprised.

“These are the cold-store payments,” he said.

Duy looked at him.

“In April. Lộc's hand. The other men.”

“Yes.”

“All seven accounts were made that night?”

“Before they came.”

“Then why keep them there?”

Duy wet his lips. “Khang wanted them to think they had been given a choice before they signed.”

Minh remembered a man calling from behind the door. He remembered turning up his radio.

He put the sheets back in the plastic. Lộc had supplied the human shape of the evening. Here was its price, divided into columns that could be carried by a clerk without anyone hearing the voice behind the door.

Duy's head had begun to droop. Minh touched his cheek.

“Look at me.”

“I'm looking.”

“Tell me where you are.”

“On a fucking roof.”

“Good. Keep being offended.”

A door opened beneath them. Minh heard someone climb the stairs inside the building, slow steps, keys touching a rail. He moved between Duy and the roof entrance before it opened.

An elderly man emerged carrying a plastic basin. He saw the blood and stopped with one foot still on the stair.

“We need to go through,” Minh said.

The man looked at Duy, then at the broken concrete beneath Minh's feet.

“Are men looking for you?”

“Yes.”

“I have people downstairs.”

“Then tell them to stay away from the windows.”

The man set the basin down. “The front door has two bolts.”

“We won't use it.”

Minh lifted Duy again. The stairwell was narrow enough that he had to turn sideways at each landing, keeping the wounded shoulder from touching the wall. The old man went ahead and opened a door into a small room where a woman sat watching television. She rose when she saw them.

“The back,” the man told her.

She took a key from a hook. She asked no question until the last door was open.

“Do you need a towel?”

Minh looked at the soaked cloth around Duy's shoulder.

“Yes.”

She brought two. He thanked her and replaced the dressing while Duy leaned against the wall. The woman watched his hands, the care with which he moved the injured arm. Whatever she believed about them, she handed him the second towel unfolded.

Outside, a shout came from the street they had crossed. The old man turned the television louder. Minh understood what the small sound was meant to conceal.

“Leave by the service lane,” he whispered. “The gate is blue.”

They did. Minh closed it behind them rather than letting it strike the frame. Duy breathed against his neck, shallow and warm.

“They shouldn't have helped,” Duy said.

“They decided.”

“They don't know me.”

“Then keep alive long enough to decide what to do with that.”

Beyond the gate, engines moved through the street. A resident shouted at the riders to slow down. Somewhere a television was still playing. The city kept offering people ordinary things to do, and Minh had become a danger moving between them.

“We're going to the police,” he said.

“Khang pays people.”

“He doesn't pay everyone.”

“He'll know before they take our names.”

“Then more people will know.”

At the end of the service lane, he saw one of Khang's cars parked on the road toward the nearest hospital. A man he recognized from the office stood beside it, watching the junction. Minh moved back before the man's head turned. The hospital was close. Getting Duy through its entrance would require passing another gun while carrying him.

He found a public telephone around the corner and called Mai.

“Don't come to the shop,” she said immediately. “Two men asked the bakery whether I was working late. I've closed it. I'm at Mrs. Lan's place.”

Minh knew Lan, who kept accounts for several neighborhood businesses and argued fiercely with Mai over the price of paper. He had once mistaken their arguments for dislike.

“I've found payments,” he said. “Things I was part of.”

“Keep the originals dry. Can Duy speak?”

“For now.”

“Bring him to the binding room. The rear entrance. I have someone coming.”

He heard her move away from the receiver, ask another person for a clean cassette, then return.

“Minh. If he dies before he tells this properly, those pages will become whatever Khang says they are.”

“I know.”

“Do you? I need you thinking about more than carrying him.”

He looked at Duy, who had begun to sag against the telephone booth.

“I do now.”

He asked Mai to telephone Định's office while he moved. She repeated the location of the call box, then the direction of the binding room. Hồng was there now. They had clean dressings, a place to lay Duy down, and a telephone that did not require Minh to stand in the street while using it.

“The nearest hospital entrance is watched,” he said. “I saw one of Khang's cars on the road toward it.”

“Then let the police arrange the hospital,” Mai answered. “Get here if it's the place you can reach.”

He looked at Duy. The man was no longer capable of crossing the distance under his own power. Every minute Minh spent inventing a perfect route would be spent with him bleeding against the wall of a telephone booth.

“I'm coming.”

“Minh.”

“Yes?”

“I heard what you said about the passage. We've told the neighbors. Keep him alive until we have another pair of hands on him.”

He hung up and put Duy across his shoulders once more.

“Nhi?” Duy whispered.

“Safe.”

“You didn't tell me where.”

“You asked me not to.”

Duy's grip tightened briefly against his shirt.

Minh turned into the service lane, carrying the man who had shown his enemies where to find everyone else.

## 20. The Night Fleet

The shortest route to the binding room passed the public square. Minh stopped before reaching it.

A white van stood beside the closed pharmacy. Two men occupied its front seats. A third leaned against the side door, smoking. He wore a pale shirt with the sleeves rolled neatly above his elbows. Minh recognized the way he held his cigarette: far from his face, looking down the length of it before each drag.

Vũ had worked for Khang longer than Minh had. He had never been as strong as the men he supervised. He remembered their wives' names, their payday, the time of the last bus to their home villages. After a man agreed to work for him, those details became part of the agreement.

Duy lifted his head.

“Don't go out there.”

“I can see him.”

“He won't try to fight you.”

“I know.”

Minh moved back into shadow. Across the lane, an open gate led into a private bus company's repair yard. Six vehicles stood beneath a sheet-metal roof. In the last bay, three mechanics were still working around an exposed engine. Minh knew one of them. Bình had brought him a fuel pump in August after two shops pronounced it ruined.

He carried Duy into the yard. The coach he had repaired stood in the first bay, its replacement parts fitted and its service panels closed. Beside it, a mechanic was cleaning the floor of the inspection pit. A day's work had continued here while Minh's narrowed to the weight across his shoulders.

Bình put his spanner down as soon as he saw them.

“Accident?”

“Shooting. I need a vehicle.”

The other mechanics stopped working.

“I'll call an ambulance.”

“Call. Tell them men with guns are following us. Then get your people out of the open.”

Bình looked past Minh toward the gate.

“You've brought them here?”

“I didn't see another door.”

The mechanic's face tightened. He pointed toward the office. “There's a first-aid box inside. Put him on the bench. The small van has keys.”

Minh laid Duy down. The man caught his sleeve with two fingers.

“They'll go to the binding room.”

“Mai knows the risk.”

“I told him you'd call her if you couldn't reach a hospital.”

Minh went still.

“Why would you tell him that?”

“He asked who you trusted. I thought if I answered the easy questions, he wouldn't ask where I hid the sheets.”

“And you told him.”

Duy looked at the concrete between his feet.

The answer was not new. It only showed how much of the betrayal Duy had managed to describe to himself as conversation.

Minh picked up the office telephone. He told Mai to leave the binding room. She listened, then said the nurse had just arrived and they had cleared an exit through the storeroom next door.

“Give me somewhere else,” he said.

“The hospital, if you can reach it. But you just said there's a man waiting outside.”

“I'm dealing with him.”

“Then deal with him. Bring Duy. We are ready here. Lan has called again. Định knows you're moving.”

Minh looked at the office around him. Its windows faced the yard. The wall beneath them was concrete up to waist height. Beyond the desk, a short passage led to the changing room, which had no window facing the gate.

“Put him there,” he told Bình, indicating the passage. “Low. Away from the glass.”

The mechanic did not ask him to explain the geometry. He helped lower Duy onto folded work clothes while the youngest worker fetched clean water. Duy caught Minh's sleeve as he turned away.

“He'll use Nhi,” he whispered.

“He doesn't have her.”

“He doesn't need to. He just needs to say it.”

Minh looked down at him. “Then remember that when he says it.”

Outside, a vehicle stopped near the gate. Minh heard doors open on both sides, not merely the driver's. The men had arrived prepared to leave quickly or spread out quickly. Vũ usually preferred arrangements that could serve more than one purpose.

Behind Minh, Bình said, “You need to come outside.”

Vũ stood at the gate with four men. The one on his right carried a pistol openly. Bình's two younger mechanics had drawn back behind the engine stand. Neither looked old enough to have learned what expression to wear in a room like this. Both were adults, and both had grease on their hands from earning their living.

“Stay behind the block,” Minh told them.

Vũ watched him approach.

“You should have taken the money when it was offered.”

“You weren't there.”

“I'm here now.”

He placed a leather bag on the ground and opened it. Banknotes filled the interior.

“For your losses. The shutter, anything inside the shop. You can tell people I apologized.”

“Go home.”

Vũ glanced at his men. “Do you remember the first job we did together? The haulers who thought thirty men would be enough?”

Minh remembered. Vũ had waited in a car while he went inside.

“Thirty-two,” Minh said.

“It took you less than a minute. I've never forgotten that.”

“Then you know what happens if you stay.”

Vũ nodded toward the office.

“You win. Duy dies. Those are both possible.”

The man with the pistol raised it an inch.

Minh let his weight settle into both feet. A bus occupied the bay between the gate and the office. Its engine was out, its rear wheels resting on wooden chocks. It still had a frame, glass, seats, fuel in a small reserve tank, and enough metal to stop the men at the gate seeing where Duy lay. He needed to move it without driving its rear through the office wall. Too much force would kill the person he intended to shelter. Too little would leave a gap.

He measured the contact through the sole of his shoe, the slight slope beneath the wheels, the uneven resistance of the wooden chocks. There was no written equation. The whole yard became an equation, with people inside the terms he could not allow to disappear.

The office windows reflected the men at the entrance. In the second pane, a shape moved where the reflection of the gate should have been empty.

Someone had climbed the side wall.

Minh heard a boot scrape the narrow maintenance platform above the office.

He kept looking at Vũ.

“You have men behind me.”

“Yes.”

“They can leave too.”

“Give him to us. You don't owe that bastard anything.”

Minh let his eyes pass across the men at the entrance. One was holding his gun too tightly. Another kept glancing toward the office instead of Minh. Vũ had told them which person needed to die. The difficulty was that he had also told them stories about the man who would be standing in front of that person.

“You can still leave,” Minh said.

The nervous gunman shifted his feet.

Vũ noticed. “Stay where you are.”

“He's allowed to hear both offers.”

“You're making an offer now?”

“He goes home with the hands he arrived with.”

The other men looked at the gunman. Minh kept his attention on Vũ. Fear could travel through a group as quickly as an order if someone gave it a shape the group understood.

Vũ changed direction.

“Nhi is with company men,” he said. “Duy ought to consider that before he asks you to make decisions.”

Minh did not move.

“Where?”

“Somewhere safe.”

“You don't know.”

“You think you know everything because you can throw a man through a door?”

“I think you would have said her sister's address if you hadn't just noticed I wasn't frightened.”

For a moment Vũ's face became entirely still.

Minh raised his voice enough to reach the office.

“He doesn't have her, Duy.”

The man beside Vũ glanced toward the room. That glance gave Minh the last part of the arrangement. The shooter above the office would wait for someone inside to stand or move toward a window. The men at the gate would keep Minh looking at the wrong level while it happened.

He had time to understand it. He did not have time to explain it.

“He told you how to get through the house.”

“His choice.”

“You knew there were two women upstairs.”

Vũ closed the bag. He had finished pretending it would help.

Minh listened to the yard's small noises. The office fan rattled once per turn. A loosened belt hanging from the maintenance platform tapped a pillar in the wind. Above it, the hidden man's breathing became shallow and stopped.

Minh moved.

His hand struck the side of the engineless bus. Six tonnes of steel and glass slid sideways across the bay. The chocks split under its wheels. It crossed the line between the gunmen and the office as the first shots struck its side.

Before the bus stopped, Minh was on its roof.

The hidden shooter fired downward at the empty space he had just left. Minh crossed from the roof to the platform and hit him with his shoulder. The railing bent around the man's back. The pistol fell. Minh caught it, crushed the barrel against the rail, and dropped both man and weapon onto the bus roof below.

He landed in front of Vũ before the men at the gate had finished turning.

The nearest gunman tried to put his weapon against Minh's stomach. Minh closed the distance until the muzzle could not rise. He struck the man's forearm with his elbow. The bones broke beneath the sleeve. His other hand seized the man's collar and drove him through the open van door.

Glass fell across the banknotes.

One man dropped his gun and ran. Another kept firing into the bus. Minh caught that man by the back of the neck and struck his wrist against the gatepost until the gun fell. He threw him into the street. The body rolled to a stop beside the pharmacy.

The last man at the gate followed the one who had run. He abandoned his weapon beside the gatepost without being asked.

Then only Vũ remained standing at the entrance.

A crash came from the office side.

The man Minh had dropped onto the bus roof had regained his feet. He held the broken railing in both hands and was climbing toward the maintenance platform, where the office roof met the side wall. From there he could drop behind the shelter the bus provided.

Minh turned.

Vũ reached into the leather bag at his feet.

Minh saw the movement and had to choose which man he would reach first.

He chose neither.

He drove his heel against the van's open door. The door tore away from its remaining hinge and crossed the gap into Vũ's arms, striking him before the pistol inside the bag could clear the banknotes. At the same time, Minh pulled the fallen gate chain from its hook and threw it upward.

The chain caught the railing in the climber's hands. Minh seized the other end and pulled.

The man came off the roof.

He fell toward the concrete, still gripping the metal that had ceased to support him. Minh moved under him, caught his belt, and redirected the fall into the side of the bus. The impact dented the panel. The man collapsed between the wheels and lay motionless, breathing in rough, involuntary bursts.

Minh dropped the chain.

Vũ was on one knee beneath the van door. The leather bag had overturned, spilling money across the ground. A pistol lay half covered by the notes.

Minh reached it first.

He lifted the gun, removed it from Vũ's reach, and crushed the mechanism against the gatepost. The small violence of the metal breaking sounded final in a yard that had heard much louder things.

Vũ looked up at him.

“You couldn't leave it as talking,” Minh said.

He pulled the van door away and threw it aside. Vũ rose unsteadily, one arm pressed against his ribs. The bus had stopped on an angle with one wheel suspended above the inspection pit. Minh looked at the office behind it.

“Bình,” Minh called. “Check the office.”

The mechanic ran behind the vehicle. Minh listened for Duy's voice and heard him answer.

Vũ took one step backward.

Minh caught him by the throat and lifted him until they were looking directly into each other's faces. Vũ's shoes scraped against the gatepost. One of his hands closed on Minh's wrist. The other hung open at his side.

“Who gave the order to fire into the house?”

Vũ shook his head. Minh lowered him enough to breathe.

“Khang.”

“Who carried it out?”

“Not these men.”

“Then tell him these men are going to hospital. Tell him I am carrying Duy into one too.”

“He'll send more.”

Minh put him down. Vũ stayed against the post, coughing.

“You can come with them,” Minh said. “Or you can remember tonight accurately.”

He went back to the office. The bus had stopped every shot aimed at it. Bình stood over Duy with the first-aid box open. One of the mechanics was speaking on the telephone while the other watched the gate with a length of pipe in his hands.

Minh took the pipe from him and put it down.

“They've gone.”

“Are they coming back?” Bình asked.

“After I leave, lock the gate. Tell the police where I went. Tell everyone.”

Bình looked at the bus. “You moved that with one hand.”

“It will need a hoist.”

“That's not why I'm looking at it.”

Minh took Duy in his arms.

The van carried tools beneath its rear seat and smelled of hot vinyl. Minh laid Duy across the cleared space with folded jackets beneath his head. Bình had given him a clean cloth and tied the dressing tightly enough to stay where it was. The shoulder wound still seeped, but the flow had slowed.

The small van started on the second turn of the key. Bình stood beside its open window long enough to write his name and home number on the back of a receipt.

“For the statement,” he said. “I saw who fired.”

Minh folded the receipt into his pocket.

“Thank you.”

Bình closed the door for him. As Minh drove out, the mechanic raised the yard lights to their brightest setting. Every hole in the bus showed plainly from the street.

“Talk to me,” Minh said as he pulled into the road.

“About what?”

“Anything you can finish.”

Duy swallowed. “You broke his bus.”

“It wasn't a good evening for buses.”

“He's going to charge you.”

“Then I expect you to be alive when I divide the bill.”

The response from the back was a painful attempt at a laugh. Minh kept listening until it became breathing again.

At the first junction he saw headlights turn behind them. A motorbike followed for half a block, then another appeared at the corner ahead. Neither approached. They were trying to keep him in sight until someone with more men could decide where the van should stop.

Minh looked at the road. A row of market carts occupied the left side. On the right, a delivery truck stood beneath an awning while two workers shifted crates across its tailgate. The space between narrowed just beyond the point where the road bent.

He slowed.

The following motorbike closed the gap.

Minh turned into the open loading yard beside the truck. He drove through it, used the far gate, and came out on the parallel lane before the rider realized he had not stopped to unload. The maneuver required a turn the van was too long to make normally. Minh put one wheel onto the low curb, felt the body tilt, and brought it down precisely between a stack of crates and the gatepost.

Nothing struck Duy's side.

Behind them, the motorbike entered the wrong gate and found the truck reversing across its path. The driver shouted at him to wait. Minh was already around the next corner.

He did not need to outrun the whole city. He needed three streets of it to contain ordinary people doing things the men behind him could not command quickly enough.

The binding room's rear light appeared ahead.

Mai opened the door before he knocked. Hồng stood behind her with a folded sheet across both arms.

Minh stopped the van, switched off the engine, and sat for one second with both hands on the wheel.

“We're here,” he told Duy.

There was no answer.

He was out of the seat and opening the rear door before Mai reached the van.

## 21. Impressions

Mai held the binding-room door while Minh carried Duy inside.

She rented the room behind Mrs. Lan's office, two streets from the printing shop. Most of her finished orders were stored here, along with the books she kept for customers who could not afford an accountant of their own.

She had blue ink on the side of her thumb. The room behind her smelled of paper adhesive and warm dust. The folding table had been cleared between stacks of wedding albums. On it lay towels, a bowl of water, scissors, and an unopened packet of gauze.

“On his good side,” she said.

Hồng came forward. She had changed out of the clothes she wore to work and tied her hair back. She did not waste time asking questions whose answers were written across Duy's shirt. She cut the cloth away and examined both wounds.

“He's breathing,” she said before Minh could ask.

She spoke Duy's name sharply. His eyelids moved. She asked him to look at her, then to tell her where the pain was worst. He answered after a pause that frightened Minh more than any of the guns had.

“You're going to keep answering,” she told him. “You can become difficult again after someone has treated this properly.”

Lan appeared with another towel. Mai moved the lamp closer. Minh stood at the end of the table until Hồng told him which side to take.

“Hold him steady while I remove this.”

He did. Duy's body jerked when the soaked dressing lifted. Minh kept the injured shoulder from twisting without putting pressure on it. The distinction required more care than the bus had.

“Sorry,” Duy said through his teeth.

“Save that for someone you've offended,” Hồng answered. “I'm working.”

Minh looked at Mai. She had blood on one sleeve already, transferred from the van door or Duy's arm. She saw him notice and shook her head before he could turn it into an injury of hers.

“It's his.”

“Định?”

“He knows the shop was attacked. The first men sent there found the damage and spoke to your landlord's niece. Lan called again about the yard. He wants you at the hospital.”

“We can drive.”

Hồng looked up. “As soon as I have this secured. Not with him sliding around the back of a van while someone chases you.”

“Someone is already chasing us.”

“Then for once don't invite them to choose where he bleeds.”

Mai took the keys from Minh's hand.

“Lan's neighbor will move the van out of sight. We have another vehicle available if we need it. Sit for one minute.”

“I don't need—”

“I know what you don't need. Sit so Hồng can reach the table without climbing over your worry.”

He sat on the edge of a stack of paper. Duy turned his head toward him.

“Your wife is angry.”

“You've chosen a poor night to discover her.”

The attempt at a smile did not last. Hồng tightened the new dressing and Duy shut his eyes.

“He needs surgery.”

“Can he talk first?” Mai asked.

“Briefly. While I'm working.”

Mai brought the cassette recorder from Lan's office. It was a heavy two-deck machine normally used to copy music, with one button repaired using a piece of tape. She tested the blank cassette, spoke the date and her name, and played the words back long enough to know the microphone worked.

Duy watched her.

“You're recording me.”

“Yes.”

“Will Khang hear it?”

“Eventually, he may.”

“He'll say I'm lying.”

“Then don't.”

Minh looked at her. She had said it without cruelty and without making the sentence easier than it was. Duy breathed through his mouth for several seconds, then nodded.

“I need to speak to Nhi.”

Mai glanced at Hồng. The nurse did not stop working.

“If you can reach her, keep it short.”

Mai dialed from the office telephone and waited through several rings. When someone answered, she asked for Nhi without giving the room's address. She listened, then brought the receiver as far as the cord would reach.

Minh held it beside Duy's ear.

“It's me,” Duy said.

The answer at the other end was too faint for Minh to hear clearly. He watched Duy's face change as his wife spoke.

“I'm hurt. Minh has me.”

Another pause.

“No. Don't come here.”

His uninjured hand rose toward the receiver and fell back before reaching it.

“I told you I was trying to keep you out of it. I wasn't. I was keeping myself from having to say what it was.”

Nhi spoke for longer this time. Minh could hear her tone: frightened, furious, holding both together because the person listening needed her not to lose either.

“I did something at the warehouse,” Duy said. “They're going to ask me. I'm going to tell them.”

He listened again. His eyes filled, but he did not ask her to make the next sentence easier.

“I know that doesn't fix it.”

Hồng touched Minh's arm. Enough.

“Stay where you are,” Duy said. “Please. Let Mai tell you when it's safe.”

Minh took the receiver away. Nhi was still on the line.

“He can hear me?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Tell him I expect him to finish the sentence in person.”

Minh looked at Duy.

“She expects you to finish in person.”

Duy nodded. It was the first promise anyone had made that night which seemed to reach him without requiring an argument first.

Mai returned the receiver to the office. When she came back, she pressed the recorder's button.

Minh stood beside the table. He could have crushed the table and the concrete beneath it. He could not make the dark pool on the towel return to Duy's body.

She asked Duy his full name. She asked him to give the date and describe what he had done at the warehouse. When he said they had planned to burn it, she stopped him.

“Who does ‘they’ mean?”

He named Khang and two managers.

“Were you in the room?”

“Yes.”

“Tell us which parts you saw yourself.”

She had prepared this. While Minh was crossing roofs, she had been deciding how a frightened man's account might survive someone attempting to tear it apart.

The nurse worked without looking at the recorder. Duy described carrying out the fire order after the equipment had been moved. He described seeing a light upstairs and convincing himself it came from the building next door. Minh watched the nurse's hand stop for half a second, then continue.

“Who told you the workers had left?” Mai asked.

“Trọng.”

“Did you check?”

“No.”

“Did you have a way to check?”

Duy looked at Minh, then away. “Yes.”

“What did Khang say when you spoke afterward?”

“He asked whether it was done. I said yes. He said the company would take care of everything else.”

“Was anyone else present for that conversation?”

“Trọng. At the office. Vũ came in while we were talking.”

Mai let him distinguish the order from the later conversation. She asked where each man had been standing, not because the room's furniture would prove the crime, but because Duy kept replacing what he remembered with summaries that made everyone inside it smaller.

The details returned reluctantly. Khang standing beside the window. Trọng washing his hands in the office basin. Vũ asking whether Duy had been seen near the road. The wall clock not moving, as usual. Duy watching its hands while waiting to learn how much of the evening would be acknowledged as work.

Minh remembered that stopped clock. He felt a cold, exact anger at the ease with which a familiar room could contain things he had never asked it to show him.

“And tonight?” Mai asked.

Hồng looked at her. Mai held up one finger: one more part.

Duy told the recorder about the transfer that never existed, the copied sheets, the questions about Minh's house. He did not say he had been tricked into supplying the answers. He said he had supplied them because he hoped they would buy him time.

When he reached Khang's demand for Nhi's address, his voice failed.

“You didn't know it,” Minh said.

“No.”

“Say that.”

Duy said it. Then he named Trọng as the man who fired and Khang as the man who stood watching.

Mai stopped the recorder.

For several seconds no one spoke. Outside, a cart wheel rattled along the lane. Lan went to the rear door, listened, and came back. It was only the storeroom owner moving empty crates out of the passage.

When Duy had finished, Mai turned the machine toward Minh.

“Your turn.”

He looked at her.

“You said you were part of it.”

She started the recorder again.

He gave his name: Trần Quang Minh. He named the cold store and the money Khang had paid him. He described three other collections. At the last, he had broken a shopkeeper's arm because the man reached for a kitchen knife. The man's wife had been behind him. Minh had remembered the knife whenever he considered that afternoon. He had remembered the wife less often.

Mai kept her hand beside the recorder until he finished.

Then she put the cassette in an envelope and wrote the date on it.

“Lan is sending a copy to the reporter she knows through the trade association. Another goes with us to the hospital. I spoke to Định again. He's meeting us there with colleagues.”

“He heard Duy is speaking?”

“Yes. He said to bring the person first. The papers can follow.”

Lan carried the recording into the office and began making the second copy. Minh heard the machine's motors turn. They made a small, steady sound, absurdly calm beside everything being asked of the tape moving through them.

He put the fourteen sheets beside Mai's hand.

“These support parts of what he said.”

She nodded. “Then we'll keep them with it. They don't replace him.”

Duy opened his eyes. “You make me sound valuable.”

Minh looked at him. “Try living long enough to become expensive.”

Hồng gave a sharp little laugh before returning to the dressing. It changed the room for an instant. Duy was still a bleeding man on a borrowed table, but he was also a person who could hear someone speak to him without preparing a sentence for a court.

Mai checked the copied recording when Lan brought it back. Duy's voice came from the speaker, thin and frightened, saying the name of the man who had given the order. Lan stopped it before the next answer. They had what they needed to know: the machine had kept the words.

“Who carries it?” Minh asked.

“Someone who isn't carrying Duy,” Mai said.

Lan had arranged for her niece, an adult who worked nearby, to take the envelope out through a different gate and deliver it to the reporter's office. The young woman arrived carrying an ordinary shopping bag. She listened carefully, repeated the destination, and asked whether there was anything else she needed to say.

“Say where it came from,” Lan told her. “Say the people on it are going to hospital.”

She put the envelope beneath the shopping and left without making the departure resemble a ceremony.

Minh watched the door close.

“She knows men are looking?”

“She knows enough to choose,” Lan said. “She's my niece. I don't send her through doors blind.”

He accepted the correction and went back to the table.

Duy's skin had become cool beneath his fingers. The man looked at him with the distant concentration of someone using more strength to remain awake than he had once used to stand.

“Is Nhi all right?”

“Yes.”

“You said.”

“I'll keep saying it.”

“I need you to know I didn't come here expecting forgiveness.”

Minh held his gaze. “Good. I haven't packed any.”

The smallest movement touched Duy's mouth. Then he closed his eyes again.

Hồng stepped back and took a fresh breath.

“We leave now.”

Mai gathered the transport sheets from beside the wedding albums. One page caught her eye. She compared its stamp with the list beneath it and turned it over.

“This page is missing its attachment.”

Duy nodded from the table. “Khang has it.”

“Then say what's on it.”

The nurse shook her head. “Now we move.”

Mai folded the page and put it away without asking the question again. The people in the room had reached the limit of what paper could do for a man losing blood.

The telephone rang in Lan's office.

Everyone looked toward it. Mai went to answer, expecting the call that would tell them where to bring the van. She listened for three seconds and turned back toward Minh.

“Khang.”

Minh crossed the room. He took the receiver from her and waited without speaking.

“You have collected a remarkable number of people,” Khang said.

Minh looked through the open doorway at Hồng, at Duy on the table, at Lan taking her bag from the shelf.

“What do you want?”

“To prevent something worse.”

“You've sent several worse things already.”

“Vũ tells me his men are hurt.”

“He should have listened when I offered them an evening at home.”

Khang breathed into the line. Minh could hear an engine behind him. Not a car engine. The slower, heavier idle of a freight truck.

“You think everything ends when the man in front of you falls,” Khang said.

“No. You keep calling afterward.”

Mai remained beside him. She had heard enough of Khang's voice through the receiver to know he had not called to ask whether Duy required medicine.

“Let me speak to him.”

“He's busy staying alive.”

“He has taken company documents. He has shot a man. He has brought his trouble into your wife's business. There is still a way to make this smaller.”

Minh looked at the clean envelopes on the desk.

“Say it.”

“Send everyone else home. Bring Duy and the papers to me. You and I discuss what happened as men who have worked together. Your wife goes back to her orders. You go back to your shop.”

“The shop you shot through.”

“Damage can be repaired.”

“I know. I repair it.”

For a moment only the engine filled the line. Minh listened to its uneven idle, one cylinder firing with a heavier note than the others. He had heard that engine before at Đông Lợi's yard. He could not yet place which truck carried it.

“I gave you a life,” Khang said.

Minh glanced toward Mai. She folded her arms and waited for his answer rather than offering one herself.

“You gave me work.”

“I trusted you.”

“You trusted what happened when I put my hands on someone you wanted moved.”

“And you took the money.”

“Yes.”

The direct answer deprived Khang of the argument he had prepared. He tried another.

“Duy set the fire. Did he tell you that?”

“Yes.”

“Then you know what kind of man you're protecting.”

Minh looked toward the table. Hồng was speaking quietly to Duy while she secured the last dressing. The man was listening because he had finally run out of anything more useful to do than obey someone who wanted him alive.

“I know,” Minh said.

“You want to ruin everything for him?”

“I want him to tell it while you're close enough to answer.”

The engine note changed. Someone at the other end had pressed the accelerator and released it.

“You have made your choice,” Khang said.

“I made it when I opened the shutter.”

“Then don't blame me for what follows.”

Minh's hand tightened around the receiver. The plastic creaked. He loosened his grip before it broke.

“I'll blame the man who gives the order. I'm becoming very particular about that.”

He put the receiver down.

Mai watched his face.

“He's in a truck,” Minh said. “One of the freight vehicles. He expects us to leave by road.”

“Can you tell where?”

“Not from that.”

She nodded. He could hear how much she valued the limit being stated plainly, even now.

“Then we keep moving,” she said.

He touched her cheek with the back of his cleanest fingers. It was a brief gesture, made in a room that had no time to accommodate a larger one. She covered his hand with hers and released it.

Minh went back to the table and bent to lift Duy.

From the front came the sound of a vehicle braking hard. At the rear, the storeroom owner shouted that someone had put a chain through the courtyard gate.

Minh straightened before Duy's feet left the table.

The room's two exits had become two places men were waiting.

## 22. Smoke

Minh heard metal strike the rear door.

It was not the chain being fastened. That sound had come from farther down the passage. This was a bar striking the wood close enough for the hinges to carry the impact into the wall.

Hồng put herself between Duy and the door. Minh moved her aside with one hand.

“Don't stand where they can shoot through.”

She took the instruction without arguing. Mai pulled the table away from the direct line of the entrance. Lan switched off the office light, leaving the lamp over Duy as the only bright point in the room.

Outside, someone shouted Minh's name.

“Bring him out.”

The voice did not belong to Vũ. Khang had found men who had not watched a bus move sideways.

He caught Mai by the wrist before she could turn. Across the room, under the front door, a thin orange line appeared.

For one second his mind arranged every object in the building: doors, shutters, paper, ink, the old electrical panel. The room was a stack of things that would burn. Someone outside had understood that too.

“Stay low. Behind the table.”

Minh could smell fuel beneath the hot paper scent. It reached him before the flame did. Someone had thrown it along the entrance, trusting the rooms behind it to provide the rest of the work.

He thought of Tấn at the freight lift, the last ordinary task made urgent by someone else's decision. He did not have time to think beyond that. The men outside were about to discover the difference between an occupied building and an occupied building containing him.

The front door blew inward.

Minh reached it before the broken lock hit the floor. He drove the first attacker backward through the doorway, carrying the man on one arm. A second man fired. Minh turned the first into the gun's path. The impact jolted the body against his chest.

He threw the dead man into the shooter and struck once. Teeth and blood hit the shutters opposite. The shooter fell under both bodies.

A third attacker stood beside a plastic fuel container. He dropped it and reached inside his jacket.

Minh crossed the street between the reach and the draw. His palm struck the man's chest. The man hit the closed bakery door hard enough to split its panel. His pistol remained inside his jacket.

A fourth man stood beside the vehicle at the corner.

He had seen the first three fall. His face changed as Minh looked at him. The gun in his hand came up anyway, carried by the decision he had made before he understood what it required.

Minh pulled the broken bakery panel free of its hinges and threw it.

It crossed the lane flat, turning once. The man fired through it. The bullet struck brick above Minh's shoulder. The panel struck the gunman across the body and carried him into the vehicle's open door.

Minh reached him before he could recover.

The man tried to raise the weapon between them. Minh caught his wrist, forced the muzzle upward, and drove his forehead into the man's face. The nose broke. Blood spread over both their shirts. A second impact put the man down against the seat, eyes unfocused, gun fallen into the footwell.

Minh bent the door inward until the man could not easily climb out.

“Stay,” he said.

The vehicle's driver was already running. Minh let him go. Behind him, the fire had reached the paper stacked nearest the front window.

Minh came back through smoke.

Mai and the nurse had Duy off the table. Mai carried the evidence in a canvas bag slung around her neck. The hem of her trousers was burning.

Minh smothered it in his hands.

“Can you walk?”

She took a step, then another. “Yes.”

The rear door shook under another blow. Through the thin gap at its edge, Minh saw the movement of a shoulder, the short outline of a weapon. The men outside had expected him to choose between fire and their waiting line.

He looked at the side wall instead.

The storeroom lay beyond it. Its owner had moved the crates from the courtyard earlier. Minh had heard his voice at the chained gate. Somewhere on the other side, the man was still trying to make an exit work.

Minh struck the wall twice with his fist, hard enough to make the plaster jump.

“Move away!”

The owner shouted something in reply. Minh waited only long enough to hear feet scrape clear.

He went ahead and drove his shoulder through the partition to the neighboring storeroom. Bricks fell around him. The owner had already opened his street door and stood there holding a fire extinguisher. Mai shouted that there were people coming through. He put the extinguisher into Minh's hands and helped the nurse over the rubble.

Outside, residents were carrying buckets. A man hauled his motorbike away from the flames. Lan emerged from a doorway farther down the street, one envelope already gone from her hands.

The fire made the lane bright and difficult to understand. People came out carrying things they could not immediately leave behind: a cash box, a sleeping mat, a sack of clothes. A woman held a birdcage against her chest while her husband tried to pull their front door shut, as if the gesture might keep the heat from learning the address.

“Upstairs,” the storeroom owner said. “There's a tenant upstairs.”

Minh looked at the staircase inside the building. Smoke had begun moving through it from the shared roof space. The owner took a step toward it, then stopped when a broken piece of ceiling fell across the lower stairs.

“He's hurt his leg. He can't come down quickly.”

Hồng caught Minh's sleeve.

“Duy goes to the street. Now.”

He carried Duy through the storeroom and laid him on the cleared bed of the owner's small delivery truck. Hồng climbed up beside him. Mai opened the canvas bag and put the copied recording beneath the seat where the nurse indicated.

“Don't wait at the front,” Minh told them. “Keep the truck beside this wall until I return.”

“How long?” Mai asked.

He looked at the second-floor window, where smoke had begun spilling around a partly opened shutter.

“Long enough to get him.”

He went up the outside wall.

There was no ladder. He used the narrow ledge above the storeroom door, one hand on the window frame, a foothold in damaged plaster. The first pull lifted him farther than an ordinary man's whole body could have managed. His second movement carried him through the upper window as the shutter tore loose beneath his shoulder.

Inside, a man sat beside a bed with a wooden crutch between his knees. He had tied a cloth over his mouth. A small suitcase stood beside him, closed and ready, an arrangement made by someone who knew he could not carry it down alone.

“Leave the case,” Minh said.

The man nodded. He had expected to argue with a neighbor or plead with the smoke. The sight of a large stranger entering through the window seemed to settle both possibilities.

Minh lifted him against his chest. The injured leg hung awkwardly. He supported it with one forearm and turned toward the window.

“Close your eyes.”

“We're jumping?”

“Yes.”

“From here?”

“You'll miss most of the view.”

The man shut his eyes.

Minh stepped out.

He struck the wall once with his free foot on the way down, changing the fall enough to clear the people gathered beneath the window. He landed beside the truck. The road cracked beneath one heel. The tenant cried out as the movement jarred his injured leg, but Minh had kept its weight supported.

He set the man on a chair someone brought from the neighboring shop.

“His suitcase,” the storeroom owner began.

Minh looked up. Flame moved behind the window he had just left.

“I'm sorry.”

The owner swallowed the rest of the request and knelt beside his tenant.

From the rear passage came gunfire.

The men at the back had realized their quarry was leaving through another building. A bullet struck the truck's side panel. Another cut a line through the sacks stacked against the wall.

Hồng folded herself across Duy. Mai dropped below the cab window.

Minh turned toward the passage.

The first gunman had reached the corner. He saw Minh and tried to stop. The man behind him kept moving, pushing him forward into the open.

Minh crossed the space in two strides.

He caught the first man's arm and drove him backward into the second. Their bodies struck the wall together. One gun fired into the ground. Minh stamped on it hard enough to drive the metal through the thin surface of the drain cover beneath it.

The second man tried to bring a blade around the first man's shoulder.

Minh seized the cutting wrist and pulled him clear. He struck once, low in the chest. The man folded around the blow and fell against the passage wall, unable to take another breath quickly enough to stand.

The first gunman was still struggling.

“Enough,” Minh said.

He drove the man's head into the brick beside him. The struggle ended. Minh lowered him to the ground rather than letting his body block the passage.

For a moment he stood over both men, listening. No more feet approached from the rear. The fire was louder now. Somewhere beyond the lane, a siren had begun.

He went back to the truck.

Mai watched his hands. There was blood on them that had not come from Duy.

“Can we leave?” she asked.

“Yes.”

She did not ask him to explain the answer while the building was still burning.

Minh looked back at the binding room. Mai's books, her orders, the work that had paid their rent while his repair shop found customers.

She followed his look.

“Get in the truck,” she said. “I'm coming with you.”

The command restored the practical world around him. Minh took the keys from the owner, gave him the location of Bình's van, and said he would return both vehicles or pay for them.

“The van isn't moving,” the man said. “They shot through the front.”

Minh looked toward the dark shape beneath the awning. Fluid had spread under its engine. Another thing borrowed in good faith had become part of the damage.

“Then don't try to start it.”

The owner nodded. He had a fire to watch and a tenant to help. He could postpone anger at the stranger who had broken through his wall until those were finished.

Lan came to the cab carrying a mobile telephone.

“My neighbor's,” she said. “He'll need it back.”

Mai took it. “We're going to the hospital.”

“I know. I told Định the first address was no longer safe.”

“Your niece?”

“She delivered the envelope.”

The words reached Minh as he climbed into the driver's seat. A part of Duy's story had already passed beyond the buildings Khang could set alight tonight. It did not make the wounded man less necessary. It made killing him less useful.

Mai shut the cab door. Hồng struck the panel twice to tell them she was ready.

Minh drove.

At the corner, the man trapped in the bent car door watched them pass. He tried to turn his face away. Minh kept his eyes on the road.

The first main street was blocked by vehicles slowing to look at the smoke. Minh could have forced the little truck through the gaps. Duy would have paid for every turn with his wounds. He stopped long enough to see how the traffic was arranging itself, then took a narrow lane where the surface was worse but the movement belonged to him.

Mai called the hospital. She gave Duy's condition as Hồng had described it and listened to the instructions. Then she called Định. The investigator was on his way, but moving across the city was not something a telephone could complete for him.

“They're expecting us,” she told Minh.

He nodded.

Behind them, two motorbikes appeared at the lane entrance.

He saw them in the mirror and reduced speed rather than increasing it. The riders had expected flight. Their engines climbed before they understood the little truck was letting them approach.

“Stay low,” he told Mai.

The first rider came alongside the cab. His passenger held a pistol against his own thigh, waiting until the road opened enough to use it without striking his driver.

Minh reached through the window.

His hand closed around the passenger's jacket. He lifted the man off the moving motorbike and brought him against the side of the cab. The gun discharged once toward the road. Minh struck the wrist against the window frame until the weapon fell, then threw the man into a stack of empty baskets beside a shuttered stall.

The driver fled ahead without looking back.

The second motorbike braked so hard its rear wheel slid. Its passenger had seen enough. He shouted at the rider to turn, and both disappeared into the cross street.

Mai remained crouched beneath the dashboard for another breath.

“Can I sit up?”

“Yes.”

She rose, looked at the torn strip of cloth still caught in Minh's hand, and took it from him before it could interfere with the wheel.

“Try to keep both hands inside for a while,” she said.

“I'll see what the road asks.”

“The road has asked enough.”

He drove out of the lane and into the broader avenue beyond it. Behind them, the fire's glow had become a stain above the rooftops. Ahead, the city continued toward midnight, full of people who knew nothing about the man bleeding in the back of a borrowed truck.

## 23. Eighteen Tonnes

The avenue opened ahead of them, wider than the lanes and almost empty beneath the lights. Hồng sat beside Duy in back. Mai held the borrowed telephone against her chest while Minh listened to the little delivery truck's engine.

It was approaching midnight. He kept their speed below what the engine could manage. Every broken surface reached Duy through the truck bed. Hồng struck the cab twice when Minh took a corner too quickly.

He eased the wheel back a fraction and felt the rear of the vehicle settle. The truck's springs were made for weight distributed across boxes, not a wounded man lying against folded cloth. He could read each movement through the steering column: the left rear tire slightly softer than the right, a worn shock absorber allowing the body to continue moving after the road had stopped asking it to.

He adjusted before the next rut, letting the wheels meet it at an angle that divided the impact. Hồng did not strike the cab again.

Mai looked at him.

“You're doing something.”

“Driving.”

“Differently.”

“He doesn't need to feel the whole road.”

She watched the mirror for a moment. Duy lay still, one arm across his chest. Hồng had braced herself beside him so the next movement would go through her body before reaching his shoulder.

“Can you get there faster?” Mai asked.

“Yes.”

“Can you get him there faster?”

He looked at her, briefly, then back at the road.

“This is the fastest version that includes him.”

She nodded. The distinction had cost him three turns and most of the speed he could have used. He was grateful she understood it without needing to hear the numbers.

Mai was speaking into the mobile telephone Mrs. Lan had borrowed for her. She described the fire, the truck, the direction they were traveling. She listened, then said the hospital's name back carefully.

“They're meeting us at the emergency entrance.”

He nodded.

The hospital lay beyond a series of junctions he knew from collecting parts nearby. There were several ways to reach it in daylight. At this hour, one route narrowed around parked delivery vehicles and another passed a market already beginning its preparations for morning. He chose the road with the widest approaches, where he would be able to see a stopped vehicle before it became a wall.

Behind them, an engine changed note.

Minh heard it through the open window. The uneven idle from Khang's telephone call had become a heavy pull under load. One cylinder still struck harder than the others. He remembered the truck now: a large freight vehicle with a repaired front crossmember and a steering wheel Trọng preferred because it sat higher than the one in the other cab.

He glanced at the mirror.

No headlights yet.

The sound came from the parallel road, separated from them by a row of closed warehouses. Minh could place its movement through the moments when the walls interrupted it. It was keeping pace, waiting for a junction where the two roads met.

“Khang's truck,” he said.

Mai turned toward the side window.

“You can hear it?”

“Yes.”

“Can we turn?”

“He's expecting that.”

He considered the smaller lanes. A turn would give the heavier vehicle less space to follow, but it would also put them among houses, narrow exits, people coming out to discover why engines were racing past their doors. Khang had already shown what he considered an acceptable answer to a building containing someone he wanted.

Minh kept to the avenue.

“Call again,” he told Mai. “Tell them a freight truck is following.”

She made the call. Her voice remained level until the person at the other end asked her to repeat the location. Then the anger entered it, clean and useful.

“We're bringing a man who has been shot. We're almost there. Listen to the street name I'm giving you.”

Minh heard the answer become more attentive. He watched the junction ahead.

“Your hands.”

He looked down. Blisters had risen where he had extinguished her trousers. Blood from the doorway fight was drying between his fingers.

“I can still use them.”

“That's not what I meant.”

He wanted to tell her he would rebuild the business. He wanted the future to arrive immediately, with clean plaster and a working press and no need to continue this conversation. He watched the road instead.

“I brought them to you.”

“Yes.”

“He thinks he can still make this a bargain.”

“Then stop waiting for him to understand why it isn't.”

Minh eased the truck around a bicycle loaded with vegetables. For years he would remember the pale stems projecting from the basket, how clear they had been beneath the lamps when everything else was narrowing.

“I have,” he said.

An engine accelerated behind them.

In the mirror he saw a freight truck swing out around a motorbike. Đông Lợi's lettering ran along the cab door. Its headlights rose and fell as it gained on them.

The driver gave no signal.

The truck's cab moved into view behind them. It was the vehicle Minh had identified by sound. Above the high sideboards, he could see the shapes of crated machine parts and broken blocks loaded unevenly into the bed. This was no empty transfer. Trọng had brought weight because he knew the man in front of him had stopped lighter things.

Khang had chosen a weapon that included his own belief in Minh's strength.

The first horn blast was long and continuous.

Hồng shouted from the back. Minh could not make out the words, but he understood the urgency. The distance between the trucks was closing more quickly than the small engine could recover while carrying Duy gently.

He pressed the accelerator farther. The delivery truck responded with effort rather than speed.

The freight vehicle crossed the center of the road, denying him an easy turn. Trọng knew this part of the city too. He knew where a smaller truck might escape, where the morning sellers left their carts, which corners required slowing before the driver could see what waited beyond them.

For three seconds, Minh saw every possible route as a line through the moving street.

Each line carried someone else's body at the end.

Minh looked ahead. A delivery van was unloading at the next intersection. Two men stood beside it, carrying crates. There was nowhere to pull their own truck clear without driving through them.

Beyond the van, the hospital entrance lights showed above the wall. Close enough that he could read the large sign. Too far for Duy to reach without being carried.

He could throw the little truck sideways. He could overturn the freight vehicle by striking it from an angle. The first choice would send Hồng and Duy across the road inside a vehicle that had never been built to survive his answer. The second would sweep the delivery workers beneath eighteen tonnes of metal and cargo.

The only route that did not pass through them ended where he stood.

He braked.

Mai looked behind, then back at him.

“Take them out on your side.”

“Minh—”

“Now.”

Mai looked at the mirror once more. Then she released the door latch.

“Come after us,” she said.

He did not promise. There was no time to make the sentence honest before the truck arrived.

He left the cab while the delivery truck was still moving. His feet struck the road, lost purchase, found it. He caught its tailgate and stopped it by pulling backward. In the bed, the nurse cried out and folded herself across Duy.

The freight truck was sixty meters away.

Minh slapped the side panel. Mai was already opening the rear latch. The two men from the van put down their crates and ran to help her when they saw the blood.

He stepped into the approaching truck's path.

At the wheel sat Khang's operations manager. Khang himself occupied the other seat. Even through the windshield Minh could see his mouth moving.

The truck accelerated.

Minh estimated its load from the compressed rear springs, its speed from the turning wheel hubs. Eighteen tonnes, perhaps more. The road beyond him contained four people carrying a fifth. He worked through the forces without words. The calculation ended in the destruction of his hands, then his arms, then whatever remained behind them.

He lowered his center of gravity anyway.

He remembered Tín's slipper touching his heel. The chair that would not move when the old man's fingers rested on it. A week spent learning to cross a room without making the floor announce him.

Four years after the first lesson, Minh finally understood where the old man had placed his weight.

He moved one foot half its width. His knees loosened. His shoulders stopped trying to become the whole answer.

The truck filled his sight.

Through the windshield, Trọng saw the adjustment and tried to turn. Khang caught his arm. Minh saw the argument happen in their faces: the driver who had watched him land on a moving launch, the owner who needed the world to contain something heavier than refusal.

Khang pointed straight ahead.

Trọng drove.

The truck hit him.

Its grille folded around his hands. Both shoes burst at the seams. His heels cut black tracks through the road surface, then opened two trenches as the aggregate broke beneath them.

Something tore in his right forearm.

The pain was immediate and specific. Two fingers lost part of their strength. The load moved toward the remaining ones, asking them to accept an arrangement no living tissue should have survived.

Minh changed his grip without letting go.

The grille disintegrated. His palms reached the structure behind it. He felt the front crossmember bend, recognized the old repair through the way the metal failed, and pushed beyond the weakness into something that could carry the force back through the frame.

The road continued sliding beneath his feet.

He could hear Mai shouting. The sound came from farther away now. Good. He let the word exist without looking toward it.

The truck's weight tried to turn him. Its right side rode over a raised patch of road, changing the load between his arms. Minh followed the movement before it could reach his spine. His left shoulder lowered. His right hip moved back. The trenches behind his heels deepened.

He was no longer simply holding something heavy. He was holding the next fraction of a second in a shape four people could escape through.

He did not release the truck.

The engine roared against him. He felt each cylinder firing through the ruined front end, the pressure climbing as the driver held his foot down. Minh pushed back. The windshield shattered. The frame bowed. One rear wheel lifted clear of the road and spun above it.

He stopped eighteen tonnes with the emergency entrance lights visible beyond the next junction.

The sudden stillness struck the vehicle harder than the collision had struck him. Cargo shifted against the forward bulkhead. A wooden crate burst, throwing machine parts through its broken slats. The cab shuddered around the men inside it.

Trọng's mouth hung open. Khang stared through the missing windshield at the man whose hands had stopped the truck.

Minh looked back at him.

There was no triumph in the exchange yet. Only recognition, arriving too late to be used for a different decision.

For a moment all he could hear was the engine.

Then the driver tried to reverse.

Minh drove the front of the truck downward. The axle struck the road. The engine stalled with a hard internal crack.

He pulled his hands free. Skin remained on the metal.

His feet stood in the grooves they had made. The shoes were gone except for torn pieces caught around his ankles. When he tried to lift one foot, pain flashed up his leg with a clarity that nearly made him laugh.

The body kept accounts too. It had simply waited until the larger payment was finished.

He stepped clear of the truck and looked toward the hospital.

Mai and the delivery workers were carrying Duy through the outer gate. Hồng walked beside his head, speaking to him continuously. A porter had seen them and was bringing a trolley. None of them had stopped moving to watch what Minh had done.

He was glad.

Khang climbed out carrying a pistol.

His expensive shoes struck the road badly. He caught the doorframe with his free hand and looked toward the gate where Duy was disappearing. Blood ran from a cut above one eyebrow, crossing the side of his face in a thin, patient line.

“They're taking him,” he said.

Minh stared at him.

Even now, Khang could make the loss sound like property leaving a yard.

“Yes.”

“You don't know what he'll say.”

“I heard him.”

Khang lifted the pistol slightly. “You could still finish this.”

Minh looked at the gun, then at his hands. He did not need all ten fingers to reach the man in front of him. He needed enough of the rest of his body to arrive before Khang remembered where to aim.

Minh tried to close his right hand. Two fingers obeyed.

Behind him Mai was shouting for the men to keep moving.

Khang leveled the pistol past Minh, toward her voice.

Minh reached him before the trigger moved. His left hand closed over the weapon and drove it upward. The shot tore through the cab roof. With his damaged right arm he struck Khang under the ribs and lifted him clear of the ground.

He could have kept lifting. He could have put the man through the metal above them.

Instead he threw him to the road and placed a bare foot across his wrist.

“Get away from my family.”

Khang tried to pull his arm free. Minh's foot remained across it. The attempt made the man's breath catch, but he tried again, unable to stop treating resistance as a delay in the agreement he wanted.

“I can pay for everything,” he said.

Minh looked at the shattered truck, the torn road, the hospital gate beyond it.

“You don't even know everything.”

“Name it.”

“No.”

The word left nothing for Khang to negotiate. He stared up at Minh with the baffled fury of a man who had spent his life finding the number beneath every sentence and had reached one that would not open.

Khang spat blood against Minh's ankle.

“You think they're going to let you go?”

Minh increased the pressure until the gun came loose.

“No.”

## 24. The Door

Trọng came out of the opposite door with a length of steel.

Minh heard his step through the truck's broken chassis. He turned, caught the bar beneath his left arm, and kept turning. The driver was pulled off balance and struck the cab face-first. He slid down it and did not rise.

When Minh turned back, Khang was crawling toward the fallen pistol.

Minh kicked it underneath the truck.

Sirens approached from the direction of the hospital. He listened until he could separate two vehicles, then three. Someone in the van had been calling for help as well. Windows were opening above the street. A resident appeared with a camera and photographed the truck without asking permission from anyone.

Khang rolled onto his back. His shirt had ridden above his stomach. He looked less like the owner of anything now.

“You've killed men tonight,” he said.

Minh looked toward the hospital. Mai and the others had disappeared through the gate. He could not see Duy.

“Are you listening?” Khang demanded.

“I heard you.”

Minh sat on the road beside him. It hurt to lower himself. Beneath his torn trousers his knees were shaking with an exhaustion he had never felt before. He extended his injured arm across his lap and held it still.

When the police arrived, he raised his empty left hand.

He gave his name again.

They separated him from Khang, took possession of the truck, and asked who had been driving. The questions came over one another. Minh answered the ones he could. When an officer told him to stand, he stood. When another saw his hands and called for a medical team, he sat back down.

One of the delivery workers came across from the van. He still held the strap he had been using to lower a crate. He pointed out where the truck had changed direction, where Minh had been standing, where the injured man and the two women had gone.

Khang told an officer the man was confused.

“I was right fucking there,” the worker said.

His companion arrived carrying the telephone they had borrowed from a shop. He had left the shopkeeper waiting in the doorway and wanted to return it. Before he did, he gave his name. The two men argued briefly about whether the larger truck had accelerated before or after the smaller one stopped. They agreed on who had been walking toward the hospital and who had tried to drive over them.

Minh listened without helping. He wanted to correct their distances. The impulse was almost unbearable. The first man put him three steps farther from the impact than he had been. The second believed the back wheels had risen together. Minh had felt only the near side lift clear.

None of that belonged to him now.

The resident with the camera asked an officer to move out of his photograph. Khang turned his face away. The shutter clicked again.

Trọng woke when they rolled him onto his side. He spat blood and asked where Khang was. The question came out with the automatic expectation that somebody else would still know what to do.

“Here,” Minh told him.

Trọng looked at the ruined truck and then at Minh's hands. He stopped asking.

Mai came out of the hospital behind a stretcher.

“Duy?” he asked.

“In surgery.”

“The papers?”

“Delivered. They listened to the recording.”

She knelt beside him without touching the ruined arm. An officer asked her to move back. She told him she was Minh's wife and would move when the medical staff arrived.

The officer let her stay.

Only then did Minh see the burn above her ankle. The cloth had stuck to it.

“You need treatment.”

“I've had someone look.”

“Mai.”

“I'll have it dressed inside. Stop trying to solve the next thing before you tell me about this one.”

He looked at the truck. Its front end had been driven into the road around the imprint of his hands. There was no explanation he could offer that would make the sight ordinary.

“I thought I could do enough good work that what I did for him would stop mattering.”

She watched his face.

“I know.”

“I didn't want you to know all of it.”

“I know that too.”

A medical attendant arrived and cut through the sleeve of Minh's shirt. Mai moved aside. The torn muscle in his forearm showed pale beneath the blood. Minh looked away. He could calculate what had happened to it. That did not make watching someone clean it easier.

At the hospital they placed him on a bed with an officer beside the door. Mai sat across from him while his hands were cleaned. The room had a steel cabinet, a sink, and one chair whose shorter leg had been supported with folded cardboard.

Outside, porters pushed a trolley with a damaged wheel. A woman argued about a missing bag. Someone brought tea to the officers and needed to know how many cups.

The first doctor who examined him asked how the injury had happened.

“A truck.”

“It hit you?”

Minh considered the distinction. “Yes.”

“How fast?”

He gave the speed. The doctor looked at the officer, who looked toward the street as though the truck might supply its own explanation if they waited long enough.

“And you walked here?” the doctor asked.

“They brought me.”

“Good. One sensible decision.”

The man worked carefully, asking Minh to move each finger before and after he touched the damaged places. Minh obeyed. He could still feel the road through his feet even with his legs resting on the bed. Each time somebody pushed a trolley outside, some part of him prepared to stand in front of it.

Mai noticed his heels move beneath the sheet.

“Lie still.”

“I am.”

“You're trying to get up with your ankles.”

The doctor glanced at her. “Keep doing that.”

“What?” Minh asked.

“I was speaking to your wife.”

The officer at the door coughed into his fist. Minh gave him a look. The man became interested in the corridor.

Later, when the bleeding had been controlled and the first dressings replaced, Hồng came in long enough to drink a cup of water. Her shirt had blood along both sleeves. She held the cup with two hands.

“He's still in theatre,” she said before Minh could ask. “They know more than I do now.”

“You kept him alive.”

“You carried him through half the city. Next time, try arriving before he loses that much blood.”

“I'll mention it to the people shooting.”

She looked at him over the cup. Then her shoulders lowered, and a short laugh escaped her. It ended as quickly as it came. She had been awake at somebody else's disaster for hours, and nothing was funny enough to make that disappear.

Mai took the empty cup.

“Go sit somewhere without him in it,” she told her.

Hồng touched Mai's shoulder on the way out. Minh watched the nurse go and understood that she had spent the night giving orders to a man capable of lifting a bus. At no point had she found that an adequate substitute for keeping pressure on a wound.

At dawn, Định came in with Mai's envelope.

He asked Minh about the cold store. Minh gave the dates as far as he remembered them. He described the men who had gone inside, the vehicle they had used, the voice behind the door. The investigator asked why he had not opened it.

Minh looked down at the dressings.

“I was paid to keep it closed.”

Định wrote that down.

There were questions about the binding room and the men left outside it. Minh said what he had done. He could remember each impact with terrible precision. When Định asked which man had fired first, he described the muzzle flash, the broken frame, the position of the people behind him. When he did not know a name, he said so.

“Don't guess one for me,” Định said. “A face and a name are different things.”

“I know.”

“You've spent the last ten minutes answering before I've finished asking. I need to hear you know.”

Minh leaned back against the pillow. “All right.”

Định turned a page.

“Vũ was found at the company office. He had changed his shirt. The woman there says he wanted the vehicle keys and the money from a locked drawer.”

Minh pictured the clean cuffs. “Did she give them to him?”

“She gave the keys to the officers. She had been alone in the office since midnight and was tired of being told different things by different men.”

“That sounds like him.”

“Which part?”

“Thinking everybody else's time belongs to him.”

Định looked up. “You can tell me what he did at the bus yard after you rest. The owner is here. He has already described some of it.”

“Bình's alive?”

“He's downstairs, explaining why an officer should stop leaning on a damaged panel.”

Minh closed his eyes for a moment.

“And Trọng?”

“Under guard. So is Khang. Neither of them is giving orders to anyone outside this hospital.”

That was smaller than saying the company was finished. It was also the thing Minh needed to know. He asked about Lan and her niece. Định told him both had been spoken to. The niece had handed over her copy in front of two people and could say when. A reporter had telephoned to ask about the fire. The fourteen sheets had been separated from the statements so that nobody would mistake a collection of paper for a person describing what he had done.

“The recording is Duy,” Định said. “I still need Duy.”

“He's here.”

“Yes.”

For the first time that night, the investigator allowed the answer to be enough.

When the interview paused, Mai used the borrowed phone to call her grandfather.

Minh heard her say that they were alive. He heard her tell him not to come yet, that there was nowhere for him to sit and more questions were coming.

Then she held the telephone out.

“He wants you.”

Minh took it awkwardly between his bandaged palms.

Tín's voice was thin over the line, familiar and entirely awake.

“Can you move your fingers?”

“Some.”

“Will they mend?”

“They don't know.”

The old man breathed into the receiver. Minh waited for a judgment, or instructions about enduring pain, or some revelation concerning the feat in the road.

“Give the phone back to Mai,” Tín said. “You're making her hold your arm up.”

Minh looked at her hand beneath his wrist. He had not noticed.

He returned the phone.

Hòa arrived with clean clothes shortly afterward. He had taken the call from his father, listened to what Mai had said through the receiver, and begun packing before either of them finished arguing about whether anyone should come.

“He wanted to come with me,” he said.

Mai shut her eyes. “Is he on his way?”

“No.”

“How did you persuade him?”

“I told him I was going.”

Hòa set the bag down. Minh looked at him over it. There was no boast in the answer and no explanation waiting behind it. The teacher wore the same sandals he used in his courtyard. His shirt had a small ink mark by the pocket.

He studied Minh's dressings, then pulled up the chair.

“Your grandfather asked whether I'd broken my hands,” Minh told Mai.

“My father asked whether the doctors thought you had,” Hòa said. “He knows better than to get a medical opinion from you.”

Mai took the clean blouse from the bag. Beneath it lay food wrapped for two people and the right size of sandals for her swollen foot. Hòa had not called back to ask what she needed.

Outside, one wheel of a loaded trolley slipped from its bracket. The frame dropped toward the doorway. Hòa reached behind him without standing and caught the lower rail between two fingers. Bottles clicked together on the top shelf. Nothing fell.

The porter froze with both hands on the handles.

“Hold the other end level,” Hòa said.

The man did. Hòa raised the corner until the failed wheel cleared the floor, then waited while a second porter brought a wooden block. Minh watched the tendons in his father-in-law's hand. They barely changed. When the trolley was supported, Hòa let go and returned to opening the food.

“Don't start,” he told Minh.

“I haven't said anything.”

“You have my daughter's expression when she is about to.”

Mai began to laugh, then stopped because it pulled at the dressing above her ankle. Hòa looked at that dressing, and the laughter left the room without anyone being asked.

“They treated it?” he said.

“Yes.”

“Properly?”

“Yes, Dad.”

He nodded once. Minh was glad, with a clarity that had nothing to do with the number of men outside, that Khang was already behind a guarded door.

By seven, Duy was out of surgery. He was alive, still in danger, and would be questioned when the doctors allowed it. The investigation into the fire was being reopened. No one promised Minh anything about his own case.

Nhi came after Hồng telephoned her. She appeared in the doorway with a bag that might have contained clothes for Duy or the things she had taken when she left him. Minh could not tell. She had changed out of the shirt he remembered from the apartment. There was a mark on her wrist where she had been holding the handle too tightly.

Mai stood and went to her. They spoke quietly in the corridor. When Nhi came in, Hòa offered her his chair and went to find water.

“They told me I can't see him yet,” she said.

“He's alive,” Minh said.

“Everybody keeps saying that.”

He waited.

“I know why. I'm glad he is. I just need somebody to understand it doesn't answer the rest.”

“It doesn't.”

She looked at his bandages. “He said it on the recording?”

“Yes.”

“What he actually did?”

“Yes.”

Nhi rested the bag between her feet. For a moment she pressed both palms against her knees, holding herself in place.

“I thought if I left, he might finally be frightened of losing something he hadn't already spent.”

Minh did not know what to say to that. Duy had carried her name through every bargain without asking what it had begun to cost her. Minh had heard him do it and had sometimes mistaken the sound for love by itself.

“He didn't tell them where you were,” Minh said.

“He didn't know.”

“He asked us to keep it that way.”

Nhi looked up. That mattered to her. He saw it matter and did not try to use it for anything else.

“Good,” she said after a while. “One thing he can tell me without changing the sentence halfway through.”

She stood. Mai went with her toward the surgical ward. Minh watched them leave together. He had carried Duy as far as his own strength could take him. Nhi would decide what happened when the man could speak again, and there was no useful amount of pressure Minh could put on that door.

Mai came back carrying two small bowls of rice porridge. She set one in front of him and held the spoon because he could not. He opened his mouth. When he had swallowed, she filled the spoon again.

“The landlord called,” she said. “Her sister is safe.”

He closed his eyes.

“The shop?”

“Damaged. She wants you out.”

“She's right.”

“Yes.”

Mai gave him another spoonful. Through the open window came the noise of people starting work, metal shutters rising, engines catching, a vendor calling from the road. Minh listened while his wife fed him.

Bình arrived before they finished. One forearm had been bandaged. He carried a broken door handle in a plastic bag.

“A souvenir,” he said, putting it beside the clean clothes. “Under the bus.”

Minh looked at it. The handle had come from the van door he had kicked across the yard.

“The bus?” he asked.

“Still at my yard. Facing a different direction.”

“The frame can be straightened.”

“You've seen it?”

“I moved it.”

“Yes. I was there for the fucking miracle. Now I have a bus with a door that closes into next week.”

Minh almost smiled. “Front crossmember. It took more of the load than the side rail. Support it behind the spring mount before you pull it back.”

Bình took a pencil from his pocket. Mai found the back of an envelope. For the next several minutes the room held a different conversation. Minh asked where the bodywork had creased. Bình described the gap at the windscreen. Minh corrected his first estimate, told him which points to measure, and refused to give a final judgment until somebody actually measured them.

“You charge for this?” Bình asked.

“Usually.”

“Deduct it from the door.”

Mai set the spoon down. “The door belongs on a list. So does the van.”

“I know,” Minh said.

“And my room. And the adjoining wall.”

“I know.”

“Then we will make the list after you can hold a pencil. I am not spending this morning adding it for you.”

Bình folded the envelope. He no longer looked amused.

“The maintenance work is still there,” he said. “When you're able. If you're able to be there. I can wait for an answer.”

Minh looked at him.

“I can't give you a date.”

“I asked about the work, not a prophecy.”

“I want it.”

“Then don't hire somebody who thinks the accelerator fixes everything.”

After he left, Mai told Minh that Phước had called the shop, found no answer, and then called Lan as she had instructed him. The apprentice wanted to know whether his second day had been cancelled.

“Tell him yes.”

“I did.”

“And pay him for yesterday.”

“I did that too.”

“What did he say?”

“That he still wanted to learn. I told him wanting and waiting are different decisions, and he should make them with his eyes open.”

Minh pictured the young man carefully laying out tools on a bench that was now broken or burned. There had been hours yesterday in which the future had looked like a small wage, a straightened axle, and somebody arriving early enough to sweep. He still wanted those hours. They would cost more than he had expected.

“And your business?” he asked.

Mai looked toward the door through which Nhi had gone.

“Lan is giving me space until I can find another room. The press wasn't there. Some finished work was. I have customers to call, paper to replace, and a husband who apparently removes walls without requesting a quotation.”

“The quotation would have been high.”

“The quotation would have included a door.”

He laughed, and the movement pulled at his arm hard enough to end it. Mai watched his face until the pain settled. Then she picked up the spoon again.

“I can help with the calls,” he said.

“You can eat.”

He opened his mouth.

When the bowl was empty, she put it aside and sat close enough that their knees touched. Neither tried to turn the morning into a promise. Outside, the hospital continued admitting people whose emergencies had nothing to do with them. Hòa returned with water. Somewhere down the corridor, Nhi was allowed through a door.

Minh leaned his head against the wall. He could hear the damaged trolley again, the wooden block removed, a different wheel now taking its weight. Someone had found the part. Someone had done the ordinary work of fitting it.

Then the investigator returned and stood at the door.

This time Minh asked him to come in.

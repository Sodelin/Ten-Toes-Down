# Book II: The Man at the Door

## Chapter 1: Door Money

Calvin Rook offered Minh a hundred thousand dollars to attend a party.

Minh asked whether he had to clap.

“You have to stand by the door,” Rook said.

“For how long?”

“Until midnight.”

“Which side?”

There was a pause on the telephone. In the shop, Mai held up two paper samples. Minh pointed at the cream one. She shook her head, turned both over, and showed him the prices.

He pointed at the cream one again.

Rook cleared his throat. “The inside.”

“Open or closed?”

“Open.”

“You can buy something for that. Little rubber thing. Goes under it.”

Mai covered her mouth with the samples.

“I know what a doorstop is, Minh.”

“You offered me a hundred thousand dollars. I wanted to make sure.”

“People are nervous. There will be substantial money in the room. Your presence will reassure them.”

“And the people outside?”

“You'll reassure them too.”

Minh looked at the carburetor on his bench. Three screws lay beside it. He had found the fourth inside a customer's air filter and charged the man for an hour he had spent looking for a problem the man had created with his own screwdriver.

“Cash,” Minh said.

“Naturally.”

“When I arrive.”

“Naturally.”

“My friend comes.”

“Which friend?”

“The one driving me home with a hundred thousand dollars.”

“Fine.”

“He eats.”

“It's a banquet.”

“You haven't seen him eat.”

Rook gave him an address in the harbor district. Minh wrote it on the back of an envelope. When he hung up, Mai took the envelope and added milk, laundry soap, and scallions beneath the address.

“We have scallions.”

“We have a jar of water where scallions used to live.”

“A hundred thousand.”

“Congratulations. Get the large soap.”

He caught her around the waist. She put one hand on his chest to keep the grease off her blouse, then allowed him to pull her closer.

“Cream paper,” he said.

“I already ordered it.”

“You asked me.”

“I enjoy our conversations.”

He kissed her. She kissed him back, then held his chin between two fingers.

“Eighteen days.”

“I know when I'm getting married.”

“You thought the rehearsal was the wedding last week.”

“Your father wore a tie.”

“He had a meeting.”

“Your father wears a tie when somebody is about to lose something.”

Mai smiled. “Then come home with the money.”

***

Leon Price arrived in a white suit and a borrowed stretch limousine.

The limousine occupied the space in front of Minh's garage and part of the bakery next door. Its rear tire rested on the curb. Leon leaned out of the driver's window wearing sunglasses despite the sun having disappeared behind the warehouse roofs.

“Get in, nigga. We employed.”

Minh looked at the car.

“Who died?”

“Nobody yet. Why you making this negative?”

“You said you'd bring a car.”

“And I brought enough for both of us.”

Mai came outside carrying Minh's jacket. She stopped at the limousine and looked through its open window. Leon's white trousers matched the seats. A little gold chain crossed the front of his vest.

“Leon.”

“Miss Mai.”

“Does the owner know you have this?”

“He knows somebody has it.”

She waited.

“I'm bringing it back before his daughter's breakfast.”

“Why does she need a limousine for breakfast?”

“She's marrying a dentist. We all suffering.”

Minh took the jacket. It was the dark one Mai liked, with a tear in the lining he had promised to repair. Under it he wore his cleanest work shirt. His trousers had a pale mark where a customer had knocked a can of primer against him. He had scrubbed it twice. The mark remained.

Leon watched him approach.

“You wearing that?”

“Rook hired me to stand.”

“You can stand handsome.”

Minh put his toolbox in the back.

Leon lowered his sunglasses. “Why the tools?”

“Door might stick.”

Mai laughed and shut the car door on both of them.

As Leon pulled away, two boys outside the bakery stopped arguing over a bicycle and stared at the limousine. A woman from the apartments raised a camera. Leon slowed enough to give her his good side.

“You going to the party?” she called.

“We the party,” Leon said.

Minh opened the envelope again.

“We also need scallions.”

***

Rook had rented the entire Hotel Meridian and placed a red carpet over the service entrance.

The hotel's main entrance faced the bay. Limousines stood along it beneath hanging lights. Men in black suits collected car keys and pretended not to see the guns some of their customers wore badly. A woman in a silver dress directed traffic with two fingers and an expression that made the drivers hurry.

Leon drove past all of them.

“Service entrance?” he said.

“That's the address.”

“Nigga, we got a limousine. Service can come outside.”

The silver dress saw them. She lifted one hand, started toward the car, and recognized Minh through the glass.

Her hand went higher.

“Mr. Trần!”

Three valets turned. A man beside the front steps looked over, stopped lighting his cigarette, and let it fall from his mouth.

Leon smiled at the windshield.

“There he is. They found the correct entrance.”

The woman opened Minh's door herself. Behind her, a valet rolled a luggage cart toward the toolbox.

“Should I take that up?”

“It has oil on the bottom.”

The valet took his own jacket off and laid it across the cart.

Leon got out slowly. “Damn. I should've brought something dirty.”

The man who had dropped the cigarette came forward with both hands visible. Minh remembered him from a pool hall on Carson Avenue. He had once tried to collect money from a bartender using a crowbar. Minh had bent the crowbar around a parking meter while the man still held it. They had stood together for eleven minutes waiting for someone to bring a saw.

“Good to see you,” the man said.

“You get the meter fixed?”

“Yes.”

“City charge you?”

“Yes.”

Leon leaned closer. “He asking whether you learned, nigga.”

“I learned.”

Minh patted his shoulder and went inside.

The hotel had covered its lobby fountain with a glass platform. A white sports car stood on top, its hood raised to display an engine plated in gold. Beside it, a sign announced that the car belonged to the evening's prize convoy. Six women in matching black dresses held trays of champagne. A seventh held a tray with a single cigar.

Leon took the cigar.

“Sir, that's for—”

He pointed at Minh.

“His friend.”

The woman looked at Minh, then at the empty tray.

“Of course.”

“Don't light it in here,” Minh said.

“I wasn't going to.”

Leon put away the lighter he had already opened.

Rook waited by the elevators with a silver briefcase between his shoes. He was sixty, broad across the shoulders, with his white hair combed straight back. His dinner jacket fitted so precisely that Minh wondered whether somebody followed him around making adjustments.

Rook smiled without opening his mouth.

“You're early.”

“You said cash on arrival.”

The smile opened by a fraction.

He lifted the briefcase and handed it over. Minh set it on the reception counter and opened it. Bundles of hundred-dollar bills filled both sides.

Leon took his sunglasses off.

“We should arrive more often.”

Minh ran one thumb over the exposed edges, lifted two bundles, and put them back.

“A hundred thousand,” he said.

“You counted that?” Rook asked.

“You short?”

“No.”

“Then we agree.”

He closed the case and gave it to Leon.

Rook glanced at the toolbox arriving behind them. “Expecting trouble?”

“You hired me.”

“I hired a reassurance.”

Leon held up the briefcase. “Nigga, you purchased the deluxe package.”

Rook stared at him.

Leon stared back, chewing the unlit cigar.

The elevator opened.

***

The ballroom occupied the hotel's second floor. Its wide entrance faced a row of windows overlooking the loading court. Two steel fire doors had been concealed inside ornamental wooden frames. Minh noticed the fresh bolts in the tracks before he noticed the orchestra.

Then the orchestra stopped.

A trumpet held one embarrassed note after the rest of the band had gone quiet. The player lowered it. Two hundred people turned toward the entrance.

At the nearest table, a man swept a pile of betting slips into his jacket.

Across the room, another man stood so quickly that his chair fell backward.

“No,” he said. “Close it. Close the fucking line.”

His companion had a telephone against one ear. “He's here.”

The man beside the entrance began applauding. Three others joined him. A woman on the far side lifted her glass and shouted Minh's name. The applause spread through the tables until Rook's orchestra began playing again, louder than before.

Leon looked at Minh.

“What you do in here?”

“First visit.”

“Nigga, they having memories in advance.”

Rook raised his hands for quiet. It took longer than he liked.

“Ladies and gentlemen. As promised, our door will remain open throughout the evening. No purchaser will be detained. No disagreement will interfere with your departure.”

He put a hand toward Minh's shoulder, thought better of completing the gesture, and pointed instead.

“Mr. Trần will make certain of it.”

A woman near the stage called, “Can he make certain of the shrimp?”

Leon answered before Rook could.

“I got that department.”

The room laughed. Rook's mouth tightened.

Minh set his toolbox beside the right door and looked at the left. A small green light shone above its track. The light belonged to something newer than the hotel.

“Don't touch that,” a voice said.

The man approaching wore a sleeveless black shirt, leather trousers, and a championship belt over one shoulder. His arms were covered with tattoos of flames. A camera crew followed him. On a low platform behind the crew stood a custom motorcycle with an enormous front wheel and polished silver pipes.

A poster above the motorcycle showed the same man smashing a concrete block.

SƠN THE UNBROKEN.

Minh looked from the poster to the man.

“You bring your own?”

Sơn stopped. “My own what?”

“Compliment.”

Leon turned away with the cigar between his teeth.

Sơn looked at the mark on Minh's trousers.

“Workers use the rear stairs.”

“Then use them.”

The nearest table went silent.

Sơn shifted the championship belt higher. “You know who I am?”

Minh pointed at the poster.

“You prepared that question.”

The cameraman made a small sound behind his equipment.

Rook stepped forward. “Sơn. Mr. Trần is my guest.”

“Your guest brought a fucking toolbox.”

“You brought a motorcycle,” Minh said. “You having trouble getting home?”

Leon bent over the back of a chair. The woman beside him moved her champagne out of the way.

Sơn handed his belt to a member of the camera crew. He unrolled his shoulders. The people around the entrance began moving their chairs, creating a clear space without being asked.

“I don't want to embarrass you,” Sơn said.

Minh took off the jacket Mai had brought him. He folded it and set it on the toolbox.

“Then help yourself out.”

Sơn hit him in the chest.

The sound cut through the orchestra. A button flew from Minh's shirt, crossed the space between them, and struck Sơn's cameraman in the forehead.

Minh looked down at the open collar.

The cameraman lowered his equipment with one hand pressed above his eyebrow.

Sơn pulled his fist back. His knuckles had already begun to swell.

“Again?” Minh asked.

Sơn kicked.

His right foot rose toward Minh's face. Minh caught the ankle with one hand, stepped sideways, and lifted. Sơn's left foot left the carpet. His arms swung out. Minh turned once, carrying him horizontally at waist height, and walked the three steps to the display platform.

“That's a hundred and forty thousand dollars!” somebody shouted.

Minh sat Sơn on the motorcycle.

The seat went through the frame. Both wheels jumped off the platform. The front fork folded backward until the tire pressed against Sơn's chest. The rear wheel rose behind his head. Minh pushed the handlebars down over his shoulders and let go.

Sơn sat inside the motorcycle with his knees near his ears.

One silver pipe detached, rolled across the platform, and fell onto the carpet.

The orchestra's drummer hit a cymbal by mistake.

Leon pointed at him. “Keep that nigga. He understands the assignment.”

People stood. Somebody began clapping again. Sơn's cameraman raised the camera with his good hand and moved three feet closer.

“Turn that off,” Sơn said.

“You're under contract,” the cameraman answered.

Minh picked up the championship belt and hung it on the folded front wheel.

“There. Complete set.”

He returned to the doorway. Rook stood beside the toolbox, watching the men at the nearest table tear up their betting slips.

“Was that necessary?” Rook asked.

Minh held the two sides of his shirt together.

“You got a sewing kit?”

A woman pushed past Rook carrying one from her handbag. She had gray hair, diamond earrings, and a grin she made no attempt to hide.

“Sit down, baby. I got you.”

“He's working,” Rook said.

She pulled up a chair beside the door.

“Then his job got a chair.”

Minh sat. Across the ballroom, Leon opened the silver briefcase just far enough to look inside again. He closed it, patted the lid, and asked a waiter where the large plates were.

Rook's son appeared on the stage beneath the auction lights. Dante wore a red jacket and a headset. He watched the doorway while his father spoke to him through a small radio.

Minh lifted one arm so the woman could thread her needle through his shirt.

The green light above the door turned red.

Steel moved inside the frame.

Minh put one foot beneath the descending door.

It stopped against his shoe with a hard clang.

Onstage, Dante pressed his headset.

Minh smiled at him.

“I think your father paid for open.”

## Chapter 2: Open Until Midnight

The door tried Minh's shoe twice.

Each time, the motor above it whined, the steel panel shuddered, and the toe of his right shoe remained on the carpet. The woman sewing his shirt glanced down.

“You want to move that?”

“It's fine.”

“I mean your arm. You're pulling my thread.”

He lowered his elbow.

Across the entrance, the second steel panel struck the floor. Its bottom edge cut through the carpet and left a white stripe in the marble beneath. A man holding a champagne glass jumped back. Champagne went into the pocket of the man behind him.

“Motherfucker,” the second man said.

“I was pushed.”

“By a building?”

Leon came back from the buffet carrying two plates. He had balanced the silver briefcase beneath his left arm. A tail of lobster meat hung over one plate's edge.

“We leaving?”

“Apparently we're staying,” Minh said.

Leon looked at the doors, then at the buffet.

“I can work with that for twenty minutes.”

Rook had reached the stage. Dante stepped backward before his father touched him. Minh saw the red jacket turn, the headset come off, the two men leaning close enough to speak through their teeth.

The woman bit her thread.

“There.”

Minh looked down. She had replaced his lost button with a black one, slightly larger than the rest.

“Thank you.”

“Don't let that fool hit this one. It came off my husband's good coat.”

“Where's your husband?”

She pointed at the man whose pocket was full of champagne.

“Still talking about a coat he doesn't wear.”

Minh stood. The steel panel rose on his foot. He lifted it another six inches, slid his fingers beneath the edge, and took the weight in his right hand.

The woman carried her chair out of the way.

“I knew you was going to do something with that.”

The motor gave a long grinding cough. Two bolts fell from the housing and bounced down the panel. Minh lifted until the bottom cleared his head, then pushed harder. The top of the panel drove through its cover. A length of broken track came loose and struck the floor beside him.

People began applauding.

He looked over his shoulder.

“Anybody leaving?”

Six hands rose. Then fifteen. A woman in blue took her husband's wrist and began pulling him toward the gap.

“We haven't bid,” he protested.

“We haven't been buried either. Keep moving.”

A guard came down the near aisle with one hand beneath his jacket.

“Everyone remains in the room until Mr. Rook—”

Minh pulled the steel panel out of its track.

The last bolts tore free in a short burst. The panel tilted toward the guard. Minh caught its top edge, turned it flat, and set it against the closed half of the entrance. The guard had stopped speaking. His hand remained beneath his jacket.

Leon set one plate on a table.

“You want to complete that sentence, nigga?”

The guard took his hand out.

“I was instructed—”

“We all been instructed. My doctor said vegetables. Look at my plate.”

The woman in blue passed between Minh and the guard. Her husband followed with his auction paddle still in his hand. She took it from him and dropped it into a champagne bucket on the way out.

More guests came. Minh stood beside the gap, nodding to the people who thanked him and accepting a napkin from one who mistook the grease on his hand for blood.

At the back of the ballroom, somebody shouted that the money was gone.

The departing line stopped.

“Which money?” Leon asked.

He moved the briefcase from beneath his arm to between his feet.

Three men pushed through the stage curtains. One had a bleeding nose. Another carried the lid of a wooden display box. The third wore a security vest with the front unfastened.

“The convoy,” the man in the vest said.

Rook looked at his son.

Dante's face had gone pale beneath the auction lights.

The man with the display-box lid held it up. A red velvet cushion was fixed inside it. The cushion had the outline of a necklace and nothing else.

“The prize lots. The cars. Everything staged downstairs.”

The man whose pocket had been filled with champagne began emptying it onto the carpet.

“I bought the necklace.”

“You haven't paid,” his wife said.

“I was going to.”

“Then enjoy being early for once.”

Two guards went to the service door behind the stage. It opened inward before either reached it. A man in a hotel vest came through backward, shoved hard enough to knock both guards into the curtain.

Behind him stood another man carrying a shotgun.

“Everybody down.”

Leon picked up his second plate.

“Now we leaving.”

The shotgun fired into the ceiling. Plaster rained across the tables. A light fitting dropped on a platter of fruit and rolled into a woman's lap. She threw it at her husband, who threw it at the floor.

Minh moved before the gunman brought the barrel level again.

The nearest table stood between him and the stage. Eight people sat around it. He put both hands on the back of an empty chair, jumped, and carried it over the table. His feet cleared a man's head by a foot. The man ducked afterward, taking his wig off with both hands.

Minh landed on the stage steps with the chair in front of him.

The gunman fired.

The shot broke the chair's back. Minh drove the remaining frame over the barrel, twisted it downward, and kicked the gunman in the stomach.

The man went through the curtain. A drum crashed behind it. The band stopped again, this time all at once.

Minh pulled the shotgun out of the chair, bent its barrel against the metal stair rail, and dropped it.

The drummer emerged with one stick in his hand.

“That's my bass drum.”

Minh pointed at Rook.

“He hired the room.”

“I hired music,” Rook snapped.

“You got percussion.”

Two more men were coming up the service stairs. Minh could see them through the open doorway: black jackets, gloves, one shotgun and one heavy pistol. The lower man raised his weapon around his partner's shoulder.

Minh caught the top of the service door and pulled it toward himself.

Its hinges came out of the wall. He turned the door on its long edge and drove it into the stairwell like a shovel.

The top man struck it chest-first. His gun went off into the concrete ceiling. The door hit the man behind him, knocked his pistol against the rail, and carried both down five steps. Their legs tangled beneath the wooden panel.

Minh went after them.

The man with the shotgun tried to lift the door. Minh put one foot on it. The bottom man screamed something muffled into his partner's jacket.

“Drop the guns.”

The pistol fell first. The shotgun followed.

Minh picked them up and set them on the landing behind him. He bent the stair rail across the door, pinning it over both men. Then he went back into the ballroom.

Leon stood in the entrance with the briefcase in one hand and Minh's jacket in the other.

“Time?” he asked.

Minh looked at the ballroom clock.

“Ten twelve.”

“Contract?”

“Midnight.”

Leon looked at the gap where the steel panel had been.

“Nigga, that motherfucker staying open until Christmas. Come on.”

***

Rook caught them in the corridor.

He had brought six men. None came closer than the length of the steel panel leaning against the wall.

“My son says your people were downstairs.”

Minh looked at Leon.

Leon lifted the plate he had retrieved.

“My people was at the butter.”

“He means other people,” Rook said.

“Then he needs another description.”

Dante came through the ballroom behind his father. He had taken off the red jacket. Sweat darkened the collar of his shirt.

“They said they came with you,” he told Minh.

“Who?”

“The loading crew.”

“Names.”

“They didn't give names.”

Minh looked at Rook. “You let men take your prize convoy because they said my name?”

Rook's jaw moved.

Leon put the plate down.

“How many times can a nigga use that? Asking for employment.”

Dante stepped toward him. Minh put one finger against Dante's chest. The young man stopped with his weight still coming forward, looked down at the finger, and moved backward again.

“I asked you who,” Minh said.

“A driver. Short hair. Brown jacket.”

“There's a city outside.”

“He knew you.”

“Apparently so do you. You're doing a terrible job with it.”

The corridor windows overlooked the loading court. Minh went to the nearest one. Below, hotel workers stood around three empty parking spaces. An armored truck waited at the far end with its rear doors open. Two guards sat on the ground beside it, their hands tied behind them.

At the court's exit, a dark sedan turned into the street.

The driver looked back through his open window.

Duy.

Minh put his palm against the glass.

The sedan disappeared around the corner.

Rook joined him. “What?”

“Your loading court needs a gate.”

“Don't make jokes.”

Minh turned toward him.

“You paid me to keep your ballroom door open. It's open. Those people tried to close it. I stopped them. You have a missing convoy and a son who hired men without asking their names.”

Dante opened his mouth.

Minh pointed at him without looking.

“Finish breathing first.”

The young man shut his mouth.

Rook moved closer to Minh. He lowered his voice.

“Twelve million dollars in cash, diamonds, and vehicles.”

Leon picked up the plate again.

“Damn. You should've hired him for outside too.”

Rook ignored him with effort.

“I want it back.”

“Then get it.”

“I'm asking you.”

“You were accusing me.”

“I'm revising my request.”

A small man in a cream dinner jacket came out of the ballroom carrying two auction paddles. Minh recognized Bách Vũ before he saw the diamond pinkie ring. Everybody on Bell Street called him Uncle Dollar. Bách had started the name himself and recently begun correcting people who forgot the second word.

He looked at the torn door, the six guards, and Rook's face.

Then he smiled at Minh.

“I had money on the motorcycle.”

“You lost.”

“I switched when I saw your shoes.”

Leon nodded. “Educated investor.”

Bách tapped one auction paddle against his palm.

“Calvin, how much are you offering?”

“This is private.”

Bách looked behind him. Half the ballroom had followed the conversation into the corridor.

“Then your privacy has witnesses.”

Rook looked at Minh.

“Two hundred thousand.”

“For twelve million?” Bách said.

“For recovery.”

“And the man who stole it gets what? Eleven point eight? I want the other job.”

Somebody laughed behind him. The man in the wet dinner jacket raised his empty champagne glass.

“Pay him a million.”

“It's not your money,” Rook said.

“That was my necklace.”

His wife touched his arm. “You still haven't paid.”

“I'm participating.”

Bách raised one auction paddle.

“A million.”

Rook stared at him.

Bách raised the second paddle. “A million from this side of the room too. Very strong demand.”

Minh watched Rook decide which humiliation he could afford.

“One million,” Rook said. “On return of the convoy.”

“Everything you listed,” Minh said.

“Yes.”

“And nobody follows Mai.”

Rook's expression changed.

“I haven't mentioned your fiancée.”

“Now you don't have to.”

Minh held out his hand. Rook took it carefully.

Bách began applauding. The corridor joined him. Leon put the plate beneath one arm and applauded with the briefcase against his palm.

Minh let go before Rook's fingers started to make noise.

“I'll call you.”

***

Downstairs, three valets were waiting with the limousine. One had polished the toolbox. Another held Minh's jacket as if it belonged in a museum.

Leon slid into the driver's seat and waited until Minh shut the rear door.

“You saw somebody.”

Minh looked up at the ballroom windows.

“Duy.”

“Shit.”

“Driving out.”

“He see you?”

“Yes.”

Leon put the car in gear. For the first block, he said nothing. The limousine rolled past the harbor gates, its white body reflected in the dark shop windows.

Then he glanced into the mirror.

“We still getting groceries?”

Minh took out the envelope.

“Large soap.”

“Nigga, we got a hundred thousand dollars.”

“Then we can afford it.”

At the all-night market, Leon parked across three spaces and guarded the briefcase while Minh went inside. The cashier looked at the soap, the scallions, the milk, and the hundred-dollar bill.

“Got anything smaller?”

Minh opened his wallet. Nothing.

He looked back through the window at Leon, who was pretending not to see him.

“No.”

The cashier pressed the intercom button.

“Manager to register two. Again.”

Minh stood beside the groceries and waited.

## Chapter 3: The House Has Rules

Mai opened the apartment door wearing one of Minh's shirts and holding a hammer.

He looked at the hammer.

“You heard?”

“The cupboard came loose.”

“Oh.”

“What should I have heard?”

Leon appeared behind him with the briefcase and the grocery bags.

“Your fiancé got promoted.”

“From what?”

“Standing.”

Mai moved aside. Leon went straight to the kitchen and put the milk in the refrigerator. He arranged the scallions in the empty jar, set the soap beside the sink, and placed the briefcase on the table with a flourish.

“All departments complete.”

Mai opened the case.

She looked at the money for several seconds. Then she took the hammer out of her hand and laid it carefully on the counter.

“That much standing?”

“I sat for some of it,” Minh said.

Leon pointed at the new shirt button. “Medical break.”

Mai touched the button. “Who sewed this?”

“A married woman.”

“That answers a question I hadn't asked yet.”

“Her husband was there.”

“Was he sewing too?”

Leon opened a cupboard and took down three cups. “I'm going to let y'all finish the investigation while I make tea.”

Minh sat. Mai remained standing over the money, one knee against his. He described the ballroom, the door, the motorcycle. Leon interrupted twice to improve the sound of the motorcycle and once to demonstrate the expression on Rook's face.

Mai laughed at that. She stopped laughing when Minh said Duy's name.

“Driving?”

“Leaving the court.”

“Maybe he was hired.”

“He looked back.”

“People look back.”

“He saw me and went faster.”

Leon put a cup in front of her.

“Duy owes people. Last week he tried selling me a watch he was still making payments on.”

“You bought it?” Mai asked.

Leon looked offended.

She looked at his wrist.

He pulled his sleeve down.

“I got a very good price.”

Minh took out his telephone and called Duy. It rang until a recorded voice offered to take a message.

He called again.

Mai sat beside him. “Don't leave a threat.”

“I wasn't.”

The recorded voice returned.

Minh waited for the tone.

“Duy. Call me.”

He hung up.

Leon nodded. “Terrifying.”

Mai closed the briefcase and slid it farther from the tea.

“Nobody comes upstairs with a gun,” she said.

“They'd have to come through the shop.”

“Then put that in your advertisement. Seventeen days until our wedding. Ground floor available for stupidity.”

Leon raised his cup.

“I want the table near the food.”

“You have a table near the food.”

“How near?”

“Leon.”

“I'm bringing a gift. Distance should reflect commitment.”

***

At eight the next morning, somebody delivered a refrigerator.

Minh heard the truck backing toward the garage and looked down from the apartment window. Two men were lowering a large silver refrigerator onto the sidewalk. A red bow covered most of its top door.

“Wrong address,” he called.

One man checked a card.

“Trần?”

“Yes.”

“Correct address.”

“We have one.”

“Now you have options.”

Mai came to the window. Behind the refrigerator truck, another vehicle stopped. Its driver climbed out carrying a basket of fruit large enough to hide a person. A third vehicle followed with a washing machine strapped into its open bed.

Mai watched the washing machine arrive.

“I should have written more on the grocery list.”

Minh went downstairs barefoot. The man beside the refrigerator handed him a cream envelope embossed with a dollar sign.

INSURANCE AGAINST WARM CHAMPAGNE.

UNCLE DOLLAR.

The washing machine had come from the woman who sewed his button. Her husband had written the card.

PLEASE STOP ACCEPTING BUTTONS FROM MY COAT.

The fruit basket contained a business card from an auction guest Minh did not recognize and three thousand dollars in an envelope labeled TRAVEL EXPENSES.

Mai lifted the envelope.

“Where did he want you to travel?”

“Away from him, probably.”

Reggie Mills arrived carrying a video camera and wearing a jacket with EASTBRIDGE COMMUNITY ACCESS sewn across the back. The final S had come loose and hung by one thread.

“Don't move,” he said.

Minh looked at the refrigerator blocking his shop entrance.

“That part's easy.”

Reggie framed the shot through his camera.

“Local man receives congratulations from business community.”

“Put that down,” Mai said.

“Local woman rejects free publicity.”

She walked toward him.

The camera lowered.

“I'm documenting prosperity, Mai.”

“Document the men scratching my doorframe.”

Reggie turned immediately.

“Sir. On camera. Was the damage intentional?”

The refrigerator deliveryman stopped pulling.

“Man, get that thing out my face.”

Minh lifted the refrigerator off the dolly. The red bow brushed his cheek. He turned sideways, carried the appliance through the garage, and set it by the rear wall. The two deliverymen stared at the empty dolly.

Reggie swung his camera back too late.

“Do that again.”

“Pay me.”

“Nigga, you just got a refrigerator.”

CUSTOMER ASSISTED.

He signed.

***

Hòa arrived while the fruit was being redistributed.

“Your father-in-law scares me.”

***

The Lê house stood three blocks from the garage, behind a short brick wall and a gate Tín had replaced twice because visitors kept reversing into it. Minh had offered to move the driveway. Tín had suggested improving the visitors.

By noon, seven cars occupied the curb. Leon's borrowed limousine was among them. Its owner sat on the porch beside him, eating from a bowl and wearing the expression of a man who had begun the morning angry and encountered food.

“He had a wedding,” Minh said.

Leon pointed at the owner.

“It got delayed.”

The man swallowed. “Dentist's mother locked herself in the bathroom.”

“Why?”

“She wanted a different photographer.”

Leon leaned closer to Minh.

“I told him you charge for doors now.”

Minh went inside before the owner could ask.

Hương stood at the stove with Mai beside her. Bích sat at the kitchen table sorting herbs into a basket. She looked up when Minh entered and beckoned him close enough to inspect the new button.

“Who did this?”

Bích turned toward Mai.

“Good stitch.”

“Everybody has inspected it,” Minh said.

“Then somebody should have told you it doesn't match.”

She returned to the herbs.

Tín sat in the living room with one slipper off and a newspaper folded on his knee. At seventy-eight, he had retained a back so straight that Minh sometimes found himself sitting straighter before the old man looked at him.

Today he was watching a delivery crew maneuver a safe through the front door.

“Mine?” Minh asked.

“Unless you've started sending me presents with your name on them.”

The safe was black, waist-high, and topped with another red bow. A card hung from its handle.

FOR YOUR NEW RESPONSIBILITIES.

Minh picked it up. No signature.

The deliverymen had caught one wheel of their dolly against the sill. Minh took the safe off it and held the weight against his chest. He turned toward the living room.

Tín extended one finger.

“There.”

“Where?”

The old man's finger touched the safe's underside.

Minh's arms rose.

The safe rose with them. Tín lifted it six inches, then another foot. Minh let go before his shoulders followed it any higher.

The old man held the safe on one finger while examining the scuff it had left on the doorframe.

“They charge for delivery?”

One deliveryman shook his head.

“Good.”

Tín lowered the safe onto the rug beside his chair. He did not get up. The floor gave a short creak.

Leon came in from the porch, saw Minh standing over the safe, and looked at Tín.

“You did it again?”

Tín put his slipper back on.

“Nigga brought it into my house.”

Leon laughed so hard the limousine owner appeared behind him to see what had happened.

Minh rubbed his palms together and looked toward the kitchen. Mai stood in the doorway with both hands over her smile.

“Don't,” he said.

“I'm being very quiet.”

“Your whole face is loud.”

***

Lunch covered the dining table, the counter, and the top of a cabinet nobody was supposed to put hot things on. Hương moved a dish from the cabinet herself and warned everyone else against doing it.

Tín took the chair at the end of the table. Bích sat beside him. Hòa waited until Mai had a seat, then pulled out the remaining chair with his foot and sat without looking behind him.

Minh told them about Rook.

Tín continued eating until he heard the name.

“Calvin?”

“Yes.”

“Yes.”

“Must have dried.”

Leon stopped with his spoon halfway to his mouth.

“Eight years, Uncle.”

“Some men take longer.”

Hòa reached for the rice. His sleeve touched the glass of water beside Minh's elbow. Minh moved to catch it as it tipped. Hòa's other hand was already beneath the glass, two fingers holding its base level.

No water reached the table.

He set it down and continued serving himself.

Tín looked at his son. Hòa looked back.

Mai nudged Minh's ankle beneath the table before he could ask anything.

“Eat.”

He ate.

Hương asked whether the wedding band had been paid. Mai said yes. Bích asked whether the photographer was reliable. Leon's guest made a strained sound and became interested in his bowl.

Tín asked Minh how much Rook had paid him.

“A hundred thousand.”

“For what?”

“Keeping a door open.”

The old man put his spoon down.

“Hòa.”

“I heard.”

“We've been doing that for free.”

Bích tapped Tín's wrist with her spoon.

“You charge people enough for coming in.”

The table broke into laughter. Minh leaned back beside Mai and let himself laugh too. Under the table, she found his hand and squeezed it.

Somebody knocked at the front door.

Leon looked toward the hall.

“That cost anything?”

“Depends what they want,” Tín said.

Reggie came in holding a stack of flyers.

He had one taped across the front of his camera. In thick black letters, it announced a celebration the following morning on Bell Street.

WELCOME YOUR NEW KING.

Below that was a photograph of Minh lifting an engine, cropped so his expression looked furious rather than bothered by sun in his eyes.

Hòa took one flyer. Mai took another.

Minh read the bottom line.

ALL DEBTS, DISPUTES, AND DONATIONS ACCEPTED.

“Nigga, you got office hours.”

## Chapter 4: Congratulations, You Owe Everybody

The red carpet outside Minh's garage ended at the liquor store.

Somebody had run it across the sidewalk without moving the trash cans. Two cans stood on it with their lids polished. A third had a bow. At the shop entrance, a man from a party-rental company was inflating gold balloons beside a banner that read LONG MAY HE REPAIR.

Mai stood beneath the banner with a cup of coffee.

“That one I like,” she said.

Minh looked down the carpet. “Who paid?”

“Everybody claims somebody else.”

Leon appeared from the liquor store carrying a crate of sparkling cider.

“Nine in the morning,” Minh said.

“Coronation got hours?”

“You put this together?”

“I improved an existing situation.”

Behind Leon, Reggie walked backward with his camera trained on a group of women arriving from the bakery. Each carried a tray. A man in a paper crown followed them with a portable keyboard tucked beneath one arm.

Minh recognized the keyboard before the man.

“That's Duy's.”

The man stopped.

“He sold it.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

“To you?”

“To my cousin. I borrowed it.”

Leon took the crate into the garage. “This street got more cousins than people.”

Minh went to the keyboard player. The man's paper crown advertised a hamburger special. He had folded the restaurant's name inward, leaving a printed picture of fries above his ear.

“What did your cousin pay?”

“Two hundred.”

“Did Duy need cash?”

“He didn't ask for a piano in exchange.”

Mai laughed into her coffee.

Minh took out his telephone and called Duy again. Straight to the recorded voice. He closed the phone and returned it to his pocket.

The keyboard player set up beneath the banner. His first chord came out much louder than he expected. A dog barked from an upstairs window.

“Sound check,” the player said.

The dog barked again.

“Everybody a critic.”

***

By ten, the sidewalk held forty people and six gifts Minh had not found room for inside.

A furniture dealer sent a leather sofa long enough to block both motorcycle bays. A barber sent a year's free haircuts and a framed photograph of himself holding the scissors. The woman who ran the corner market brought the change Minh had left waiting for the previous night.

“Manager didn't come?” he asked.

“Manager came. You went to the soap aisle and picked up the whole box.”

“It was faster.”

“My cashier thinks you're opening a laundromat.”

Mai accepted the envelope of change. “We have the machine.”

The woman looked at the washing machine by the back wall.

“Congratulations.”

Then Bách arrived in a pale yellow Rolls-Royce.

Two men got out before he did. One carried flowers. The other carried a cake shaped like a garage. The cake's tiny front door stood open. A sugar figure of Minh occupied the doorway wearing a gold crown and holding what appeared to be a wrench the size of a tree.

Leon studied it.

“They got the shoulders.”

Mai leaned closer. “They made you taller.”

“How much taller?” Minh asked.

“Tasteful amount.”

Bách handed Minh the Rolls-Royce keys.

The conversation around them stopped.

Minh looked at the car, then at the keys.

“For me?”

“For the wedding.”

“To keep?”

“You plan to get married temporarily?”

Leon made a small reverent noise.

Mai held out her hand. Minh passed her the keys. She went straight to the driver's door.

Bách watched her sit behind the wheel.

“She drives?”

Minh smiled at him.

“You about to find out.”

The engine started. Mai adjusted the mirror, lifted one hand through the window, and drove the car twenty feet forward into an empty space before somebody else's gift truck took it.

She got out and locked it.

“Thank you,” she told Bách. “The seat needs fixing.”

“I can have someone—”

She pointed at Minh.

“I have someone.”

Bách laughed and clapped Minh on the back. His hand stung. He concealed the sting by reaching immediately for a slice of cake.

“Now,” he said, “about the flyers.”

“You printed them?” Mai asked.

“No.”

“You distributed them?”

Bách considered his cake.

“Some.”

“How many?”

“Enough to support the occasion.”

She stepped closer.

“Bách.”

“Two thousand.”

Leon whistled. “Strong occasion.”

Minh took a flyer off the stack Reggie had placed by the entrance.

“Who printed them?”

“They arrived at my restaurant before sunrise. I assumed you had made an announcement.”

Mai folded the flyer once. She rubbed the ink with her thumb and smelled the paper.

“Not mine.”

“We know,” Leon said. “Yours would've spelled donations correctly.”

Minh looked down.

DONATIONS had become DONATONS.

“That sounds like a large animal,” Reggie said.

“Put the camera down,” Mai told him.

He lowered it six inches.

“It's still recording,” she said.

“History keeps happening.”

***

A man with a shaved head approached Minh carrying a folded envelope. He wore a mechanic's shirt from a garage across town.

“Mr. Trần.”

“Minh.”

“I need a favor.”

Leon appeared beside them with a fresh slice of cake.

“We open.”

The mechanic looked at him.

“You work here?”

“Emotionally.”

The man turned back to Minh. “There's a crew on Foster taking money from my drivers. I pay once. They come twice. I pay twice, now they asking about my wife's salon.”

He held out the envelope.

Minh did not take it yet.

“Who's asking?”

“A man called Al.”

“Big Al or Little Al?”

“Big.”

“He's little.”

The mechanic hesitated.

“The one with the dog.”

“That's Little Al. He's big.”

Leon pointed his fork at the man. “This why we need names on paper.”

Minh took out his telephone. He dialed a number from memory and waited.

“Al. You at Foster?”

A voice answered loudly enough for Leon to lean away.

“Stop charging the garage.”

The voice became quieter.

“Because I'm asking.”

Minh listened.

“No. You can keep the dog. I didn't mention the dog.”

He hung up and took the envelope. Inside lay five thousand dollars.

The mechanic looked astonished.

“That's it?”

“He'll call you.”

“And if he comes back?”

“Then I come with you.”

The man shook his hand with both of his. He left so quickly that he almost collided with the cake table.

Leon watched him go.

“You fixing cars and men now?”

Minh put the envelope in his jacket.

“Men keep bringing money.”

Bách raised his cup. “A diversified business.”

Mai folded her arms beneath the banner.

“The cars have appointments.”

“Then make appointments for the men.”

“Do I look like your receptionist?”

Minh held up the envelope.

She glanced at it.

“I charge separately.”

***

The flower van arrived while Leon was explaining why the cake figure should remain intact for future generations.

It was a dark green delivery van with white lettering and a sliding side door. The driver stopped beyond the Rolls-Royce. A man in a florist's apron got out carrying a tall rectangular arrangement of lilies.

Minh saw the stems move without the flowers moving.

He put his cake plate in Leon's hand.

“Down.”

The florist's right hand came out of the flowers holding a compact gun.

Minh threw the cake table at him.

The folding legs struck the man's chest. The cake went over his shoulder and burst against the van's windshield. He fell backward, firing through the tabletop as it landed across him.

People dropped behind the parked cars. Leon shoved Reggie beneath the Rolls-Royce's front bumper and lay across his legs.

Minh crossed the sidewalk in two steps. The gunman pushed the table up. Minh stamped it down and caught the gun barrel as it poked past the edge. He twisted it until the man's wrist turned with it.

The gun fell.

Minh pulled the table away, grabbed the man by his apron, and threw him into the van's open side door.

Another gunman had been kneeling inside. The florist hit him across the shoulders. Both went backward into a rack of buckets. Water and white petals spilled into the street.

The van's driver hit the accelerator.

Minh caught the rear bumper.

The van moved two feet. The bumper began to peel from its mounts. Minh stepped closer, slid his hands beneath the rear frame, and lifted.

Both rear wheels rose off the street.

The driver kept accelerating. Tires spun above Minh's head. The van's nose scraped the pavement, throwing sparks from beneath the front bumper. In the open side door, the gunmen slid forward through the water and struck the backs of the seats.

Minh held the van tilted on its front wheels.

“Turn it off.”

The engine screamed.

He lifted another foot.

The windshield cracked across the cake frosting.

“Turn it off.”

The engine stopped.

For a second the only sound was the keyboard, still holding a note because its player had fallen against it.

Leon raised his head from behind the Rolls-Royce.

“Let go of the music, nigga. We heard you.”

The note ended.

Minh lowered the van until its rear wheels touched the road. He opened the driver's door and took the keys. The driver stared at him with his mouth open.

“Who sent flowers?”

“I don't know.”

Minh looked at the cake across the windshield.

“You know something.”

“I was paid to drive.”

“By who?”

The man looked toward the open side door.

The florist tried to climb out. Mai stood beside the door holding a tire iron from the garage. She struck the metal step in front of his hand.

He pulled his hand back.

“There's a door charge,” she said.

He stayed inside.

Minh leaned closer to the driver.

“You see my fiancée?”

The driver nodded.

“She's having a good morning. Help her keep it.”

“Benny,” the driver said. “Benny at Crown Arcade. He paid me. That's all.”

Minh took the man's wallet, looked at the identification, and handed it back.

“Sit.”

The man gripped the steering wheel with both hands.

Reggie crawled out from beneath the Rolls-Royce.

His camera remained on his shoulder.

Leon stared at him.

“You got all that?”

“Most.”

“You got me pushing you to safety?”

“Your elbow covered the lens.”

Leon shut his eyes.

“Nigga, I risked my suit for a close-up of skin.”

***

Sergeant Ray Carter arrived without a siren. He parked behind the flower van, got out, and looked at its broken nose.

Then he looked at Minh.

“I was told shots fired.”

Minh pointed at the florist.

Carter looked at the table full of bullet holes.

“And a traffic accident.”

Leon brushed dirt from his trousers.

“Separate department.”

Carter had known Leon long enough to ignore him on the first attempt. He asked Mai whether anyone had been hurt. She showed him a small cut on Reggie's cheek. Reggie moved his camera so Carter could see the better side.

“I have footage.”

“Good.”

“Station can buy a copy.”

Carter turned toward him slowly.

Reggie nodded. “Public service discount.”

While the officers collected the guns, Minh walked to the Rolls-Royce. Mai joined him. Cake frosting covered its hood and half the windshield. The little sugar figure of Minh lay on its side beside the wiper.

Its crown had survived.

Mai picked it up and handed it to him.

“Long may you repair.”

He bit the crown off.

She stared at him, then began laughing.

Across the sidewalk, Leon found the mechanic from Foster waiting beside the garage. The man held another envelope.

“What's that?” Leon asked.

“Al called. Said to give him this too.”

Minh accepted the second envelope. Ten thousand dollars.

From behind him, Carter said, “I don't want to know.”

“Then stop looking over my shoulder,” Minh said.

Carter stepped beside him instead.

“I want a statement.”

Minh looked at the flower van, then at the clock above his workbench.

“You want it now?”

“Yes.”

He put the envelope away.

“Leon. Call Crown Arcade. Tell Benny I'll be late.”

Leon took out his phone.

“How late?”

Minh glanced at Carter's notebook.

“Depends how fast he writes.”

## Chapter 5: The Arcade King

Benny had put a sign on Crown Arcade's front door.

CLOSED FOR PRIVATE MAINTENANCE.

Minh read it through the windshield of Leon's own car, a brown sedan with a blue passenger door.

“He got your message,” he said.

Leon switched off the engine.

“Told him you were coming. He said he wasn't there.”

“On his own telephone?”

“Nigga answered enthusiastic. Had to become absent halfway through.”

Minh opened his door. Before he could get out, a young man appeared beside the car wearing red trainers, loose black trousers, and a shirt that read MASTER CHIGGA in gold letters.

The young man leaned down to the window.

“You Minh?”

“Depends what your shirt wants.”

The man looked down at himself.

“It's a brand.”

Leon got out on the other side and came around the hood.

“Brand of what?”

“Me.”

“How sales?”

“Building.”

Minh looked at the arcade. Through the darkened glass, a shape moved away from the door.

“You work here?”

“I play pool here.”

“Name?”

The young man tapped the shirt.

“Before the shirt,” Minh said.

“Tú.”

“Tú. Move.”

Tú stepped aside so quickly he nearly tripped over the curb. Leon caught his elbow.

“Mastering sidewalks next?”

“Those shoes new?” Tú asked.

Leon looked down at the scuffed white shoes he had refused to change after the flower attack.

“They were.”

Tú nodded sympathetically. “Hard launch.”

Minh knocked on the arcade door.

Somebody inside held up a handwritten note.

READ THE SIGN.

Minh put his hand on the door handle and pulled.

The lock broke out of the frame. He caught the piece before it hit the sidewalk and handed it to the man with the note.

“Maintenance.”

***

The arcade smelled of fried food, warm electronics, and the carpet cleaner somebody had poured over a much older smell. Rows of machines flashed in the half-dark. Beyond them, a rope divided the games from a pool room lit by green-shaded lamps.

A woman behind the change counter looked up from a magazine.

“Benny isn't here.”

“Where is he?”

She turned a page.

“Ask the sign.”

Leon put both hands on the counter and smiled.

“Veronica.”

She looked at him over the magazine.

“You still owe me thirty dollars.”

Leon stopped smiling.

“Different conversation.”

“Same mouth.”

Minh placed thirty dollars on the counter.

Veronica picked it up.

“Office. Back left. He'll tell you he left.”

Leon followed Minh toward the rope.

“You didn't have to do that.”

“You didn't have to borrow it.”

“It was for a gift.”

“For her?”

Leon said nothing.

Minh looked at him.

“You borrowed money from her to buy her a gift?”

“She liked the earrings.”

Behind them, Tú made a noise that became a cough when Leon turned.

The pool room held three tables and eight men. On the nearest table, a triangle of balls waited at the far end. A man in a gold waistcoat stood beside it holding a cue. Minh recognized Benny from the driver's description of an enormous watch and a small, irritated head.

Benny smiled.

“Didn't think you'd come yourself.”

“You sent flowers.”

“That wasn't me.”

Minh took the cue from his hand.

Benny let it go after a moment in which both of them watched his fingers.

“I want the man who hired your driver.”

“People hire drivers every day.”

Minh looked at the racked balls. He placed the white ball at the near end, leaned over the table, and struck it.

The break cracked through the room.

Balls scattered into six pockets. One striped ball hit the side cushion, jumped onto the neighboring table, struck its white ball, and sent that into the corner pocket. The last red ball circled the first table, tapped the eight, and followed it into the side.

The felt went still.

Tú stared.

“Nigga.”

Leon nodded without looking away.

“That's the correct technical term.”

Minh laid the cue down.

“Your table leans left.”

Benny's smile had become much smaller.

“You could make money doing that.”

“I'm trying to make money doing this.”

He stepped closer.

“Who hired the driver?”

Benny looked at the men around the room.

Three moved. The first came from behind Minh holding a pool cue by its narrow end. The second lifted a bar stool. The third took a knife from his waistband.

Minh caught the first cue under his arm without turning. He stepped backward into the man holding it. The man's breath left him. Minh lifted him by the belt and laid him across the pool table, face-down, hard enough to bounce the cue ball out of the return tray.

The stool came at Minh's head.

He ducked beneath it, put one palm against the attacker's stomach, and pushed.

The man flew backward through the rope and hit a racing machine. Its plastic steering wheel broke across his back. The screen flashed GAME OVER while he slid into the seat.

Leon looked at the machine.

“Nigga got there on one quarter.”

The knife man stopped.

Minh turned toward him.

“You can keep it in your hand if you stay over there.”

The man looked at Benny.

Benny opened his mouth.

“Answer him yourself,” Minh said.

The man put the knife on a table.

Benny backed toward the office.

Tú moved to block him. Benny shoved him into a chair. Tú fell over the chair and came up holding it by both legs.

“Nigga, I was trying to keep this professional.”

Leon took the chair from him.

“You need a license for the first part.”

Benny ran.

Minh picked up the pool table.

He lifted its near end until the legs left the floor, turned it sideways, and pushed it through the gap between the other tables. The far edge struck the office door as Benny reached it. The door slammed shut in his face.

Benny bounced off it, staggered backward, and found the pool table across his path.

Minh stepped onto the raised edge and looked down at him.

“That's private maintenance.”

Veronica called from the change counter.

“You breaking the pool tables?”

“Moving them.”

“Benny owns those.”

“Good.”

“Don't touch my machines.”

The man sitting in the broken racing game groaned.

Leon pointed at him. “That one bought a ride.”

***

Benny's office contained a desk, two filing cabinets, and a photograph of Benny beside a boxer who clearly had not wanted the photograph taken. Minh put Benny in the chair beneath it.

Leon stood in the doorway. Tú hovered behind him, trying to see around his shoulder.

Minh put the driver's description back into the conversation.

“Green van. Flowers. Men with guns.”

Benny rubbed his nose.

“It was supposed to scare you.”

“It scared the cake.”

“I wasn't there.”

“You're here.”

Benny looked toward the window. It had bars on the outside. Minh waited until he finished evaluating them.

“Dante,” Benny said.

“Rook's son?”

“He said you took something.”

“What?”

“A car. Money. I don't know. He paid for a message.”

Minh took the telephone off Benny's desk and held it out.

“Call him.”

“He's not answering.”

“Then he'll disappoint us together.”

Benny dialed. Minh listened to the ringing. After six rings, a voice answered.

Benny's face changed before he said a word.

“Dante. It's Benny.”

The voice at the other end spoke sharply.

“No. No, he's—”

Minh took the receiver.

“Hello.”

Silence.

“I got your flowers.”

The line clicked.

Minh put the receiver down.

Leon folded his arms.

“Young man got manners problems.”

Minh opened the top drawer. Benny reached toward it, stopped halfway, and put his hand back in his lap.

Inside lay cash, three watches, a packet of photographs, and a key ring bearing a brass tag.

LOT 7.

Minh picked up the keys.

Benny swallowed.

“That's mine.”

“Which car?”

“A Cadillac.”

“Color?”

“White.”

Leon looked toward the street. “White Cadillac out back.”

“I won it,” Benny said.

“From who?”

“Duy.”

Minh put the keys on the desk.

“When?”

“This morning.”

“How?”

Benny looked toward the pool room.

Tú pushed past Leon.

“That's bullshit. You made him play on the short table.”

“I lose here. I got standing.”

“Duy was here?”

“Six in the morning. Sweating. Had keys for three cars and a bag he wouldn't put down. Benny offered him a game.”

Tú nodded.

Leon looked at Minh.

“That nigga could fall into a bank vault and come out owing the hinges.”

Minh picked up the photographs. They showed prize cars parked in the hotel's loading court. The white Cadillac appeared in the first image. Beside it stood Dante, one arm around Duy's shoulders.

Minh stared at the arm.

“I don't know anything about a robbery. I won a car. That's it.”

“He asked for a phone.”

“Who did he call?”

“I didn't listen.”

Minh raised his eyes.

Benny put both hands flat on the desk.

“Blue Palm Car Wash. I heard Blue Palm. That's all.”

***

The white Cadillac waited beneath a canvas awning behind the arcade. Its paint had a pearl finish. Chrome covered its wheels. A little plaque on the dashboard identified it as the seventh lot in Rook's auction.

Leon opened the passenger door and sat down.

“Master got comprehension.”

Thirty thousand dollars.

“Duy didn't know that was there.”

“He sold his keyboard for two hundred dollars.”

“Leon.”

“I heard her.”

## Chapter 6: The Bride's Driver

Mai's fitting was at one.

At twelve fifty-eight, Minh drove the yellow Rolls-Royce through a puddle and arrived outside Felicia Jackson's dress shop with one clean side.

Mai came out onto the sidewalk before he switched off the engine.

“You're early.”

“Two minutes.”

“I was preparing a speech.”

He opened her door from inside.

“Save it. I'll disappoint you later.”

He shouted inside it.

The tire washer stared at Hòa's hand still resting on the tailgate.

“Who the fuck are you?”

Hòa looked toward the porch.

“Late for lunch.”

## Chapter 27: The Practice Dinner

“Why?”

Bách's banquet hall stood three blocks inland from the harbor.

For the wedding, he had replaced the sign outside with a photograph of Minh and Mai large enough to be seen from the next intersection. Mai had approved a modest portrait. Bách had added a gold border, two painted lions, and the words A UNION OF GREATNESS in letters a foot high.

“The lions again.”

Minh came beside her.

“These have the right number of fingers.”

“Lions have paws.”

“Then we're improving.”

Bách met them in a white dinner jacket.

“Look at this room.”

Minh did.

“You moved the bar.”

“Closer to your table.”

Bách beamed.

Mai took his arm and turned him toward the far end.

“Put it back.”

His smile faltered.

“It's a gesture.”

He signaled two employees, and the portable bar began its return journey across the room.

Bách watched him approach with immediate suspicion.

Minh looked at the key.

“Both engines?”

Bách disappeared into his office.

Mai closed the box gently.

“You understand what you've started.”

Moss watched the office door.

“Absolutely.”

The rehearsal began with the officiant asking everybody to stand where they would stand tomorrow.

Sơn looked over his shoulder.

Mai stared at him.

Leon looked at Minh.

Minh looked at the ceiling.

“He'll try,” Mai said.

Hương applauded once from the front row.

***

When they practiced the walk back down the aisle, Minh put a hand at Mai's waist and started too quickly.

She slowed him with two fingers on his wrist.

“We're leaving a ceremony.”

“I know.”

He shortened his stride.

“Now this.”

Minh looked down at their feet.

He took her hand.

“No,” Mai said.

Tín rose from his chair.

Minh did.

Minh looked down at the two pale marks his shoes had left.

Mai covered her smile with her fingers.

“He follows beautifully,” Tín said.

“You looked graceful.”

Bách ran from the office carrying a red envelope.

Hương looked at him.

“The man or the piano?”

Minh came beside him.

“I'll take it.”

“You could have moved it farther from the wall.”

Then Bích came between them and pointed to the dinner tables.

They went.

“Bad wheel.”

“I saw.”

“Then eat.”

Mai took Minh's arm and guided him toward their table.

***

Bách's red envelope contained another key.

“The hall,” he said, placing it beside Minh's plate.

Moss set down his fork.

Mai looked at Bách.

“You're giving us the building?”

“Access,” Bách said quickly. “Whenever you want. Family occasions. Business. Private entertainment.”

Moss picked up his fork again.

“So a key.”

Minh put it in his pocket.

“Thank you.”

Bách relaxed.

“You see? He understands.”

Moss leaned toward him.

“My key starts engines.”

“My key feeds three hundred people.”

Mai passed them a platter.

“Here. Compete quietly.”

Minh came beside him.

“You rehearsing the speech?”

“No.”

“That's a relief.”

Leon smiled faintly.

“Sometimes I hear that shot again.”

Minh turned toward him.

“I know. It was good food.”

Minh nodded.

“You showed up.”

“You were loud.”

“I'll stay loud, then.”

Leon laughed properly this time.

Minh put his hand on the man's uninjured shoulder and squeezed once.

“Tomorrow, you stand beside me.”

“Apparently in the bathroom.”

“Somebody has to make sure you don't run.”

Minh unfolded it.

The first line read: I have known this beautiful nigga since he was poor enough to listen to me.

Minh began laughing before he reached the second.

Leon took the paper back.

“Exactly. That's how it starts.”

## Chapter 28: The Man at the Door

On his wedding morning, Minh woke to somebody knocking on the apartment door with the certainty of a person who owned the street.

“You're late.”

Minh sat.

The food arrived in such quantity that the argument about the two keys ended.

Duy ate beside Sơn and explained why a recovery truck required more judgment than a sports car. Sơn listened for three minutes, then asked whether the judgment happened before or after Duy backed into the trailer. Tú's mother made her son take off his jacket so he would not spill sauce on it. He complied without being reminded of his rank.

At the next table, Sergeant Carter sat beside Navarro, whose swollen ankle was propped on a spare chair. Carter had come off duty and wore a suit that made Leon accuse him of hiding money. Navarro kept describing the ship's damaged engine to the cook from the red-awning restaurant. The cook listened politely until he realized nobody was going to serve the engine and changed the subject to lunch.

Rook remained in custody. Carter mentioned it only when Minh came over to thank him for attending.

“His attorney says he's uncomfortable.”

Minh looked toward the padded chair beside Carter.

“Send him a brochure.”

“I'm off duty.”

“Then enjoy your food.”

Carter raised his glass.

Minh touched his own to it and moved on.

He took pleasure in every table. People stood to shake his hand, pulled out chairs for Mai, slipped envelopes into their palms, and called them back for photographs. A jeweler whose necklace had come out of Rook's case gave Mai a small velvet box and refused to discuss its price. She opened it just enough to see earrings, thanked him, and put it safely beside her plate.

Marisol told Minh that his photograph was staying above her counter.

“Use the wedding one,” he said.

“No. In that one you look available for conversation.”

***

The only trouble at the door arrived after dessert.

Ernie stood outside in a red jacket that had survived four years of fashion by ignoring all of it. He carried a bottle with a ribbon around its neck and looked offended by Tú's clipboard.

“Tell him I supplied the car for his first date.”

Tú looked through the list again.

“What name is that under?”

Minh heard the exchange while walking toward the bathroom and came outside.

Ernie spread his arms.

“There he is.”

Minh looked at the bottle.

“Is it paid for?”

“You're never going to let that go.”

“The tow truck did.”

Tú looked from one man to the other with intense interest.

Ernie held out the bottle.

“Congratulations. I mean it.”

Minh took it. Through the doorway, he could see Mai watching from their table. She recognized Ernie, raised one eyebrow, and waited.

“You got something to say to her?” Minh asked.

Ernie looked into the hall.

“I'm sorry about the restaurant.”

“Louder.”

Ernie drew a breath.

“I'm sorry about the restaurant, Mai.”

Several nearby guests turned. Mai looked at him for a moment, then pointed toward an empty seat beside the cook.

“There's food left.”

Ernie nodded quickly and went inside before she changed her mind.

Tú lowered the clipboard.

“That's it?”

“That's it.”

“I thought something was going to happen.”

Minh put the bottle in his hands.

“You're going to eat cake.”

He held the door open while Tú went through, then followed him back to his wife.

***

Their first dance went well.

Minh waited for Mai's step. She moved close, her hand resting lightly on his shoulder, and they crossed the floor in a slow turn. The room softened around them. People still spoke, glasses still touched tables, but Minh heard the music and Mai's quiet laugh when he successfully avoided the edge of her train.

“See?” she said.

“I was always good.”

“There he is.”

He turned her beneath his arm and brought her back against him.

Leon applauded too early. Felicia pulled his hand down. Reggie moved sideways with the camera and nearly backed into Bách, who was attempting to remain in the background of every important photograph.

When the song ended, Minh did not immediately release her.

“Motorcycle?” he asked.

She smiled.

“Later. I have another dress.”

***

They left after dark.

Mai changed into a shorter white dress and low shoes. Minh took off his tie, put Hòa's bar carefully into its box, and gave the wedding jacket to Felicia with a formal promise that it had encountered no fish.

His motorcycle waited at the side of the hall. He had cleaned it himself that morning before Leon arrived with the chicken. Two helmets rested on the seat.

The guests gathered around them. Hương put a package of food into the small rear case. Bích added another. Tín watched until Minh latched it and then tested the fastening with one finger.

“Good.”

Hòa shook Minh's hand.

“Tomorrow, sleep.”

“That's the plan.”

Mai kissed her mother and grandmother, hugged her father, and touched Tín's cheek. Then she came to the motorcycle and fastened her helmet.

Leon leaned toward Minh.

“If you need anything—”

“Tomorrow,” Mai said.

Leon stepped back.

“A healthy boundary. I respect it.”

Reggie laughed behind the camera.

Minh started the engine. Mai settled behind him and wrapped her arms around his waist. He felt her hands join over his shirt, exactly where they had been on rides home four years earlier.

He pulled away slowly enough for everybody to wave.

At the corner, the light turned red. They stopped beneath it while a bus passed through the intersection. Mai rested her helmet against his back.

“Husband,” she said.

He turned his head a little.

“Wife.”

She squeezed him once.

The light changed.

He took the long way home, along the water, with the city lights on their left and the dark harbor on their right. Nothing chased them. The motorcycle ran cleanly beneath him. When they reached their street, he slowed for the familiar uneven patch in the pavement so she would not bounce against his shoulder.

At home, he parked beneath the shop's outside light and helped her down.

Mai took the food from the rear case. They climbed the stairs above the garage together, and he unlocked their door. He opened it wide, stepped aside, and watched her carry the packages into their kitchen.

Then she set them down, came back to him, and took his hand.

“You too.”

Minh went inside.

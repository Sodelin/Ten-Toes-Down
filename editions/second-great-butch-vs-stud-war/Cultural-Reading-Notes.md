# Cultural reading notes

These are cultural reading notes, not a taxonomy of people. The dialogues are original fiction: each character has a partial perspective and no character speaks for an entire community. The fictional 1967 feud is not actual LGBTQ history. The notes below distinguish what was inspected from what was not.

## C1. Black lesbian gender and sexual culture: celebration and resistance

Wilson’s qualitative study describes gender and sexual roles in one African American lesbian community as sources of both expectations and resistance. That situated finding cautions against treating a character’s label as a universal rule about behavior, desire or power.

**Read scope:** Abstract only; full article not inspected for this revision.

[Source](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19296308/)

## C2. Brown Boi Project — About Us

The organization describes work among masculine-of-center women and queer and trans people of color across racial and gender justice. Its account helps frame masculinity as something people can enjoy, examine and change without assuming identical experiences or power.

**Read scope:** Organizational self-description and participant testimony on the About Us page.

[Source](https://www.brownboiproject.org/about-us.html)

## C3. The Combahee River Collective Statement

The collective connects race, gender, sexuality and class, and pairs solidarity with criticism of sexism. Its position informs the distinction between cooperation and erasure of internal conflict. It is a specific 1977 political statement; it is not evidence for the invented 1967 feud.

**Read scope:** Selected sections of the primary statement, especially pages 1–4 of this reproduction.

[Source](https://lgbtqhistory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Combahee_River_Collective_Statement.pdf)

## C4. Stone Butch Blues — selected author notes

These notes emphasize context, changing terminology, self-definition and collective connection. They provide a reason to let different generations use language differently, rather than force every fictional person into a single present-day vocabulary. The novel’s scenes and prose are not reproduced.

**Read scope:** Selected author notes, printed pages 333–335 / PDF pages 350–352. No fresh full reading of the novel is claimed.

[Source](https://www.lesliefeinberg.net/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Stone-Butch-Blues-by-Leslie-Feinberg.pdf)

## C5. What Is a Stud? The History of Black Lesbian Masculinity

The interviewees describe varied personal expressions of stud identity, including nonbinary experience, within Black queer culture. These accounts resist a checklist of clothes, sexual roles or personality traits. The fictional Rae speaks for herself.

**Read scope:** Brief use of first-person interviews with Cortney Matthewis and $leazy EZ; no interview quotations reused.

[Source](https://www.them.us/story/what-is-a-stud-the-history-of-black-lesbian-masculinity)

## C6. The Gender of Desire: Joan Nestle’s Last Interview — Our Dyke Histories

Nestle’s first-person account brings pleasure, class, race and risk into the same social world. It helps foreground femme desire and agency, rather than making a femme character a passive partner or a reward. No interview scene or erotic passage is adapted.

**Read scope:** Selected written interview/transcript passages; audio not auditioned.

[Source](https://www.queergeographies.com/the-gender-of-desire-joan-nestles-last-interview/)

## C7. Mabel Hampton Oral History Project — Reflections on Lesbian Fashion

The exhibit’s presentation of Hampton helps check against erasing Black butch lives or equating butchness with whiteness. Clothing and self-description belong to a particular life. June is an invented character, not a portrait of Hampton.

**Read scope:** Curatorial descriptions inspected; recordings not auditioned and no new transcript made.

[Source](https://herstories.prattinfoschool.nyc/omeka/exhibits/show/reflections-on-lesbian-fashion/mabel-hampton-oral-history-pro)

## C8. Sylvia Rivera’s 1973 speech — transcript in Document Set 3

Rivera’s speech challenges exclusion within a movement and asks who provides material support to vulnerable community members. It informs the coalition’s concern with paid work, resources and accountability. Rivera does not appear in the fiction, and no speech is assigned to a fictional character.

**Read scope:** Speech transcript on PDF page 2. The packet’s Stonewall biographical claim is not relied upon.

[Source](https://teachrock.org/wp-content/uploads/Document-Set-3-Sylvia-Rivera.pdf)

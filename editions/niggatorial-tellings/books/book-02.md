# BOOK II: SKY HIGH, TEN TOES HIGHER

## 1. They Put My Parking Space in Heaven

The first time I drove my police Lamborghini into the clouds, the sky opened up and a woman told me I was already late for my own award.

I had not enrolled yet. Nigga, I hadn't found the parking lot. Some other nigga was already polishing a prize for me while I was trying to work out whether this cloud counted as a turn.

Tank called from the Mercedes behind us.

“This nigga said you late for an award?”

“Woman said it.”

“Aiden, nigga, don't get technical in heaven. What you win?”

“I don't know yet.”

“How this nigga win something he don't know?”

Shawna leaned toward the phone. “Tank, you've met him.”

That settled the question. I heard my nigga laugh, heard Rosa laugh beside him, heard the Mercedes engine rise through the white air. A nigga could get used to that sound: all his people following because the impossible road had finally decided to hold his weight.

Shawna sat beside me with her bare feet on the dashboard and a pair of white sunglasses bigger than the sins we had committed on our honeymoon. She had a new diamond on her wedding finger and Dante's old watch on her wrist. I had my badge in my pocket, his cross under my shirt, and a twelve-cylinder reason to believe the universe had finally gotten serious about respecting me.

The invitation said Sky High Advanced Hero Academy. A postgraduate program for heroes who had already graduated and somehow still wanted homework. It said my rescue in Vegas had demonstrated exceptional aptitude.

Exceptional aptitude was when you parallel parked without touching the curb.

I had driven through a burning auditorium and still delivered my valedictorian speech before the principal finished crying.

They should have put exceptional motherfucker.

“Aiden,” Shawna said. “Cloud.”

“Baby, I see it.”

“Inside the car.”

A little piece of weather rolled through the open window and rested on her lap. She pushed her sunglasses down and stared at it. The cloud drifted over her wedding ring, turned pink, and dissolved.

“Your diamonds doing shit to the climate now,” I said.

“Keep looking at the road.”

There was no road. That was the first issue.

There had been a road until thirty seconds ago. It had climbed a mountain, passed between two stone lions, and ended at a gate reading WELCOME, HEROES. Then a guard in silver armor had glanced at my gold badge, looked at my face, and saluted so hard his helmet fell off.

“Captain Aiden,” he'd said. “It's an honor.”

“Nigga, which way?”

“Up.”

I had appreciated his confidence.

Now the Lamborghini was climbing an invisible ramp through the clouds at a speed Shawna described as “why the fuck.” The wheel vibrated in my hands. Beneath us, the mountains shrank until they looked like somebody's pile of dirty clothes.

Behind us Tank's black Mercedes rose through the mist. Rosa sat beside him, singing into a hairbrush. Marcellus was in the back wearing a gray suit, carrying a small case, and looking as if he had already decided heaven charged too much for lunch. Dr. Vega had come for a three-day visit and was currently holding Tank's headrest like she intended to remove it.

Mercedes and Porsche followed in a red convertible I had bought them after they pointed out that I owned an island's worth of cars and kept asking them to share an Uber. The women were arguing over whose turn it was to meet a man who could fly.

All my people were ascending.

And at the top of the clouds waited a whole fucking city.

Not a building. A city.

White towers rose around a stadium shaped like a golden shield. A glass train moved between them without touching anything. Gardens hung off stone balconies. An enormous waterfall poured from one edge of the island and disappeared into blue air. Little vehicles streamed across bridges suspended between floating blocks of land. Farther out, a ring of separate islands carried hotels, stores, and a casino with a flashing sign visible from what appeared to be several religions.

SKY HIGH stood over the front gate in letters taller than my mansion.

I slowed down just enough for the Lamborghini to make its noise.

Shawna smiled.

“There he go.”

“Who?”

“My husband about to introduce himself to the atmosphere.”

“Baby, the atmosphere been waiting.”

The invisible ramp became a solid white drive. Students stopped where they were. A man with transparent wings folded them and turned. A woman carrying three refrigerators over her head looked at me and forgot she was carrying refrigerators. Somebody in a crimson cape walked straight into a fountain.

I parked in front of the main staircase.

There was a gold sign.

DEAN.

I read it carefully and remained parked.

Tank pulled up beside my door, lowered his window, and looked at the sign. Then at the car. Then back at the sign, giving the English language every opportunity to save me.

“Nigga, you know somebody named Dean?”

“Could be welcoming me by position.”

“Your position is illegally parked.”

Shawna reached across me and tapped the horn. Once. A tiny, expensive note of warning.

“Move the car, Aiden.”

“Baby, let me establish a relationship.”

“Nigga, with reverse.”

Behind Tank, Porsche had stopped sideways to take a picture. Mercedes leaned across her and straightened the phone.

“Get the sign in it. This nigga gonna claim they reserved it.”

“They might.”

“Then we got a before picture,” Mercedes said.

Rosa came over, read DEAN, and hooked her arm through Shawna's.

“Girl, first day and your husband already trying to graduate the administration.”

“I dressed him myself. I thought that would use up the difficult part.”

“Nigga came accessorized with a jurisdiction,” Tank said.

I opened my door with the dignity available to a man being publicly coordinated by everybody he loved. My shoe touched white stone. The engine settled into a low, satisfied rumble.

“Listen to that,” I said. “Car agrees.”

“That nigga burns gasoline,” Shawna said. “He'll agree with a garage fire.”

Tank bent down toward the exhaust.

“You happy, little nigga?”

“Don't talk to my car like that.”

“I'm establishing a relationship.”

The women broke. Rosa leaned into Shawna. Mercedes lowered the phone because she couldn't keep it steady. Even Vega loosened one hand from Tank's headrest.

There it was: one decent laugh, and suddenly we had ground under us again, even up here. I could work with that.

Then somebody above us cleared her throat. Every nigga in our arrival committee remembered we were parked in front of a school.

A tall woman descended the steps wearing a white suit with silver lightning along the collar. Her hair fell black to her shoulders except for one white streak. She had the kind of face that made a man stand up straighter before he remembered he had a wife beside him. The air sharpened around her. Her eyes were gray, and behind the gray something bright moved.

“Captain Aiden,” she said.

I stepped out. “Dean.”

“Seraphine Storm.”

“That's a hell of a name.”

“I earned it.”

“So did I.”

We shook hands. A tiny spark jumped between us.

I did not let go.

Her eyebrow rose.

“Most visitors flinch.”

“Most visitors ain't me.”

Shawna came around the car. Storm's attention moved to her ring, then to her face.

“Mrs. Aiden.”

“Shawna.”

They shook hands without sparks. I respected the weather for knowing when to behave.

Tank parked behind me. “Nigga, you in her space.”

“I know where I'm at.”

“We can arrange another space,” Storm said.

Tank looked from her to me and slowly took off his sunglasses.

“We just got here.”

“I make a strong first impression.”

From the staircase a blond man in a blue uniform laughed once. He was broad through the shoulders, clean-shaven, with a silver emblem on his chest and a jaw he plainly expected other people to remember.

“That's the famous graduate?” he asked.

Storm didn't look away from me. “Victor Voltage. Twenty-seven. First in his division until this morning.”

Victor's smile stopped.

My smile began.

“Nigga, don't look at me. I ain't touched your division.”

“Your provisional placement puts you first overall,” Storm told me.

“Based on what?” Victor demanded.

She gestured toward the Lamborghini.

He looked at the car as if it had personally stolen his childhood.

“That's a vehicle.”

“The entrance ramp was closed,” Storm said.

Everybody got quiet.

I looked back at the sky.

Tank looked back at the sky.

Shawna took off her sunglasses very slowly.

“Aiden.”

“Now hold on.”

Storm's smile grew. “We were testing our emergency magnetic ascent field. You drove through its narrowest boundary at exactly the velocity required to remain inside it. Then three civilian cars followed your path.”

Dr. Vega got out of the Mercedes.

“I would like to go home.”

“You can use the open ramp,” Storm said.

“There was an open ramp?”

I put my arm around Shawna. “Baby, I brought us the scenic way.”

She pinched my side. The old wound still had opinions about people touching it.

“The scenic way to fucking death.”

“And look how beautiful we survived.”

Then the sky train exploded.

Not the whole train. The front coupling burst in orange flame as it came around the stadium. A sharp crack punched across the courtyard. Heads turned. A hundred windows flashed.

The train tilted.

Forty people screamed at once.

Victor was moving before the sound finished. Blue-white energy wrapped his fists. He launched himself upward, fast enough to knock spray out of the fountain. Storm raised both hands. Wind slammed through the courtyard.

The train's front carriage lurched back toward its rail.

The rear two kept falling.

I saw a woman pressed to the back window. Saw a man drag another man off the floor. Saw the distance between that train and the edge of the island closing.

Nigga, I was supposed to be receiving something.

Instead I had to go earn it again.

I jumped into the Lamborghini.

“Tank!”

He already had the Mercedes door open.

Shawna slid into my passenger seat.

“You staying here,” I said.

“Drive.”

“Baby—”

“Those people do not have time for you to win this argument.”

I hit the gas.

The car shot between the fountain and the steps. Somebody tried to tell me that was a pedestrian path. He said the first half to the windshield and the second half to the exhaust.

The rear carriages struck a maintenance platform beyond the stadium. Metal shrieked. The platform bent toward the open air. Victor hovered above it, both arms spread, electricity coursing into the damaged suspension rings. Sweat ran down his face. His boots flickered.

He was powerful.

He was also holding forty people over a drop nobody had bothered measuring for survivability.

I understood his problem immediately.

Too many niggas. Not enough ground.

I took a turn so hard Shawna's hair whipped across my mouth. The Lamborghini climbed a service stair, hit the landing, and flew over a strip of garden. We landed on the maintenance platform with a crash that made my teeth feel individually expensive.

The platform dipped.

“Get that car off!” Victor shouted.

I looked through the windshield at the hanging carriage.

“Shawna. Door.”

She unbuckled. “Which?”

“All of them.”

I shoved the Lamborghini nose beneath a sloping support beam. The hood buckled. A warning screamed from the dashboard. I kept going until the car wedged between the broken platform and the stone service wall.

The platform stopped dropping.

Victor stared at me.

“Nigga,” I shouted, “keep doing the light shit!”

Shawna was already at the carriage. She hit its emergency release, hauled the door open, and caught the first woman through it by both arms.

“Walk toward the wall,” she ordered. “Keep your hands on the rail. Don't look down.”

Somebody looked down and froze.

My wife slapped him.

“Look at me instead.”

He did. He moved.

Tank brought the Mercedes onto solid ground and jumped out. Rosa followed him. She took one look at the gap and began singing, loud and steady, a beat people could move to. Not a sad song. One of the nasty ones she performed in Vegas when men forgot how much cash they had brought.

Forty terrified adult heroes began crossing a ruined platform to a song about getting paid before breakfast.

I climbed onto my hood.

It was an expensive hood. I would mourn it when niggas stopped falling out of the sky.

Inside the last carriage, a man had his leg trapped beneath a fallen seat. A silver-haired woman knelt beside him. One whole side of the carriage was opening over empty blue air.

I jumped through the door.

The woman looked at my badge.

“Are you rescue?”

“Today.”

I grabbed the seat. It didn't move.

The trapped man said, “My power's density. I can't turn it off when I'm scared.”

“How heavy?”

“A lot.”

“Nigga, that is a terrible number.”

The train shifted. Outside, Victor yelled. A burst of blue light turned the windows white.

I braced my shoes against the opposite seat and pulled. The metal bit my palms. Nothing. The man shook so hard the floor rattled.

I got close to his face.

“What's your name?”

“Elias.”

“Elias, listen to me. My wife standing outside this train.”

He blinked.

“That woman thinks I'm the greatest man alive. I happen to agree. Now you gonna make both of us look stupid?”

“No.”

“Then stop trying to weigh more than my reputation.”

He laughed. Just once. A frightened little cough of a laugh.

The floor lifted.

I tore the seat aside.

“There we fucking go.”

The woman helped me drag him. We reached the door as the back window broke away. Wind hit us. My cross slapped against my chest.

Below, the waterfall hung like a white ribbon all the way into the clouds.

Victor's left hand went dark.

The carriage dropped six inches.

I shoved Elias through the door into Tank's arms. The woman followed. I grabbed the rail.

Then the platform tore loose.

My Lamborghini went with it.

For one ugly second I saw my whole future reflected in that dark blue roof: car gone, wife watching, nigga floating down to Earth with a gold badge and an unpaid insurance argument.

Shawna caught my wrist.

Tank caught her waist.

Storm's wind caught the car.

Everything stopped.

Not gently. My shoulder made a noise. Shawna swore. Tank planted his feet and shouted, “I got all you motherfuckers!”

The dean stood at the end of the broken walkway with lightning in her hair. The entire Lamborghini hung sideways beneath us inside a spinning cradle of cloud.

“Captain,” she said through clenched teeth. “Would you please stop adding weight?”

I hauled myself up beside my wife.

“I'm trying to take some off.”

Victor brought the last carriage down into the grass. Storm lowered my car beside it. The engine ticked. The left headlight hung out like a tired eye.

Silence spread over the service yard.

Then Elias began clapping.

The silver-haired woman joined him.

By the time I stood up, everybody was doing it.

Shawna grabbed my face and kissed me hard enough to settle the disagreement we had not finished in the car. I held her against me. Around us forty rescued people cheered louder.

“That's my husband,” she said when she pulled back.

Nobody argued.

Victor landed beside the wrecked train. His uniform smoked at the wrists. He looked at the Lamborghini. Looked at my bleeding hands. Looked at Shawna holding me.

“You have no powers,” he said.

“I got several good qualities.”

“No. I mean you have no measurable power signature.”

“Nigga, measure the people who ain't dead.”

His mouth opened.

Then he looked at Elias sitting safely on the grass.

He closed it.

Storm came toward me. Her hair settled. Behind her, students poured around the corner, calling to the passengers, filming the wreckage, shouting my name even though I had introduced myself to approximately four people.

“Your award ceremony,” the dean said, “will now include an additional citation.”

I looked at my car.

“Will it include a body shop?”

“The best engineering faculty in the sky.”

“Good. That nigga need a doctor.”

Dr. Vega stepped around Tank carrying a medical bag.

“The car can wait. You are bleeding.”

“Little bit.”

“That is also a terrible number.”

She took my hand. Shawna stood against my other side. Tank looked at the shattered platform and shook his head with a smile he couldn't hide.

“You been here twelve minutes.”

“Thirteen,” Storm said.

“See?” I told him. “I gave the school an extra minute.”

At the edge of the crowd stood a thin man in a charcoal suit. He had silver hair combed straight back, a black cane, and eyes that did not seem interested in blinking. Two guards stood behind him in uniforms darker than the others.

Storm noticed him and straightened.

“Chancellor Voss.”

He approached the broken coupling first. Bent his head. Touched a scorched piece of metal with one gloved finger.

Then he turned to me.

“An extraordinary arrival.”

“We try.”

His smile showed no teeth.

“You saved a number of people whose gifts are important to this institution.”

I glanced at Elias. He was holding the silver-haired woman's hand. His whole body shook now that it was allowed to.

“Saved the rest too.”

“Naturally.”

Voss's eyes moved to my badge, then my cross, then Shawna. He lingered on none of them long enough for me to call it a problem.

But a nigga can feel a problem dressing itself.

“We will investigate the equipment failure,” he said.

“Do that.”

His guards collected the broken coupling before anybody else touched it.

Storm watched them go. Her face had changed for one second, then changed back.

She offered us the grand staircase, the open doors, the city of people waiting to meet me.

I put my good arm around Shawna. Tank lifted my overnight bag from the ruined car. Rosa took his other arm. Marcellus appeared carrying the case he had somehow never set down.

“First impression?” he asked.

“Strong,” I said.

He looked at the wrecked train, then at the whole school applauding.

“I should have brought a larger safe.”

We went up those steps together.

Above the entrance hung a banner they had apparently prepared before I arrived.

WELCOME, EXCEPTIONAL HEROES.

Somebody was already climbing a ladder to change it.

I didn't tell them to.

Nigga, excellence makes suggestions of its own.

Our rooms occupied the top floor of a tower overlooking the western edge. There were two balconies, a bath large enough to qualify for boat ownership, and a bedroom ceiling that showed the actual stars above the clouds. The bellman set down our bags and waited to be tipped.

I gave him enough money to change the direction of his afternoon.

He looked at it. Looked at me.

“Sir, I have worked here eleven years.”

“Then you was overdue.”

He left walking backward.

Shawna closed the door and leaned against it. Her smile had gone soft. That was the one that always made me feel more dangerous and less prepared at the same time.

“You scared me,” she said.

“I had it.”

“I know you had it. That's not the same thing.”

I crossed the room. She put her hands beneath my open jacket and rested them against my ribs, careful of the old hurt. Outside the balcony a woman flew past carrying a bouquet larger than herself. Somewhere below us people were still shouting about the train.

“You came with me,” I said.

“Always.”

“Even when I'm parking wrong.”

“Especially. Apparently that's when you become first in your class.”

I kissed her forehead. Then her mouth. She pulled me closer, and for a little while the biggest thing happening in the sky was our door staying shut.

Later, when we stood on the balcony wearing the school's expensive robes, I saw a second city beneath the first.

It wasn't really a city. More a collection of black buildings suspended under the island's lower rock. Bridges joined them. Red lights burned along their roofs. One tower descended farther than the rest and ended in a circular chamber that glowed through its windows like a covered furnace.

“What's that?” Shawna asked.

“Basement with ambition.”

A train moved along a track beneath it. The cars had no windows.

She stopped smiling.

The train entered the glowing chamber. For a moment the windows turned a strange yellow-white. Then every light went out together.

Above us, music began on the school lawn. People laughed. Somebody sent a ribbon of purple fire curling between two towers.

Shawna rested her head against my shoulder.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “I'm finding somebody who'll tell me what that is.”

I looked at the dark place under all our beautiful rooms.

“Tomorrow,” I said, “I'm getting my fucking car back.”

## 2. My Superpower Was Being That Nigga

The engineering professor cried when he returned my Lamborghini. This nigga had repaired half the greatest machines in the sky, and now he was holding a polishing cloth to his face because my car had made him believe again.

Actual tears. One fell on the hood, and he polished it away with his sleeve before he let himself have the next one.

His name was Professor Atlas Bell. He was forty-six, had four arms, and treated engines like other men treated their firstborn sons. He stood beside the car in the morning sunlight with two hands folded, one holding a clipboard, and the last stroking the repaired fender.

“It was beautiful before,” he told me. “But what you did with it—”

He had to stop.

Tank leaned toward me. “Nigga, you made a man cry with traffic.”

“It wasn't regular traffic.”

“Nigga was crying when we got here.”

“Maybe he started early.”

“No, nigga. Rosa asked that nigga what happened, and he said your suspension.”

I looked at Bell. The professor nodded gravely, like my suspension had died for his sins and risen on the third wheel.

“A magnificent structure,” he said.

Tank pointed at him. “See? Nigga grieving and praising at once.”

“Let that nigga feel his feelings,” I told him. “He brought my car back beautiful.”

Bell had restored the dark blue paint. The police lights were now hidden beneath the front edge, so the car looked like pure money until I decided somebody should be nervous. He had reinforced the suspension for campus roads and fitted the wheels with the academy's standard field receivers. That meant the Lamborghini could travel along the marked sky lanes like the other vehicles. It did not mean it could fly wherever I wanted.

He explained that last part three times.

I heard him all three times. I just refused to make my face less hopeful.

Bell laid the clipboard on the hood and drew a blue road with a pen. He put a little square on it.

“Your car.”

“Nigga, give it some shape.”

He added a triangle at the front.

Tank leaned in. “Now it's a refrigerator that heard something.”

“It is a diagram,” Bell said.

“Of a crime against design.”

One of the professor's hands covered the drawing while another retrieved his polishing cloth. The third pointed at me. The fourth pointed at Tank. Nigga had enough arms to tell both of us to shut up and still protect his work.

“Inside the marked field, the receivers support the vehicle. Outside it, they have nothing to receive. Do you understand?”

“Nigga, yes.”

“Explain it back.”

Tank slapped the hood. “Ooh. He assigned this nigga consequences with oral examination.”

I pointed to the line. “Car goes here.”

“Correct.”

I pointed beyond it. “Car should not go here.”

“Cannot.”

“Nigga, it can go there. You just won't like the direction.”

Bell took a breath. Considered correcting me. Decided gravity could keep its small victory.

“Exactly.”

Shawna had reached us in time to hear that. She took the pen and drew a second square beside the first.

“What's that?” I asked.

“The hospital bill.”

“Baby, that one's bigger than the car.”

“Nigga, I included emotional damages.”

Bell nodded at her drawing like somebody had finally entered a useful number.

Tank inspected both squares. “Put my ass in a circle. I ain't being included.”

“You're always included,” I said.

“That's what I'm appealing, nigga.”

Bell tore off the diagram, folded it, and gave it to Shawna. She slid it into her pocket. I watched my own operating instructions change hands without anybody requesting the manufacturer's consent.

“Now,” Bell said, uncovering the car, “we may admire it.”

“Nigga, finally a subject everybody studied.”

Shawna wore a white tracksuit with my name embroidered over her heart. Rosa wore red. Mercedes wore green leather. Porsche had found a silver jumpsuit and a pair of boots that made a nearby man forget how stairs worked.

“We going to your test,” Shawna said.

“What test?”

Victor emerged from the archway behind her.

“Power placement.”

He said it like an execution date.

“I already got a parking place,” I told him.

“Let's see how you perform without a car.”

“Nigga, ask my wife.”

Shawna slapped my chest and started laughing. Victor looked up at the sky as if requesting a different assignment from God.

The placement hall was bigger inside than outside. It had retractable walls, a floor divided into colored circles, and a spectator gallery rising around all four sides. Students filled the seats. Word of the train rescue had apparently traveled faster than the train was supposed to. Half the crowd wore little gold paper badges somebody had made overnight.

One woman held a sign reading ARREST ME, CAPTAIN.

Shawna saw it.

The woman slowly turned it around.

Storm stood in the center beside a stone pillar holding a blue crystal. The crystal wasn't the size of a jewel. It was the size of a man's head, cut into an uneven heart, and its light pulsed down through the pillar into the floor.

“The Heart of the Sky,” she said as we approached. “It anchors the academy's central field. Today you will be assessed around a fractional projection of its output.”

I looked at the thing.

“Whole school running off one diamond?”

“A power core. It amplifies gifts; it doesn't create them.”

Marcellus bent toward the crystal. He did not touch it. His expression narrowed.

“Interesting cut.”

Voss stood near the gallery stairs. “Please keep your distance, Mr. Vale.”

Marcellus straightened. “I was admiring it.”

“Of course.”

Storm clapped once. The first wall folded back, revealing a corridor full of swinging steel blocks. Beyond it stood three platforms and a bell suspended over a pool.

“Movement,” she said. “Judgment. Control. Rescue. We rank useful outcomes, not destruction.”

Tank looked at the steel blocks. “Good. Because some nigga absolutely about to destroy something.”

Victor went first.

I gave him that much: the man was magnificent when he stopped talking.

He turned into a streak of blue light, passed between the blocks, landed on the first platform, and sent a bolt into a target above the second. A bridge unfolded. He crossed it, caught a weighted rescue dummy as it fell, and reached the bell in twenty-three seconds.

The crowd roared.

He set the dummy down carefully. Looked straight at me. Rang the bell once more.

I nodded.

“Clean.”

He seemed annoyed I meant it.

A woman named Petra Stone went next. Twenty-nine, broad shoulders, calm eyes. She could turn her skin into white marble. She carried the rescue dummy through a steel block instead of around it. Her time was slower. The steel block's prospects were considerably worse.

Mercedes stood up clapping.

“Girl, yes!”

Petra looked up, spotted her, and nearly dropped the dummy.

Porsche leaned against the rail. “You already choosing?”

“I'm appreciating strength.”

“You appreciate everybody with shoulders.”

“Only if they use them right.”

Then Storm said my name.

The entire gallery stood.

Nigga, I had not done anything in this room yet.

I took off my jacket and gave it to Shawna. She smoothed my collar with both hands.

“Come back with all your teeth.”

“Baby, that's the minimum.”

At the starting line, a faculty member asked whether I required a special condition.

“Such as?”

“Reduced gravity. Protective barrier. A powered escort.”

“Some music.”

He blinked.

Rosa began tapping the railing.

Tank joined her. Then Porsche. Then half the room. A big rolling beat traveled through the hall. My people had turned a superhero examination into the entrance to my own heavyweight championship.

Storm lifted her hand.

The blocks began swinging.

I ran.

First block left. Second block right. A narrow opening appeared between them, and I went through sideways with my shoulder almost kissing steel. The third descended straight at my head. I dropped under it, slid across the floor, and came up laughing because a nigga knows when his body has decided to make him famous.

The first platform was farther than it looked.

I jumped anyway.

My fingers caught the edge. I pulled myself up, rolled under a sweeping bar, and saw the target Victor had struck with lightning.

I had no lightning.

I had shoes.

I took one off and threw it.

The shoe hit the center of the target.

The bridge unfolded.

Tank shouted so loud I heard him over everybody else.

“Nigga used a loafer!”

I crossed in one shoe, caught the falling dummy over my shoulder, and took the last platform in three strides. The bell hung above the pool. Victor had flown to it. Petra had jumped from the edge.

I threw the dummy onto the safe pad, caught its rebound with my knee, and used the spring in the pad to launch myself up the pillar beside the bell.

My hand struck gold.

The sound filled the room.

Twenty-two seconds.

I landed in the pool.

Nobody's masterpiece is dry all the way through.

When I surfaced, Storm was smiling. The faculty member stared at his clock. Victor stared at my shoe, which was slowly sliding off the target.

Shawna had both hands over her head.

“That's my fucking man!”

I climbed out dripping. Walked to Victor. Held out my hand.

He looked at it.

“You used the rescue dummy as a springboard.”

“I rescued it first.”

“That's not—”

Storm glanced at the safe pad. “The dummy remained entirely within the designated safety zone.”

Victor shook my hand.

Hard.

I shook it back.

Harder.

We both smiled like niggas deciding whether a friendship ought to begin with a fight.

The next test involved doors. Behind one stood a fire. Behind another, a trapped volunteer. Behind a third, a man pretending to be an attacker. You had to choose in six seconds using information flashed on a screen.

I looked at the screen.

Then I looked at the doors.

Then I walked up to the third door and knocked.

The volunteer attacker opened it out of surprise.

I took his padded weapon.

“You under arrest.”

The gallery exploded.

He said, “But the scenario—”

“Nigga, you armed behind a door while people dying in the next room. Come carry something.”

I put him to work. Together we carried the trapped volunteer through the safe corridor while Petra smothered the training fire with a wall of stone. The faculty member tried to say assistance wasn't part of the test.

Storm asked whether real disasters prohibited asking another adult for help.

He looked at his clipboard and discovered a deep interest in the lower half of it.

By lunch they had awarded me first place in movement, judgment, improvisation, and something called command presence.

Command presence was when other niggas did what you said because it sounded like the part of the day they should survive.

I had been practicing that since Vegas.

Then the real attacker came through the ceiling.

He wore a black flight harness and a mirrored mask. Three more followed him through the skylight. They fell in a line, landing around the crystal pillar with weapons already raised.

The first shot shattered a gallery lamp.

Screams replaced applause.

Victor moved toward the pillar. One of the attackers threw a dark metal hoop. It struck his chest and opened around him, pinning his arms to his sides. His lightning flared once, then guttered.

Storm lifted her hand.

The man in the mirrored mask held up a small device with a red light.

“Wind,” he said, “and the south gallery falls.”

Beneath the south seats, red lights blinked.

Shawna was in the south gallery.

So was everybody I loved.

I felt the whole room go narrow.

The man saw my badge.

“Sit down, officer.”

I looked at him.

My people were beautiful in every corner.

Rosa sang with Lena, then stepped offstage and let her take a song alone. Tank stood beneath the lights watching both women, but his face changed whenever Rosa looked his way. The nigga's whole expression went soft and proud, and I loved seeing him unable to hide it.

Mercedes danced with Petra. Porsche danced with one Leon while another fetched drinks and the third stood near the terrace door recovering from being asked to do too much with the same legs.

“He's only got a minute,” Mercedes warned her.

“Then he better use it beautifully.”

Petra laughed so hard the floor beneath her shoes briefly turned white with marble dust.

I won another hand.

The dealer looked at me with open admiration.

“Sir, I have dealt cards for twenty years.”

“You doing good.”

“I have never seen this.”

Tank slapped the table.

“Nigga, that's what the school said!”

Shawna took the chips from in front of me and put them into a little velvet bag.

“You're finished.”

I looked up at her.

“Baby, the universe talking.”

“It can call tomorrow. You're dancing with me.”

I stood so fast the finance manager nearly cried with relief.

“Excellent decision,” he said.

I pointed at him.

“Nigga, don't get comfortable. We walking past roulette.”

Shawna pulled me away laughing.

We danced beneath the new moon with no chancellor waiting to interrupt us. Nobody asked her for a private word. Nobody threatened a locked door. The only thing coming toward us was a waiter carrying drinks and looking mildly frightened that I might somehow win those too.

She rested her cheek against mine.

“You like being a superhero?”

“I like the parking.”

“Aiden.”

I thought about the blue charge in my hands. The fatigue afterward. Victor sitting beside my car and telling me to listen when my body said enough. Storm standing in the air to hold an entire school. Lena making one word travel where bullets couldn't reach.

“I like knowing another thing I can do,” I said.

Shawna nodded.

“That's a better answer.”

“I had it behind the parking.”

She smiled against my cheek.

For a while we let the song do the talking. I could feel the room looking at us, but it stayed outside the small space between her hand and my shoulder. My fame had gotten louder in the sky. My love for her had gotten quieter in certain places, stronger where it didn't need anybody listening.

Then she spun away from me, came back with both hands on my chest, and said, “Show these people what all that greatness does on a dance floor.”

Nigga, a direct request from my wife was a sacred assignment.

I did the turn I had practiced in the bathroom.

Perfectly.

The orchestra saw us and changed its rhythm. Rosa laughed into the microphone. Tank climbed onto a chair to announce that the captain was now operating across multiple disciplines. I turned Shawna again, caught her at the waist, and dipped her low enough for the ruby at her throat to catch the moonlight.

She looked up at me, smiling.

The ballroom applauded.

This time I didn't even pretend to dislike it.

Oren Titan approached afterward with Opaline on his arm. The big man had apparently recovered from the indignity of watching me rescue his wife while keeping a drink upright. He carried a trophy he had bought himself: a small silver pedestal with two champagne glasses fixed to its top.

“For the man with the steadiest hands in the sky,” he said.

Tank looked at the trophy.

“Nigga, another building?”

“A sculpture,” Oren said.

“These niggas keep doing architecture to us.”

Opaline hugged Shawna, then thanked me again. Oren did not look smaller beside her gratitude. He looked relieved he had found a way to join it without making the whole thing about the moment he had failed to catch her.

That was progress.

I shook his hand.

“Next time we lift the furniture together.”

He laughed and agreed immediately.

Across the room, Claudine raised her glass toward us. The older casino guest wore enough emeralds to make Marcellus turn his head despite himself. She saw Shawna's ruby pendant and smiled with the satisfaction of a woman who had given the right thing to the right person.

Shawna went to her.

They talked for a long time.

I didn't follow. Some gifts kept becoming conversations after the man who carried them had left the room.

Near the terrace, Marcellus sat at a small table with Miriam and Bell. A blue cloth lay between them. On it rested the Blue Mercy ring, a tiny clock mechanism Miriam had brought from the depot, and one of Bell's unfamiliar engine parts.

Three beautiful things. Three people who knew how much care could fit inside something a careless man might call an object.

Marcellus looked up when I approached.

“Come admire something you aren't allowed to drive.”

“Nigga, that sentence leaves me very little.”

He showed Miriam the ring. Told her its name. Told her about Celeste, briefly, in the voice he used when the person mattered more than the story surrounding the jewel.

Miriam listened without trying to make his grief resemble hers.

Then she showed him the clock piece. It had belonged to a workshop where she worked before she was taken. She had kept it hidden because it was proof she had once fixed things for people who said thank you.

Marcellus held it carefully.

“We could make it run again.”

She looked at him.

“I would like that.”

Bell quietly cleared space on the table.

The party went on around them while they bent over a small machine and gave it the possibility of another morning.

I left them to it.

On the terrace, Tank stood alone looking out at the clouds. He had taken off his jacket. The red ribbon remained on his wrist. Rosa's singing drifted through the open doors behind us, warm enough to make the night feel less high.

I stood beside him.

“You good?”

He nodded.

Then shook his head.

Then laughed at himself.

“Nigga, I don't know what this is.”

“Clouds.”

“Fuck you.”

I smiled.

He looked toward the ballroom. Rosa was laughing with Lena between songs, one hand at her waist, head tilted back. My brother watched her the way a man watched a door he had finally realized he was allowed to walk through.

“I keep thinking about houses,” he said.

“You got a house.”

“A different kind.”

I let him find the words.

“A place where her coat already there. Where I don't got to ask if she staying. Where I come in and she mad I forgot some little shit, and I get to go back out and buy it because that's the problem we having that day.”

He looked embarrassed by how much he had said.

I looked at my brother until the embarrassment had nowhere useful to stand.

“Nigga, that sound like something worth buying the little shit for.”

He laughed quietly.

“You got that.”

I looked through the doors and saw Shawna searching the room for me.

“Yeah.”

“Don't get stupid with it.”

“Nigga, you met me.”

“I said with it. You can keep being stupid everywhere else.”

We stood there another minute. Then Rosa came out and slipped her arm through his. He kissed the top of her head without checking whether anybody was watching.

I went back inside to my wife.

The next morning, the school attempted to hold a quiet farewell breakfast.

That lasted until somebody unveiled a statue of me on the lawn.

Nigga, the statue was taller than the dining hall.

It showed me standing on the hood of a Lamborghini with lightning in one hand and a diploma in the other. The sculptor had given my chest an extra six inches of confidence. My coat blew backward in a wind that seemed to exist solely because I deserved dramatic weather.

Tank walked around the base in silence.

Then he looked at me.

“This nigga got a permanent entrance.”

Shawna put both hands over her face.

I stood very still.

Because I respected art.

Porsche took a picture from directly below its chin.

“Look at this nigga. Even the stone got an angle.”

Mercedes circled to the far side. “They gave him shoulders twice.”

“Perspective,” I said.

“Nigga, from here he could carry the breakfast hall.”

Shawna lowered her hands, looked from me to the statue, and bent to read the little plaque at its base.

“They got your name right.”

“Fundamental.”

“Everything else appears to be a negotiation.”

Tank stood beside one enormous carved shoe and put his own foot against it.

“Nigga, you could rent this loafer.”

“Good location.”

“Terrible landlord.”

Rosa leaned against him laughing. Behind her, two students were trying to decide whether their banner should hang across my stone knees or between the statue's feet. Tank directed them toward the knees with the authority of a man already serving on the board.

“Keep the entrance clear.”

“Nigga, stop leasing me.”

“You said good location.”

The sculptor approached with pale dust still on his cuffs and a face full of nervous pride. He was an ordinary-sized man who had spent his week making an unreasonable-sized version of me.

“Is it satisfactory, Captain?”

I looked up. At the extravagant coat. At the diploma. At my car beneath the feet, every line given the kind of attention Bell would demand.

“You did all that?”

“Had help lifting.”

“Nigga, I should hope.”

He laughed, relieved, and I shook his dusty hand.

“It's magnificent.”

Shawna slipped her arm through mine.

“It is,” she said. “We're still leaving it here.”

I looked at her.

“I hadn't—”

“Nigga, your face was measuring the garden.”

Tank pointed at the carved shoe again.

“Put that much man in your yard, the real nigga gotta knock.”

Storm came beside me with a smile she was not trying hard enough to hide.

“The students insisted.”

“All of them?”

“Enough of them to carry the stone.”

Bell cleared his throat.

“There was disagreement about whether the car should receive a separate statue.”

I looked at him.

He looked at the Lamborghini parked nearby.

“I remain in favor.”

Shawna started laughing so hard she had to hold my arm.

The statue's lightning rod chose that moment to catch a harmless little spark from the passing weather. Blue light ran down the stone arm and into the carved car.

The students applauded.

Tank pointed at it.

“Nigga, even the statue got powers.”

I looked up at my enormous stone face.

The sculptor had gotten one thing exactly right.

That nigga looked pleased to be there.

Breakfast became a parade before anybody could stop it. Students lined the main drive with banners. The orchestra marched between the towers. Faculty walked beside our cars, shaking hands through the windows, offering invitations we would never have enough ordinary days to accept.

Miriam brought Bell's final inspection sheet for the Lamborghini and made me promise to listen if the engine sounded unhappy. I promised. She looked at Shawna until my wife promised too.

“Now I believe it,” Miriam said.

Jakob played his violin beside the fountain. Not a song I knew. A song he had chosen for a morning with open air around him. Rivka sat nearby with her bag at her feet and a cup of coffee in both hands. She watched the absurd parade, then caught my eye and raised the cup.

I bowed my head to her.

No speech needed.

Harlan and Anne stood beneath a newly repaired arch of flowering branches. He wore the brown shoes. She had one hand tucked into his arm like she intended to keep it there for the rest of the day and perhaps several years afterward. He showed me a small flower growing out of a crack in the stone.

“This one wanted to be first,” he said.

Tank nodded approvingly.

“Nigga got good influences.”

We said goodbye to people whose lives would continue after our engines faded. That mattered to me more than I expected. Sky High wasn't becoming a place we had saved and could now forget. Victor would remain to train the next advanced class. Petra had accepted a rescue-instructor post and made Mercedes promise to visit before either of them tried pretending distance was a personality trait. Mercedes kissed her and said she had never been shy about booking a flight.

Porsche said goodbye to Leon three times because he insisted on making the most of his gift.

“Don't get arrogant,” she told him after the third kiss.

“You kissed all three.”

“Nigga, I was checking consistency.”

Lena and Rosa promised to sing together again. Amaya had accepted a place at the academy's aquatic center, with a pool whose doors opened from the inside and a teaching schedule she had helped choose. Reiss was staying long enough to dismantle every remaining part of Voss's experiment. After that, he said, he wanted to see an ordinary beach with no physics involved beyond the waves.

I told him that sounded excellent.

Storm waited by the front staircase where I had first parked in her space.

The gold sign was still there.

It now read CAPTAIN AIDEN — WHENEVER HE IS HERE.

I looked at it.

Then at her.

“Dean.”

“Captain.”

We shook hands. No accidental spark this time. I knew enough to keep the little blue current inside me quiet.

“Come back,” she said.

“You sure? I made repairs expensive.”

“You made the place worth repairing.”

She embraced Shawna. Then Tank. Rosa. The whole crew, one by one, without making it ceremonial enough to hide that she meant it.

Victor stood beside my car.

He put a hand on the roof.

“One more race when you return.”

“A fair one. No collapsing trains. No overloaded rings. No rescuing anybody halfway through.”

“You asking a lot from the environment.”

He laughed.

Then he hugged me, hard.

“Thank you,” he said quietly.

I held him a second longer.

“Stay magnificent.”

He stepped back and smiled.

“You make it difficult to do anything else.”

The invitation arrived before I could get into the car.

A black airship descended beyond the front gate. Its side carried a gold crown above a checkered flag. A woman in a white driving suit came down the steps holding a long envelope on a silver tray.

Tank watched her approach.

“Nigga, another school?”

“I can't keep graduating. We running out of wall.”

Victor's smile faded slightly.

“Cruel,” he said.

Storm looked at the crown on the airship.

“An ambitious host.”

Inside lay a heavy card showing a coastline, a harbor full of yachts, and a race circuit curling between pale buildings beneath a blue Mediterranean sky. The winner would receive a prize fleet and the deed to a private island called Isla Gloria. The invitation promised a month of racing, music, luxury, and competition among people who considered losing a personal insult.

Nigga, they had written me a holiday.

There was a second card for Shawna.

An invitation to headline one of the evening performances.

And one for Rosa.

“Aiden.”

“I see it.”

Shawna studied the picture of the ocean.

Not the yachts.

The water.

She looked at me.

“Nigga, it says island too.”

Victor shook his head with a grin that had returned in full.

The messenger looked from me to the Lamborghini, to the statue behind us, to the school full of people still waiting to wave goodbye.

“Tell that nigga to make room.”

Then I got into the Lamborghini.

Shawna sat beside me. Tank and Rosa climbed into the Mercedes. Mercedes and Porsche took their red convertible. Marcellus and Vega would travel with us as far as the city below before the whole crew arranged the next flight together.

The Heart remained above us, holding Sky High where it belonged.

The time door remained gone.

My new power rested quietly in my hands, a little blue promise with very clear limits.

The engine needed none of it to sound magnificent.

I started the car.

The whole school cheered.

We rolled down the open ramp this time. The proper one. The road shone beneath the tires and curved gently through the clouds. Behind us the academy rose into sunlight, scarred, repaired, loud with people who had earned the right to enjoy their own morning.

Shawna took my hand off the gearshift and held it against her knee.

“You happy?” she asked.

I looked at my wife. At the ruby. At the watch. At the road opening beyond the windshield.

Then I looked in the mirror and saw my people following.

“Nigga,” I said, “look how much world we still got.”

She smiled.

I put my foot down.

The clouds opened, and we went toward the sea.

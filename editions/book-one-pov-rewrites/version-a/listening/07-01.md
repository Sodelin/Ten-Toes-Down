## 7. The Man Who Cut the Heart Out of a Mountain

Before Shawna got much farther, my body interrupted her.

I shifted in the chair and couldn't keep the pain off my face. She called Rosa back, and between the women and Tank the story moved upstairs. Tank took my coat, draped it over a chair, and helped me onto the bed. Rosa followed with Shawna and took her into the bathroom. I heard complaints about the number of pins, then Rosa emerged carrying the silver dress and Shawna came back in a robe. By then I was propped against the pillows with one leg straight and the other bent because apparently that was the arrangement under which my ribs would permit information.

Shawna put the blue invitations on the bedside table and sat beside me. Dante's watch rested loose against her hand.

She told me their wedding room had rose petals all over the bed. Dante had begun gathering them into the ice bucket as soon as he locked the door.

“I asked if they were bothering him,” Shawna said. “He said they had dirt on them.”

She smiled a little at the memory, then repeated his answer in her own quiet voice: Dante hadn't wanted her sleeping in dirt. She had laughed and kicked off her shoes. According to her, he watched the shoes land as though he hadn't quite believed she was going to stay.

That fucked with me more than the picture of the wedding bed. I had looked at things she set down in my house the same way. The watch beside a lamp. A bag left open on a chair. Little proofs that she might mean to be there in the morning.

I asked her to go on.

Dante had sat beside the ice bucket and told her about prison. Nine months of his life, a little more than three years before the afternoon when I heard the story. He had been twenty-eight. Shawna made a point of giving me the age, as if she wanted me to see a man then rather than only the one she had buried.

His first afternoon, he saw another prisoner spill soup down an older man's shirt.

The older man looked down at the stain and kept eating. The man who'd done it stayed beside his table. Dante told Shawna the silence bothered him before the violence did: a whole room carrying on as if everyone already knew which person was allowed to do this to which other person.

Shawna described the bully leaning over the older prisoner's bowl, telling him to look up. When the old man finally put down his spoon, he pointed to the bully's hand.

“You have my soup on your hand,” Shawna quoted him.

The man slapped him.

Shawna drew a breath. “Dante said he got up. Next thing he remembered, he had the man against the wall and blood on his shirt. He was telling somebody to bring the old man another bowl.”

I looked at her. “What happened between those things?”

Shawna gestured from one end of the bed to the other. “I told you what happened.”

I shook my head. “Told me somebody got hit. Skipped where the nigga found out.”

She stared at me for a moment. Then Shawna settled back against the pillows and pulled her robe clear of her knees.

“The man had two friends,” she told me.

I made myself comfortable enough to pay the proper attention. “There we go.”

One friend moved into the aisle when Dante crossed between the tables. Dante described him to Shawna as a big, shaved-headed man of forty, with a towel over his shoulder. The man planted himself in Dante's way and ordered him to sit down.

Dante kept coming. The man asked whether he was deaf.

“Dante told him no,” Shawna said. “Then asked if he was finished.”

The man swung. His fist caught Dante across the jaw. Dante still had his food tray in both hands, and he drove its edge into the man's face. The man folded over the end of a bench with both hands against his nose. Food flew across the aisle. A cup fell beneath somebody's shoe. Men stood around them, benches scraping the floor.

The soup-spiller grabbed the older man's collar and pulled him halfway off the seat. Now the table lay between Dante and the two of them. Shawna held one hand out to show me the distance, then lifted it across the bed.

“He went over it,” she said.

In my head, I saw the movement. Feet leaving the floor. Hands reaching before there was any guarantee of landing properly. I'd made that choice myself: a person needed help, there was space in the way, and for one stupid second you believed you could settle the matter of space by refusing it.

I didn't enjoy finding that much of myself in her husband. I listened anyway.

Dante hit the man holding the prisoner with both knees. They went down together. The older man fell against the bench, steadied himself, and reached beneath the table for his spoon.

“He got the spoon?” I asked Shawna.

She nodded. “He liked that spoon.”

I rested my hand over the cross on my chest. “Respect an attachment.”

The last man came behind Dante with a chair raised. According to Dante's account, the older prisoner put his foot against the man's ankle as he passed. The man's step broke. His chair struck the table instead of Dante's head, and Dante turned into him before he could lift it again.

Shawna showed me how Dante said he'd caught the chair. One hand against its back, the other closing where a leg met the seat. He tore it sideways. The man held on, so Dante drove it back into his ribs and shoved him against the table. Dante had broken a finger somewhere in the fight. He hadn't known which movement did it until the guards were holding him and it began to hurt.

The man who'd spilled the soup tried to rise. Dante hauled him up the rest of the way and put him against the wall. That was the part Shawna had given me first: blood on Dante's shirt, his hand at the man's collar, his voice carrying across the room asking for another fucking bowl.

The guards arrived. Dante let the bully drop. Three men were on or near the floor, and the old prisoner stood by his table with the spoon in his hand.

Shawna told me a guard ordered Dante toward the door.

“He said the old man hadn't eaten,” she said. “The guard told him to move again.”

The older prisoner touched Dante's shoulder and said he could wait. Dante looked at the empty bowl, picked it up, and put it into the guard's hand.

“So can they,” Shawna quoted Dante, nodding toward the place she'd used to indicate the men on the floor.

She smiled after saying it. I let her have the smile. Her dead man had earned that much.

I held up three fingers. “Three.”

Shawna looked at my hand. “Three.”

I lowered it. “I had twelve.”

She tapped my good shoulder with a pillow, gently enough that the bullet declined to comment.

“You asked for the story,” Shawna reminded me.

I caught the pillow before she could put it back. “Can listen and hold the record.”

She laughed. I put the pillow behind her, and when she leaned against it, her shoulder rested against mine. For a moment, hearing about Dante didn't feel like being pushed out of my own bed.

The fight had bought him punishment and a friendship.

The old man's name was Marcellus Vale.

When Dante returned, Marcellus sat on his bunk with a little paper box. Inside were a broken comb, some buttons, and a gold-colored wrapper folded into a heart. He offered Dante the heart for his trouble.

Shawna made the shape between her fingers. “Dante asked what he was supposed to do with it. Marcellus told him to give it to somebody.”

Dante had asked whether he meant a woman. Marcellus said that depended on whether Dante liked women. Dante assured him that he did.

Shawna lifted her chin slightly as she repeated the old man's answer. “Then perhaps one will like a heart.”

I looked at her hands. I could picture the little folded wrapper more easily than forty million dollars. Somebody confined to a room had still found a way to make a gift with what the room allowed him.

The folds were so fine that Dante asked whether Marcellus made jewelry. Marcellus said he used to. Dante admitted that he used to steal it.

“Then we may have met professionally,” Shawna quoted Marcellus.

Dante laughed. The old man shifted along the bunk so he could sit down.

They ate together for two months. Marcellus taught him cards. Dante offered advice about which men in the prison were dangerous and which ones mainly needed other people to look frightened. I asked Shawna how long Marcellus had already been inside.

“Years,” she told me.

I shook my head at the ceiling. “Dante gave him the tour anyway?”

Shawna rested her head against the pillow. “You never told somebody something they already knew?”

I looked back at her. “Not in the last few seconds.”

She went on before I could improve that record.

At night, Dante saw Marcellus holding a photograph where the corridor light reached his bunk. The picture showed a woman on a little stage, singing with her eyes closed. A blue stone rested at the base of her throat.

Celeste. His wife.

When Dante said she was beautiful, Marcellus said he knew. When Dante asked whether she still visited, Marcellus turned the photograph over and answered no.

Dante left it alone until a letter arrived. Marcellus carried the unopened envelope through dinner. Dante offered to read it for him, but the old man said he already knew what it said.

Shawna glanced toward the invitations on my bedside table. “Dante asked why they sent it, then.”

She gave me Marcellus's answer slowly: some people thought they could finish a love affair by closing a bank account.

Celeste had died three months earlier. Dante learned that sitting on the opposite bunk, looking at an envelope the old man couldn't bring himself to open. It wasn't the letter from Celeste that I would read later. It was one more official piece of business arriving after the person who might have made it bearable was gone.

After a while, Dante asked him about the stone in the photograph.

That was how the story reached Shawna, and then me: Marcellus talking in prison, Dante repeating it in their wedding room, Shawna sitting against my pillows with his watch on. I was hearing a life through the people who had held on to it. I kept reminding myself of that when I wanted to demand a date, a name, a piece of proof she couldn't possibly pull out of her robe.

Marcellus had bought the rough stone years before the prison conversation. The seller brought it in an empty cigarette packet. He paid enough to empty his account, fought with two men who thought they deserved a share, and came home with a bruise spreading across his jaw.

Celeste was frying chicken. According to the story Marcellus told Dante, she turned down the burner when she saw his face and asked how much he'd spent.

He put the cigarette packet on the kitchen table and asked her to look first.

Shawna held her hand flat as if setting it down herself. “She asked him again. How much, Marcellus?”

Then Celeste opened the packet. Marcellus described the rough blue surface to Dante as something that looked as if a piece of sky had been buried in dirt. I had only Red's price in my head until Shawna told me that. The little packet and the chicken pan made the number feel absurd in a different way.

Celeste looked at the stone, then at her husband.

“We are not eating that,” Shawna quoted her.

Marcellus held it toward the window. Light passed through and laid a blue shape across Celeste's white sleeve. She stopped talking. He remembered that she said only one little word when she saw it: oh.

He watched her instead of the stone. That was what he later told Dante. It mattered to me that he'd once known where to look without being reminded.

Cutting the diamond took months. Marcellus worked in a room behind the house. Celeste sang at a lounge that barely paid her and brought him food between sets and errands. He told Dante about finding meals cold beside his tools because he'd forgotten she had come in.

At the end of the first month, she brought his pillow to the workshop.

At the end of the second, she put a photograph of herself beside his lamp.

Shawna reached toward our bedside lamp as she gave me Celeste's explanation. “So you remember who the fuck lives out here.”

I laughed before I meant to. Shawna looked back at me, and I put my hand down from the cross. I had been holding it again.

At the end of the third month, Marcellus put on his good suit and went to the lounge.

Shawna described him walking toward the stage with a small box in his hands. Celeste was halfway through a song. The pianist saw him coming and lost his place. Celeste turned, saw her husband, and spoke into the microphone.

“Don't propose again,” Shawna quoted Celeste. “I fell for that shit once.”

People laughed. Marcellus reached the steps and told her he'd made something for her. She came down to him. He opened the box.

Shawna couldn't show me the blue he had described to Dante. Dante hadn't been able to show it to her either. On their wedding night, she said, he'd held her hand beneath a lamp and tried to explain how the color deepened when Marcellus moved it away from the light. It looked wet even when you knew it wasn't. I watched the light on Shawna's fingers while she told me, trying to follow what three people had meant by the same color.

Celeste put her fingers over her mouth. Marcellus lifted out the necklace and asked her to turn around. He fastened it behind her neck. Small pale stones surrounded the blue one, making it appear darker against her skin.

Shawna touched the base of her own throat. “She asked what he called it.”

Marcellus answered Blue Mercy. Celeste asked what needed mercy.

“The last three months,” Shawna quoted him.

She told me Celeste put her arms around him and kissed him in front of the room. For all the money I had thrown at the house around us, that was the part I kept imagining: a man finally coming out of a little room and letting his wife touch him again.

Red had been in the lounge with Valentino. Twenty-eight then. Marcellus identified both men in the story he told Dante. I wondered whether they'd clapped along with the room. Knowing what followed made that possibility harder to stomach.

I wanted to interrupt Shawna. I wanted to ask whether Dante had kissed better, promised more, understood faster. Those were the questions rising in me while she told me about a man who had spent months forgetting his wife for a stone.

I kept my mouth shut.

For once, I wanted to hear what happened more than I wanted to hear myself.

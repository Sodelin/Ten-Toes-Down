On the third afternoon, somebody delivered a coffin.

The nurse called from the private entrance. She sounded offended, which I respected. She worked in an expensive hospital, and someone had rolled a direct criticism of the establishment through the doors.

She said there was an item for me. I asked whether it was flowers. Larger than flowers, she said. I tried piano next.

“It is a coffin, Mr. Aiden,” the nurse said over the phone.

Across the room, Tank stood so fast his food hit the floor.

I told the nurse not to open it.

“I wasn't planning to try it on,” the nurse told me.

Shawna took the phone from me and began telling her to move everyone away. I reached for my trousers. Tank told me no. I told him to get my shoes. He pointed out that I already had hospital socks.

“Then get shoes worthy of the fucking socks,” I told Tank.

Mercedes and Porsche were visiting. They had brought fruit, champagne nobody was allowed to open, and an enlarged photograph of the Mercedes in the air above the boat. Somebody on the sidewalk had caught the jump. You could see Shawna screaming and me concentrating with tremendous visual appeal.

Mercedes went to the window. I pulled myself upright and joined her, one hand against the wall. Through the glass entrance below us, I could see two men beside a black lacquer coffin on a trolley. Silver handles gleamed along the sides. My name crossed the lid in letters big enough to make dying seem like a sponsorship opportunity.

“Even your enemies know you’re expensive,” she said.

Porsche stood beside her counting the men near two white sedans across the private drive.

“Four more by the cars,” Porsche said. “Those two aren't delivering shit.”

I looked beyond the coffin toward the white sedans. The men outside kept watching the entrance instead of the street. One adjusted his jacket without taking his eyes off it. The men by the coffin reached beneath theirs.

Tank dragged me back as the first shot broke glass below us.

The hospital changed in a breath. Alarms started. Somebody screamed beneath the balcony. A man ordered people onto the floor. In the hall outside our room, the brisk sounds of ordinary work became running feet.

I took my pistol from the clothes piled beside Tank's chair. I told Shawna to take Rosa into the bathroom and lock the door.

She started to argue. I caught her eyes.

“Be alive when I come back so you can cuss me out,” I told Shawna.

She pressed her lips together, caught Rosa's hand, and went into the bathroom. Mercedes followed them with a heavy vase in both hands, taking a piece of the hospital’s expensive taste into the defensive plan.

Porsche stayed by the door and pointed at the wheelchair.

“The man can't walk fast,” Porsche told Tank. “He can roll.”

I had spent most of the argument defending my feet. Porsche had found me wheels. I appreciated that.

Tank helped me into the chair and pulled my shoes on over the socks. Porsche pushed me into the hallway while he checked the corner ahead. A nurse was struggling with a bed farther along. The patient had one leg in a cast and was trying to hold the back of his gown closed while she steered. The bed kept catching the wall because every time he reached for the rail, his ass became a public matter.

I told Porsche to help them. She wanted to know who would push me.

“I got hands,” I told Porsche, setting them on the wheel rims.

She caught the foot of the bed before it struck the corner. I laid the pistol across my lap, gripped the wheel rims, and followed Tank. Each turn pulled at my side. The chair traveled about as fast as a man pretending the pain wasn't deciding the route. Whenever Tank stopped, I took the gun up again.

A gunman came through the stairwell door at the far end. He stopped facing Tank. Then his eyes dropped to my chair, and he smiled.

“That's the king?” the gunman asked, pointing at me.

“That’s the nigga,” Tank said.

The man lifted his gun. Tank threw himself against the wall, leaving a clear line between me and the weapon. I leaned past the arm of the chair and fired. The gunman fell backward through the stairwell door, and his weapon skidded across the polished floor. More feet sounded on the stairs behind him.

Porsche and the nurse got the bed around the corner. The old man had both hands clamped over his gown. I told him not to look down.

“Wasn't planning to, son,” the old man told me, gripping his gown.

“Good,” I answered. “Your nigga's got this.”

A shot struck the sprinkler above us. Water burst across the ceiling and fell over the corridor. Tank seized the back of my chair and ran.

The wheels rattled over seams. Water hit my face. I held my wounded side with one hand and kept the pistol up with the other. I twisted in the chair and saw two men come around the corner behind us. The man I had shot carried one arm low; the other held his pistol out in both hands. Their shoes slid as they tried to chase across the wet floor.

I shouted Tank’s name. He said he saw them.

“Then quit sightseeing and push!” I shouted up at Tank.

We struck the swinging doors to the atrium and went through onto an upper gallery overlooking the entrance. The nurse and Porsche brought the bed after us. To our left, stairs curved down beside the wall; a service lift stood in a recessed opening beyond them. Both routes emerged near the main floor’s far exit. Below, the gunmen at the coffin controlled that stretch of open floor.

The coffin and its trolley were now in the shallow fountain beneath the gallery. One delivery man crouched behind it. The other stood in the water with his gun aimed up at us.

I fired before he did. His raised arm dropped, and he fell against the fountain's rim. His gun hit the water beyond his reach. The man behind the coffin shot back. Tank pulled my chair away from the edge as the shots struck the tiles and cracked a section of the rail. The chair tipped. I caught the arm, went over with it, and landed on one knee. My lungs emptied so completely I couldn't make a sound about what my side was doing.

Tank fired back through the gallery doors, forcing the men pursuing us into the corridor. Porsche stood in front of the nurse and her patient with a chair raised across her chest. The lift was only a few yards away, but I could see the gunman below watching its doors. He could catch them when they reached the ground floor. Staying here meant waiting for the men behind us to come through again.

I looked down at the coffin with my name on it.

“Who sent it?” I shouted.

The man using it for cover looked up. He actually answered.

“Who you think?” the gunman below called back.

“I think your taste is fucking awful,” I told him.

I kicked the overturned wheelchair toward the broken section of rail. It scraped across the wet tile, tipped into space, and fell into the fountain. The frame hit the coffin and shoved it sideways through the water. Its trolley buckled. The gunman sprang out from behind it, and Tank fired from above, driving him behind a marble column.

That opened the route to the lift. It didn't keep it open. The man could come back around the column before the bed reached the floor, and the gunmen in the corridor were still trying to get through behind us.

I got my feet under me and stood. The pain stood too. That motherfucker was committed.

Tank followed my gaze over the broken rail.

“Don't you dare,” he warned me.

“Get them down,” I told him. “Catch up.”

I went over.

The coffin lid took my feet in the middle of my own engraved name. Black wood split. Water burst over the sides. My legs bent farther than I wanted, and a white shock ran up through the wound. I stayed upright by putting a hand against the broken lid and refusing to consider what the other option would cost.

People lay behind furniture all across the lobby. They stared at me standing in a coffin in a fountain with a gun. I had come to this hospital for rest. The reviews were going to be complicated.

The man behind the column charged.

He didn't fire. I never learned whether his gun was empty or he had simply decided the wounded man ought to accept delivery. I tore a loose length of the lid upward and swung it into his shoulder.

The wood cracked. He fell against me. We went into the fountain together, his hands reaching for my throat while the water closed over my mouth. My side opened into a kind of pain I couldn't separate from the struggle. I drove him away with my forearm, came up coughing, and brought the pistol close enough to his face for him to stop.

I asked whether he was finished. He nodded, lying half against the broken coffin.

“Use your words,” I told the man in the fountain.

“Finished,” he said, keeping his hands where I could see them.

“Then stay fucking finished,” I told him.

Tank came down the stairs while Porsche and the nurse used the service lift with the bed. He reached the fountain first, kept his gun on the man, and gave me an arm thick enough to trust my full weight to. I had no strength left for making the climb look good.

I put my pistol back in my waistband while Tank held me upright.

The lift opened across the lobby. Porsche checked the way before the nurse pushed the bed toward the exit. The old man lifted a fist at me as they passed.

“That's my boy!” the old man shouted from the bed.

I had never seen him before that morning. I heard pride in his shout and took it anyway. I was collecting fathers faster than the bad ones could die.

Through the broken entrance I saw one of the men from the upstairs corridor burst out of a side door and run to the second white sedan. Another man already had the first sedan running. Both tried to leave. The lead car reached the gate just as a police cruiser struck it broadside. The second stopped behind it with nowhere to go. A woman came out of the cruiser with a long gun. Her dark glasses stayed exactly where they belonged while she shouted for the men to show their hands.

Every hand in those cars appeared with remarkable enthusiasm.

Chief Gloria Knox came through the broken entrance a couple of minutes later. She was fifty-three, as I learned afterward, with silver at her temples and a blue uniform so crisp it looked personally disappointed in every wet surface around her. She checked the fountain, the coffin, the broken gallery rail, and finally me.

She asked whether I had jumped from there.

“Nigga was in my way,” I told Knox.

“And that solved it?” she asked, looking from me to the broken coffin.

The man in the fountain groaned behind Tank.

Knox looked at him. “Apparently.”

I heard an officer call that the corridor was clear. Then Vega arrived with Shawna, Rosa, and Mercedes. My doctor saw my wet trousers and the blood spreading beneath my shirt. She said my name in a tone that made the bullet feel almost conversational.

I told her everybody around us was alive.

“You were in bed,” Vega said to me.

“Didn't have wheels,” I explained, leaning harder on Tank.

Porsche raised a hand. “I tried a chair.”

Vega shut her eyes. When she opened them again, she looked past me at the nurse beside the bed. The nurse was shaking now that the movement had stopped.

“You saved my nurse,” Vega said.

“She saved my ass first,” I told Vega, nodding toward the nurse.

The nurse began crying. The old man demanded to shake my hand. I offered the one that wasn't braced against Tank and tried not to let him see how little grip remained in it.

Knox put a card where I could take it.

“When you're tired of people sending coffins, come see me,” Knox said, holding out the card. “I'd rather give you a badge.”

Tank stared at her. “This nigga?”

“Especially this nigga,” Knox told Tank.

Before I could decide what to say to that, Shawna got her arms around me. She laughed, called me an impossible motherfucker, and kissed my face in front of the chief of police. Her hands moved carefully around the dressing, though she kept telling me what she ought to do to my ass. I stood with fountain water running out of my clothes while the people around us watched. I felt as if I had arrived from somewhere higher than the gallery.

They put me in another chair. Vega supervised that part personally.

That evening, I watched from my window as the broken coffin left on the back of a truck. Two of the captured attackers sat beside it in handcuffs. The man from the fountain kept looking up at my window. I raised my hand in a small wave. He looked away before I had finished it.

Then I let Vega put me back in bed. I had run out of arguments and, more seriously, out of the ability to remain upright while making them.

The days that followed did not resemble a victory montage. I slept. I woke sore. I learned that crossing the room and sitting down again could constitute a full morning’s work. By the time five days had passed in the hospital, Vega allowed me to leave. She made it clear that leaving meant she trusted my people to keep me reasonably still. It did not mean my body had forgiven me.

“No heroics,” she said at the door.

Tank waited behind my wheelchair with two duffel bags and Rosa’s red coat. Before we left the room, Shawna lifted Dante’s chain off her own neck and settled it around mine again, easing the cross flat against the clean shirt. Then she promised Vega I would behave.

Vega turned to her. “You either.”

Shawna nodded. Vega held her eyes until she nodded again. I watched their agreement take shape and felt several of my preferred methods leave the building without me.

Downstairs, six black cars waited under the entrance canopy. Men straightened beside them when Tank rolled me out. One opened the Mercedes door and welcomed me back.

Back. As if I had possessed a kingdom before I had a woman. As if these men hadn't learned my name while I bled into somebody else’s sheets. The word carried expectations I wasn't strong enough to lift, so I concentrated on the smaller task directly in front of me.

I stood up.

I wanted Shawna to see me do it. When my knees trembled, she took my arm and held it firmly against her side. I hoped the men by the cars would mistake the help for simple affection.

“Where we going?” Shawna asked, keeping hold of my arm.

Beyond the canopy, the city moved in bright afternoon traffic. We had people, cars, money, enemies, and a bullet that would apparently be joining all future plans. The one ordinary thing we did not possess was somewhere that belonged to both of us.

I looked down at her.

“Home, baby,” I told her. “We just gotta get one.”

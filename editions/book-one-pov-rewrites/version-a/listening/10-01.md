## 10. Forty Million and One Woman

The morning after I graduated, four men robbed a casino while I was buying coffee in uniform.

That was poor timing. Parking their van across my Lamborghini's bumper was personal.

I stood outside the coffee shop holding two cups. Shawna waited in the passenger seat with sunglasses on and my captain's jacket over her dress. She'd borrowed it because, she told me, the gold braid felt good against her wrists. The department hadn't owned me long enough to begin inventorying what she'd taken.

Across the street, an alarm sounded. Four men came out of the casino carrying bags. One kept a pistol pointed back toward the doors. Another had a split lip. The biggest carried a bag in each hand and kept looking toward their van, which occupied the space my car needed if I intended to go anywhere with the coffee.

They crossed the road between stopped cars, the gunman still pointing back at the casino. The man who turned out to be the driver hurried ahead and opened the rear doors. I looked at him, then at the cups, then at Shawna.

“Hold these,” I told her.

She took both through the open window. “Getting involved?”

I stepped away from the car. “Involved me when they parked.”

The driver stood by the open back of the van, waving his men toward it. He saw me coming and laughed.

“Keep walking, pretty boy,” the driver said.

I turned so he could see the badge on my uniform shirt. His eyes went to it. The laugh stopped coming out of his mouth and stayed there as an unhelpful shape.

“Police?” the driver asked.

I looked past him at my blocked bumper. “Captain.”

He looked at my face again. “Don't look like police.”

I pointed at the van. “Police improving. Move it.”

The armed man had a short beard and a pistol coming up toward my chest. I caught the open rear door and drove it into his forearm. The shot struck the street beside us. I pushed him against the van's side panel and knocked him down to one knee.

The driver ran for the front. The big man tossed his bags inside, grabbed the bearded gunman beneath his arms, and hauled him through the back of the van. The man with the split lip climbed in after them. All four were aboard when the engine rose and the van lurched forward, its rear doors still open.

A money bag rolled out and burst against the curb. The split-lip man reached for another one sliding after it. I caught the rear door handle as the van moved past me.

“Aiden!” Shawna shouted from the car.

I looked back once. “Coffee!”

I wasn't going to let these men ruin eight dollars of breakfast and a police jacket before I'd properly worn either one out.

The van pulled me around the corner. My shoes struck the curb, found purchase for one step, then slipped. I kept hold of the handle with both hands, got a foot onto the rear step, and hauled my knee up beside it.

The man with the split lip kicked toward my face. I ducked under his shoe and caught the doorframe.

“Let go!” the split-lip man shouted.

I pulled myself high enough to meet his eyes. “You let go.”

He kicked again. “It's our van!”

I caught his ankle. “Should've moved it.”

I came through the opening shoulder first, driving him backward into the money bags. The bearded gunman sat against the side panel holding the arm I'd struck. The biggest man crouched among the bags, trying to keep them from rolling out around us.

The driver looked back at the noise. The van climbed halfway onto the sidewalk. A newspaper stand burst against the windshield, and pages flew past the open rear doors. I saw my own graduation photograph turn over in the air before the paper went beneath a wheel.

Yesterday's miracle was having trouble with this morning's circulation.

The man I'd knocked onto the bags reached toward his waistband. I put my foot on his wrist and drew my own pistol.

“Don't,” I told him.

He looked up. His eyes moved over my face properly for the first time.

“You're Aiden,” the split-lip man said.

I kept my weight on his wrist. “Easier day knowing that at the coffee shop.”

The big man raised both hands when I looked toward him. The bearded gunman let his pistol fall beside his knee. I kicked it across the floor behind the driver's seat and braced myself against a shelf as the van turned.

Concrete closed over the daylight. The driver had taken us into a parking structure. Tires squealed on the first ramp. Through the open doors I could see a central well between two sets of ramps, cars parked around the outer edge, arrows on the floor going in opposite directions.

Then I heard my Lamborghini.

Shawna came through the entrance behind us with the siren on. She sat forward over the wheel, sunglasses still in place. I held the van's shelf with one hand and watched the blue nose appear at the foot of the ramp as though the garage had ordered something beautiful to correct its morning.

We reached the second floor. Then the third. On each turn, the driver glanced back through the open van.

“Still behind us!” the driver shouted.

I watched Shawna take the turn below. “She's thorough.”

The driver faced forward again. “Who asked you?”

I tightened my hold as the van tipped into another turn. “Brought me.”

Shawna cut across a parking level toward the other side of the central well. Through the open space between the ramps, I saw the Lamborghini climbing against the painted arrows while our van followed the longer turn around the parked cars. Then she disappeared behind a pillar. At the next turn, the Lamborghini came up the neighboring ramp and swung across the connecting lane directly ahead of us.

The driver hit the brakes.

The van slid. I caught the back of the passenger seat with my free hand and kept the pistol pointed away from the men tumbling behind me. The three in the cargo space struck the bags in a heap. It sounded financially painful.

Through the windshield, I saw Shawna get out of the Lamborghini with her silver pistol. She stood beside the door, keeping the car between herself and the van's driver.

“Everybody out,” Shawna called.

The driver stared at her. “Police too?”

I looked through the windshield at my woman. “Worse. Disappointed.”

He raised both hands.

We got all four men onto the concrete. I kept the driver where I could see him while Shawna watched the others. She told me she'd called for officers as soon as she entered the garage. Ten minutes later, Knox's people came up the ramp to find the money in the van and my Lamborghini across the lane like the city had finally bought itself an answer with good lines.

Once the officers had all four men in cuffs, I put my pistol away. A sergeant with a gray mustache shook my hand. I asked him to leave enough of it functioning for the coffee.

“Been after this crew for months, Captain,” the sergeant told me.

I looked at the van. “Parked wrong.”

He studied its position, then my car. “Should've tried that.”

Shawna brought my cup over. It was still warm. I looked inside the Lamborghini and saw the other cup upright in its holder, my jacket spread neatly over the back of her seat. Not a drop. She'd chased a van full of robbers and preserved breakfast while doing it. Every useful thought I'd ever had about marriage stood up to applaud.

The sergeant saluted her.

“Ma'am,” he said.

Shawna held her coffee away from the gesture. “Can call me Shawna.”

The sergeant nodded. “Yes, ma'am.”

Respect had declined her suggestion.

The casino owner came to the station before lunch. I was wearing my jacket again by then, though Shawna had told me she would need it back when the air-conditioning offended her. The owner brought two gold watches and an envelope. He kept trying to put everything into my hands at once.

Two of his employees sat nearby waiting to give statements. One held an ice pack against the side of his face. The other had a bandage over scraped knuckles. The robbers had knocked them down on the way out. I gave a watch to each of them and kept the envelope long enough to buy lunch for the whole shift.

Officers carried plates through the station and took turns getting pictures beside the Lamborghini. Knox watched from her office doorway. When I came past, she looked toward the car.

“First morning?” Knox asked.

I held up my cup. “Need bigger holders.”

Knox laughed. “Anything else?”

I leaned against the doorframe. “Raise.”

Knox glanced at the clock. “Four hours.”

I gestured toward the lunch arriving at the next desk. “Look what the four hours did for you.”

By evening, people all over the station called me by name. By the following afternoon, several had offered to wait outside the Saint in case my evening needed company. I told them to stay close to Knox. I wanted Red looking at the badge, wondering how much of the city stood behind the man wearing it.

That afternoon was the day of the gala. I went to buy a white suit.

The tailor began with black. When that failed, he brought out dark blue. Shawna sat behind me in a little velvet chair, drinking champagne and telling him I looked handsome every time he asked. She had not looked up from her phone once. I could have been wearing a fucking curtain.

I put my hand against the white cloth I'd chosen. “This. Want the whole room seeing who came.”

The tailor's eyes went to the bandage beneath my shirt. “With your injury—”

I looked at him in the mirror. “Suit going to heal me?”

He turned back toward the cloth. The conversation became more productive immediately.

Tank stood by the door in a black suit we'd bought forty minutes earlier. He reached toward a bowl of peppermints. A seam opened across his shoulder with a dry little ripping sound. The tailor paused, then continued smoothing my sleeve as though the noise had happened in another business.

I looked past him. “Get Tank another one.”

The tailor glanced at the torn shoulder. “Larger?”

I held my arms out while he worked. “Whatever size a cathedral is.”

Tank put the peppermint back.

I watched him in the mirror. “Eat. Rich enough for the candy and whatever seam comes with it.”

Shawna laughed then. She looked up at me, phone forgotten against her skirt. For one moment I wasn't trying to read Dante or Red or a missing fortune in her face. My woman was laughing because I stood full of pins and meant to dress Tank like a building.

I would have bought an actual church to keep her there.

The waiting had been harder than the public disasters. Graduation. A badge. The van. Those gave me something to do with my hands. The calls to Red required me to sit beside Shawna while she sounded like a woman considering him again.

There had been more calls after the one we'd planned at the dining table. Once she called him baby. I knew why. I had agreed to the plan. Every intelligent sentence I'd spoken in support of it left my body when I heard that word come out of her mouth.

Afterward, I followed her from the bed to the window.

“Don't have to say it like that,” I told Shawna.

She turned with the phone still in her hand. “Like what?”

I pointed at her face. “With your mouth.”

She stared at me for a few seconds.

“Aiden,” Shawna said.

I folded my arms. “He's got a name. Use it.”

She set the phone down and kissed me. I let the argument end there. The next night I made the same complaint, and she kissed me again. By the third time I began it, she put her hands on my waist and told me I could come over for a kiss without starting a fight first.

I denied the accusation while moving closer.

Now, in the tailor's shop, her phone lit again. I watched her answer while the man knelt beside my shoes.

“Him?” I asked Shawna.

She kept typing. “Rosa.”

I tilted my head toward the screen. “Let me see.”

She held it up. Six gowns lay across a bed in the photograph. Rosa wanted to know whether I was paying for all of it.

I nodded. “Tell her yes.”

Shawna looked back at the picture. “Don't know how much.”

I faced the mirror again. “Capital fucking letters.”

Shawna sent the message, put her phone away, and came around the tailor to straighten my collar. She smoothed one edge, then the other. I watched her hands in the glass.

“Allowed to be scared tonight,” Shawna told me.

I looked over her head at my reflection. “I'm not.”

She pressed the collar flat. “Okay.”

I looked down at her. “You are?”

Shawna met my eyes. “Yes.”

When she started to step back, I took her hand gently. Dante's watch slid against my fingers. I let it stay there.

“Then I'm scared too,” I told her.

She kissed the corner of my mouth that wasn't cut.

“Good,” Shawna said. “Maybe you'll listen.”

I listened to everything. The difficulty came afterward, when I sometimes decided the world needed correcting for what I'd heard.

At home, Rosa had turned our bedroom into a place where my belongings existed only until a woman needed the space. Shoes occupied the chairs. Perfume hung in the air. A champagne bottle stood in the bathroom sink beside my toothbrush. Someone had moved my spare gun into its case to make room for a heated hairbrush.

I stopped in the doorway. Rosa looked up from a pair of earrings and asked why I was there.

“Sleep here,” I told her.

Rosa pointed toward the hall. “Not while we're busy.”

She wore red with diamonds at her throat. Shawna sat farther inside with her hair being pinned up. I could see one eye in the mirror and the beginning of a smile when Rosa tried to send me away.

I put a hand against the doorframe. “Need Shawna.”

Rosa addressed her reflection. “Your nigga needs patience.”

I lifted my free hand. “Bought some. Arrives tomorrow.”

Rosa picked up a shoe. I went downstairs before she could explain the shipping options.

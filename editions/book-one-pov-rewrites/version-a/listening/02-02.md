The valet had the Mercedes waiting. I put the case on Shawna’s lap, got behind the wheel, and pulled away from the entrance. I had barely started learning where the controls lived when she looked in her mirror and said my name.

“Don't look,” Shawna told me, holding her eyes on the mirror.

Naturally I looked. I had owned this Mercedes for eleven minutes. I was inspecting everything that approached it.

A black Charger followed with its lights off. Two men sat in front. In the back, a third leaned across the seat with something long in his hands.

The rear window burst.

Shawna folded over the case. I ducked, jerked the wheel, and clipped the edge of a fountain with the right fender. Water rose over the hood and washed across three men in suits standing with their chests out at the entrance. Their posture became considerably less formal. We struck the street still moving. A horn screamed beneath our own engine.

I shouted to ask whether the men were hers.

“You think I'd ride with you if those were my niggas?” Shawna shouted back.

“You met me tonight!” I reminded her, dragging the wheel straight.

“I'm trying to meet you tomorrow!” She pointed at the open road. “Drive!”

A yellow Ferrari slid out of the next street and joined the Charger. It came level on my side. The driver smiled at me while his passenger raised a pistol. That smile bothered me. There should be a limit to how comfortable a man gets while doing this shit.

Shawna had braced one hand against the center of the wheel when the glass broke. I pointed at it.

“Baby, move your hand,” I told her. “The horn. You're leaning on the horn.”

She lifted her wrist. The screaming stopped. In the sudden space it left, I heard the Ferrari’s engine climbing beside mine.

I turned toward it.

The gunman pulled his pistol up against his chest. Beside him, the driver's hands jerked on the wheel. Yellow metal scraped our side, caught the street pole, and spun away across a valet entrance. Sparks flew beneath the car. Men in expensive shoes climbed onto the backs of other men in expensive shoes to get out of its path.

Shawna stared after it. “That’s a Ferrari!”

“That’s one Ferrari,” I told her. “Keep up.”

The Charger still filled the mirror. The man in the back shifted his long gun into another position, and before I could decide how much road I had to work with, a red convertible burst out of a hotel drive between us.

Mercedes stood in its passenger seat, holding her heels together in one hand.

“I told you about my fucking flowers, Aiden!” Mercedes shouted from the red convertible.

Porsche drove. She had told me she was twenty-nine at the party where we met. Now I saw dark red hair, a red dress, and eyes so fixed on the lane ahead that the car seemed to be following an argument she had already won. I had met her once at a party where a rich man bought her a sports car and cried when she actually left in it. She kept the car. Later, she showed me the apology he sent. She had kept that too.

Porsche shouted for Mercedes to sit down. Mercedes shouted that I owed her.

“He owes the whole street right now!” Porsche answered, steering across the Charger’s nose.

The black car braked. Shawna turned to me with her mouth open.

“Your friends?” she asked.

“Good people,” I told her.

“Your good people are going to get us killed,” Shawna said.

“Look behind us,” I told her, keeping the Mercedes ahead of the convertible. Porsche was keeping the Charger from passing. Whenever its nose appeared on one side, the convertible moved across it. Mercedes had dropped into the seat, but her arm kept appearing above the door with a shoe in it, contributing what she could to traffic management.

Shawna laughed. One bright sound, short enough that I turned to make sure I'd heard it. I can still hear it more clearly than the gunshots that came before.

Then the Charger hit the convertible’s rear bumper.

The red car swung toward the wall. Mercedes grabbed the windshield frame. One silver heel left her hand, caught the streetlight in a flash, and disappeared into somebody’s outdoor dinner.

I braked hard enough to drag Shawna forward against her belt.

“Not leaving them,” I said before she could finish my name.

The Charger surged toward us. I saw the driver properly now: a white scar over his eyebrow, teeth showing, his head turning between the two cars.

“Choose me, nigga,” I said, looking at him in the mirror.

I put the Mercedes behind the red convertible. The Charger hit us instead.

The impact drove my head into the seat. Metal folded at the rear. Shawna caught my arm, and for an instant the world narrowed to the grip of her fingers and the work of keeping the wheel from being torn out of mine.

Porsche recovered before she reached the wall. She lifted one finger as she pulled clear. I took it as something stronger than a thank-you.

Ahead, the boulevard opened across an intersection. A transport truck moved through it carrying a silver speedboat over the front of its long trailer, with the back portion empty. Beside the intersection, a closed hotel’s delivery ramp climbed out of the street. I saw the rising concrete, the boat crossing beyond it, and the patch of road on the far side.

Shawna looked from my face to the ramp.

“No,” she said, catching the door handle.

“You don't know what I'm thinking,” I told her.

“I know exactly what you're thinking,” Shawna said. “Your hands already voted.”

I brought the car toward the foot of the ramp. “Then hold the case.”

We hit the incline with the engine roaring. The hood lifted. For a moment the tires hammered over the ridges in the concrete, and then that sound stopped completely.

The car left the ramp.

My stomach rose against my ribs. Through the windshield the Strip spread out below us, bright and impossible, while the boat’s mast and flag slid beneath the nose. Shawna screamed my name. I heard terror in it and hoped that somewhere inside the terror there was affection. Whatever else it contained, the volume would have put an ordinary marriage ceremony out of business.

We cleared the boat by less than the height of its flag.

The landing drove my teeth together. A tire burst. The Mercedes lurched, leaned, and found enough of the street to continue. A collision behind us reached me through every missing window. I glanced in the mirror and saw the Charger sideways against the bare rear of the trailer.

Shawna looked back too. In the mirror, a black wheel rolled across the intersection by itself.

Our pursuers had become a collection of separate transportation problems.

I slowed at the next light. The steering pulled against my hands, and my ribs were beginning to remind me that I had started this adventure with twelve men’s worth of damage already installed.

Porsche came alongside. Mercedes still held the surviving shoe. She looked as if she intended to find whoever had invented heels and make him build her a replacement under supervision.

“You jumped a fucking boat!” Porsche shouted.

“Had to get over it somehow,” I called back to Porsche.

She leaned across the seat toward my window. “With a car, Aiden!”

On the sidewalk, a man began clapping. The couple beside him joined in. Then the taxi line took it up, people lowering suitcases to applaud. I could already imagine how badly I was going to hear that story repeated. I raised my hand to accept what the city had finally recognized.

Shawna caught it and returned it to the wheel.

“They can admire you moving,” Shawna told me, keeping my hand on the wheel until I took hold again.

Mercedes pointed her remaining shoe at Shawna. “Keep him. He’s expensive, but he comes with a show.”

The light changed. Porsche kissed the air toward me and turned away. The two women left together, carrying one shoe. I watched them go and knew I owed them more than a replacement heel. I drove toward the desert on a damaged tire and the sort of pride that will keep a young man from hearing the noises his car is making.

Shawna touched my cheek. She told me I could have kept going when the Charger hit the women.

“Wouldn't have been me,” I told Shawna, keeping my eyes on the road ahead.

She watched me for a moment, then rested her hand on my leg. I felt that small weight more powerfully than anything the men outside the club had done to me.

We passed the last casinos before she asked me to pull over.

I said T’s house was farther out. Her hand left my leg.

“I know where his fucking house is,” Shawna told me. “Pull over.”

I turned off behind a shuttered wedding chapel. Half the sign had burned out. Gravel rasped beneath the ruined tire as I took us into the empty lot, out of sight of the main road. For the first time since the Saint, there was no engine chasing ours.

Shawna opened the case on her lap.

Money filled it. Tight bundles, edge to edge. Three million dollars, she told me. I stared at it and started to repeat what T had said.

“T says a lot,” Shawna said, lifting an envelope out from beneath the cash.

She opened the envelope. The photograph inside had a crease across its middle. It showed her standing in front of this same chapel three years earlier, wearing a white dress. A man stood behind her with his arms around her waist.

The cross against his chest was the one against mine.

“Dante,” she said. “His brother. My husband.”

I looked at the chapel, then the photograph, then at her. The whole ride changed shape behind us. Her fingers closing around the cross at the window. The way her eyes had gone somewhere else when I said T had given it to me. I had been carrying her dead husband through the night and talking as if the world began wherever I happened to park.

She put a cigarette between her lips. The lighter struck twice without catching. I took it gently, lit it, and held the flame steady while she leaned forward.

She told me how she had come to Vegas at twenty-three. A suitcase, eight hundred dollars, and a number a girl in Phoenix had given her. She wanted to sing. The number belonged to Valentino.

I knew Valentino's name from the clubs. I had seen his white suits and the diamond in his tooth, watched men laugh around him and women keep their eyes on his hands. What he had done to Shawna was new to me.

Shawna told me he had bought her clothes and found her a room. He had promised introductions to people in music. By the time she understood what he demanded in return, she said, her clothes, her room, and the life she had started building in them belonged to him.

She blew smoke toward the broken side of the windshield and kept the photograph flat against her knee.

“Dante came into the club on my birthday,” Shawna told me. “Everybody else wanted something. He asked what kind of cake I liked.”

One corner of her mouth rose. She looked down at the photograph before continuing.

“He came back with a whole carrot cake,” she said. “Not a slice. A whole fucking cake. Put it between the bottles like we had all night.”

She told me Valentino had laughed at him. Dante had knocked the diamond tooth out of his mouth.

“My nigga,” I said before I remembered whose story I was hearing.

Shawna looked up from the photograph. “That’s what I said. Exactly what I fucking said.”

She wiped hard beneath one eye, then told me they had married here six weeks later. T stood beside his brother. Red paid for the room. Everybody smiled in the pictures.

I asked what happened. She watched the chapel while she answered.

Dante had taken three million out of a deal. He said they were leaving. He said he had a brother, so he didn't have to be afraid.

I lowered my eyes to the cross. The back had turned outward against my shirt.

Shawna turned the photograph over. Written there were the words behind those initials: Damn, boy. Magnificent.

Shawna described Dante looking at himself in the chapel mirror after their wedding, saying those words as if God had just completed the finishing work. She told me she had nearly dropped her flowers laughing. The next morning she had the words put on his cross.

“Nigga was right about you,” I told her.

“He was talking about himself,” Shawna said, looking up at me.

I looked down at the gold. “Then he finally got it on the right neck.”

The laugh escaped her so suddenly she covered her mouth. For a second the woman from the photograph looked out through the woman beside me. I didn't mistake that for a victory. I was grateful to have seen her.

Then she held the picture to her chest and told me they had left Dante in the desert. Valentino brought her his watch. He said Dante had died begging for somebody to take care of her.

I wanted to kill a man I hadn't met. By then Valentino was already dead, but anger doesn't always concern itself with the schedule.

Shawna told me she had waited eight months. She returned to Valentino and let him assume she had nowhere else to go. By the end, she said, he was telling her she loved him.

“Last Tuesday, I shot him in his car,” she told me. “He was talking when I did it. Kept talking after the first one. So I shot him again.”

Then, she said, she took Dante's watch out of his glove compartment.

She held her wrist where I could see it. The watch was too large. It had turned toward the side of her arm, and she pushed it upright with her thumb.

She told me Red had taken her in afterward and promised protection. Tonight, she said, she had taken the three million back.

I asked where T fitted into this. He had promised to get her out of Vegas, she said.

“You believe him?” I asked Shawna.

Shawna slid the photograph inside her dress. “I believed his brother.”

She closed the case. Then she leaned across the space between our seats and kissed me.

I felt her hand against my face and remembered the man in the photograph. I was wearing his cross. I had just repeated his joke. Part of me wondered how much of that kiss belonged to what she remembered and how much belonged to the man sitting beside her. I hoped she had seen something in me when I went back for Mercedes and Porsche. I didn't ask. Her fingers stayed against my cheek. I wanted her so badly I couldn't make myself pull back.

I tasted smoke. I forgot about the fortune on her lap.

When she pulled back, her fingers stayed against my cheek.

“Don't take me to that house,” Shawna said, still holding my face.

“Then this nigga ain’t taking you,” I said. “Give me somewhere else.”

My first night in the family, and I had already found something I was willing to lose it for.

I took her to a motel outside town. It had an outdoor stairway, six rooms on the upper floor, and a blue sign that blinked as if electricity was being rationed by a man with a personal grievance. I paid for all six rooms and told the clerk nobody was to come upstairs.

He checked the money twice before asking whether I really meant all six. I told him yes. He pushed the keys across the counter without attempting further hospitality.

In our room, Shawna set the case on the second bed and put her purse and pistol on the nightstand. She took Dante's photograph from her dress and laid it beside the lamp. I locked the door and set my pistol on the other side of the bed. Trucks passed on the highway beyond the lot. Downstairs, a couple were arguing beside a car, their ordinary fury rising through the window while the rest of our night waited outside it.

Shawna unbuttoned my shirt to look at my ribs. The bruising had darkened. She touched beside it and said my name softly, without a joke in it.

“I'm good,” I told her.

“You're bleeding,” Shawna said, touching the dark edge of a bruise.

“I've been bleeding all night,” I explained. That seemed a useful distinction to me. She went into the bathroom and came back with a wet towel, which was a fairly complete review of my argument.

She cleaned my face. Her hand steadied my jaw. Whenever the cloth caught the split in my lip, she apologized. I kept telling her not to. It had been a long time since somebody's touch made a hurt easier to carry.

When she finished, I lifted the chain over my head and put the cross in her hand.

She looked down at it. Her thumb found the back. Then she began to cry, bending over the little cross until I moved close enough for her to lean against me.

I held her while she cried. I didn't tell her Dante would want this or that. I hadn't known him. I didn't promise the pain would become useful. I knew how to stay on the edge of the bed and keep my arms around her without making her explain why she was shaking. For once, that was enough for me to do.

After a while she lifted her head.

“Stay,” Shawna said.

“I'm here, baby,” I told her, keeping an arm around her.

“Tonight,” she said. “I mean tonight.”

“I'm right fucking here,” I promised. She pulled me down to kiss her.

She laid the cross beside the photograph and turned the lamp off herself.

We spent what remained of that little piece of night together, with the money on the other bed and our guns close enough to reach. Afterward, she rested against my chest while the blue light from the motel sign moved over the ceiling. There was nothing elegant about the room. The air conditioner rattled. A spring complained whenever I shifted. I remember it as one of the first places where I wanted morning to find me exactly where I was.

Shawna asked what I wanted. I told her I wanted her.

“You don't know me,” she said, her head still against my chest.

“Then tell me more,” I answered.

So she did. She talked about the songs she had wanted to sing. She said her mother in Phoenix still believed she worked in a restaurant. She told me Dante had wanted a house near the water, though neither of them knew how to swim.

I told her we would buy it. I told her we would go wherever she wanted.

I had known her less than three hours. A wiser man might have offered something smaller. At twenty-four, with her head on my chest, I would have tried to carry a fucking coastline into the room if she had said she liked the view.

My phone lit up on the nightstand.

T’s name filled the screen. I let it ring. A moment later a photograph arrived: the Mercedes parked below us, taken from close enough to show the damage along its side.

Under the picture, he had written: Come down, son.

I put my clothes on and slipped my pistol back into my waistband. Shawna sat up behind me. Before I opened the door, I saw her put the photograph and cross inside her purse.

The empty swimming pool lay beyond a metal fence at the far side of the motel lot. T waited beside it with Tank and two men I didn't know. He wore the same shirt he had worn behind the nightclub. My blood still marked one cuff.

I came down with my hands visible. Behind me, our room door closed. I was aware of that sound in a way I wasn't aware of the trucks or the light buzzing over the stairs.

T asked where the money was. Upstairs, I told him. He asked about Shawna. Upstairs too.

T nodded slowly. He looked me up and down before speaking again.

“One thing,” he said. “I gave you one fucking thing to do.”

I told him she had spoken about Dante.

Tank looked away. That frightened me before T answered. My new brother couldn't look at me while I asked about the dead one.

“Dante stole from his family,” T said.

“He was your brother,” I said to T.

“Then the nigga should've known better,” T answered.

I had brought a little hope downstairs with me. It was embarrassing how little I required of him: a denial, another name, anything I could hold between the man who had kissed my forehead and the man who could let his brother die. He did not even bother giving me a lie.

He blamed Shawna instead. Said she had put Dante in the ground and now I was ready to follow.

“You put him in the ground,” I said.

T stepped close. I smelled the same cologne that had been against my face when he gave me the cross. He asked who had made me. Who had given me the car, the money, the name people would now say across the west side.

“You think she would've looked at you without all that?” T asked, pointing toward the car he had given me.

I didn't answer. It hurt because I had spent the first part of the evening believing the gifts had made me worth looking at.

“She’s upstairs with my three million,” he said. “That’s what she’s in love with.”

Tank shifted behind him. I looked toward the sound; T moved closer, filling my sight again, and gave me one last order: go get her.

Above us, the room door opened.

Shawna stood at the balcony rail holding the case. She had come out barefoot. The blue motel light moved across her face while she looked down at T.

“You want your money, T?” Shawna called from the balcony.

He looked up.

She swung the case over the rail and let go.

It struck the empty pool below us. The latches gave, and cash burst across the cracked blue tiles. Bundles split on the slope toward the drain. A few loose bills fluttered long enough for everybody to watch them land.

“There,” Shawna said. “Now tell him what I love.”

T drew his gun toward the balcony.

I struck his arm as he fired. The shot broke a window above the stairs. Tank caught me from behind, locking one arm across my chest. One of the other men came toward me with his fist up. I drove my forehead into his face and felt a bright red pain spread through my own skull.

Tank’s hold loosened. I twisted, drove him into the pool fence, and heard the wire rattle behind his back.

Shawna fired from above. The man beside T dropped.

T shoved his pistol against my side.

The shot felt like an enormous hand closing inside me. Sound came after it, or seemed to. I couldn't take a breath. My knees wanted to fold, and the front of T’s shirt filled my sight because I had fallen against him without letting go.

“Should’ve listened,” he said.

I caught his gun wrist with both hands. Blood ran beneath my shirt. I could feel it moving, could feel my strength trying to follow it out. But the balcony was above his shoulder, and Shawna was on it, and I would not give him that angle again.

Tank hit me. The blow reached me from somewhere far away.

I drove a knee into T, pulled one hand free, and got the pistol from my waistband. Even then, I wanted him to stop. I wanted the man who had called me son to look down at what he had done and become somebody else before I had to answer it.

He kept holding the gun.

I fired. He struck the fence. I fired again, and he went down with his hand still reaching toward me.

The wire shook after he stopped moving.

Tank stared at him, then at the gun in my hand. He began to say my name.

“Back up.” I could barely get the breath behind it. “Tank, back the fuck up.”

He moved away. I tried to remain standing long enough to understand what I had done.

Shawna ran down the stairs with the pistol in her hand and her purse slung over one shoulder. When she reached me, she put the pistol inside the purse and pressed both palms against the blood spreading over my shirt. I heard her tell me to look at her. I tried to tell her I was good. My mouth made the shape; nothing useful came out.

She turned on Tank. “Get the fucking car!”

He looked at T one more time. Then he ran toward the Mercedes.

That was how the west side became mine. Not with the keys landing in my hand, not with T announcing it over the money. With a man I had wanted for a father dead beside the pool and a woman still wearing another man’s watch trying to hold my life inside my body.

We left the cash where it had fallen. I remember that much of it: blue tiles, money everywhere, nobody bending to pick it up while Shawna got me into the car. There wasn't a dollar in that pool that could buy me another minute standing beside it.

She drove. One hand on the wheel, the other holding a towel against my side whenever the road allowed. The sky had begun to lighten over the desert. Behind us, the casinos kept shining, indifferent as a room full of people who hadn't heard their names called yet.

“Stay with me,” Shawna said.

“I'm here,” I told her, forcing enough air into my chest to make it audible.

“Then keep talking,” Shawna said. “Tell me about that house.”

So I talked about the house I had promised at the motel. I told her there would be a room just for her music. We would learn to swim. She asked where the windows would face, and I told her the sunrise. I was bleeding through a motel towel and selling waterfront property to keep the woman driving from hearing how hard I was working for each breath.

She kept asking about the windows. I kept giving her more of them.

At the edge of town, she pulled over. I thought something had gone wrong with the car until she took out the cross. She put the chain around my neck and settled the gold against my chest.

“Don’t,” I said. “That’s his.”

“I know,” Shawna said.

She closed my fingers around it. Then she kissed me and returned to the wheel. The meaning of that would take us longer than one drive to understand. At the time, I held on because she had asked me to.

The sun rose beside her face. My phone kept ringing. Some numbers I recognized from T's people or Red's. Others meant nothing to me. On the third call, I answered with my name.

There was a silence at the other end. Then a man asked what I wanted them to do.

I looked at Shawna. She watched the road. Dante’s watch had slipped around her wrist again, but she couldn't spare a hand to turn it back.

“Some nigga better find me a doctor,” I said. “And tell everybody the girl is with me.”

I had started the night looking for somewhere to belong. By sunrise, belonging had stopped meaning the men who would let me stand beside them. It meant the woman beside me, the people we had stopped for, and whatever I would have to stand through now that I had chosen them.

Ten toes down. All the way to the end.

## 9. Our Valedictorian Has a Gun

I learned I was graduating from college while deciding which gun went with breakfast.

The kitchen table had become crowded enough that I kept the pistols on the empty chair beside me. Shawna poured coffee. Rosa argued with Mercedes about toast. Tank came in holding a cream envelope with a gold crest and my name written across it by somebody who had put more commitment into the A than I'd put into several relationships.

“School,” Tank said, setting it beside my plate.

I looked up. “Whose?”

Tank pulled out a chair across from me. “Yours.”

I put down the pistol I'd been considering. “I got a school?”

Shawna stopped pouring. Rosa turned around. Mercedes lowered her toast and looked at the envelope as though it might explain something troubling about her own education.

Tank read the crest. “Las Vegas College.”

I nodded. “Oh. Them.”

Shawna set the pot down. “You went to college?”

I opened the envelope. “Went to the building.”

Shawna leaned toward me. “When?”

I remembered the dining tables before I remembered the year. “Twenty-two. Free lunch. Excellent chicken.”

Tank took the second page from beneath my hand and stared at it.

“Says valedictorian,” Tank told the room.

I reached for my coffee. “Paying attention.”

Rosa came around the table with her hand out. “Give me that.”

I moved my cup out of her way. “Don't wrinkle my achievement.”

Rosa held the letter close to her face. “Looking for the payment demand. Mercedes, see if he's on a milk carton too. Been somewhere two years and forgot.”

Mercedes leaned against Rosa's shoulder to read. “Highest marks in the college's history.”

Tank looked from them to me. “Marks in what?”

I nodded toward Mercedes. “History. She just said.”

Shawna turned my chin toward her. “Baby. What did you do to these people?”

I thought about the chicken.

Two years earlier, a friend told me the college had food at its adult scholarship day. You had to be at least twenty-two for the program. I was exactly twenty-two and hungry enough to consider a future if it came with a plate.

The woman at registration told me lunch was for applicants. I became an applicant before she finished the word.

She gave me a booklet and pointed me toward a table. There was math, reading, music that looked wrong before I even heard it in my head, and a question about two promises that couldn't both be kept. I ate and wrote. When the chicken was gone, I corrected the music and took two rolls. The back page was blank, so I used it to explain what else they'd got wrong.

I told everyone at breakfast that part. Rosa lowered the letter. Tank looked at my plate for so long that I pushed it an inch toward him.

“You ate a degree,” Tank said.

I took my plate back. “Thought while I chewed. Acting like forbidden technology.”

Mercedes reached around Rosa and turned the page. “They want you there at eleven.” Rosa passed the letter back to Tank, who checked the time for himself.

Shawna looked at the kitchen clock. Nine twenty. She put her coffee down without drinking it.

“Need clothes,” Shawna said.

I looked toward the blue gala invitations on the sideboard. “Got plans.”

Porsche came through the doorway with sunglasses on and a garment bag over one arm. She stopped just inside, apparently having heard enough of the discussion from the hall.

“Your plans got a hat,” Porsche told me. “You're going.”

I pointed at the invitations. “Red's gala—”

Porsche looked at the clock. “Two nights away. This is a hundred minutes. Even you can count that far.”

Tank lifted the college letter. “Actually, highest marks—”

“Don't help him,” Rosa told Tank.

Mercedes put her toast back on the plate. “Please don't.”

Shawna ignored both of them and kissed me. Then she stayed close enough for me to hear her without the rest of the room.

“Want to see my man graduate,” Shawna said.

I could have graduated from the moon if she'd said it like that. I stood, secured the pistol I'd chosen, and put the other one away before anyone started treating the breakfast table like a faculty meeting.

“Get dressed,” I told them. “My niggas going to college.”

Tank looked down at his unfinished breakfast.

I pointed at the plate. “Bring it. Apparently that's the exam.”

We arrived in a convoy because education deserved respect.

Tank drove our Mercedes. Its door latched again. I had heard him arranging that repair while the rest of us argued about clothes, and I was beginning to suspect the men fixing the car slept beside their tools with one eye open.

Porsche drove Leon's newest Bentley, dark green this time. She told us he'd said white brought back too many memories. Mercedes sat beside her with a camera. Rosa wore a yellow suit that made the campus flowers look as if they had lost their funding. Marcellus wore his clean shirt and a tie Rosa had selected. I watched him straighten the tie in a car window before we left. Then he looked toward me and asked whether it sat properly. I told him it did.

Shawna wore white again. Nobody had needed to die for this outfit.

I wore black trousers, an open-necked shirt, the cross and a jacket that covered the bandages. My gun sat beneath it. I had read the letter twice while getting dressed, and neither page asked me to arrive with fewer problems than usual.

Blue gowns filled the campus entrance. Grown men and women stood beside the fountain holding flowers and programs, adjusting caps, calling to people across the paving. I lowered my window while Tank eased the Mercedes past a little group near the water.

One man pointed at our car.

“That's him,” the man said to his friend.

His friend looked up from a program. “Who?”

The first man leaned close to the window. “Mercedes nigga.”

His friend waved the program at the parking lot. “College, Marcus. Seventeen Mercedes over there.”

Marcus shook his pointing hand. “Flying Mercedes.”

His friend looked properly then. His mouth opened. He checked the program and began hitting Marcus's shoulder with it.

“Our valedictorian?” the friend asked.

Marcus put both hands out. “What I'm saying!”

The friend stared through my window. “That nigga?”

Marcus leaned down until he could see me smiling back at them. “That nigga.”

By the time I got out, the people around the fountain had turned toward us.

A woman hurried down the steps carrying a blue robe over both arms. Silver glasses, precisely pinned hair, dark skin, a little out of breath. She looked about forty-six and held the robe clear of the paving even while weaving around people to reach me.

“Aiden,” she said. “Professor Yvonne Halcyon. We met at your admission.”

I recognized her when she smiled. “You had the chicken.”

Halcyon's smile widened. “You remember.”

I took the robe from her. “Don't forget a good plate.”

Halcyon looked past me at the car. “We've been looking for you.”

I followed her glance. “Been around.”

She faced me again. “We've seen.”

Principal Percival Mercer came out behind her wearing enough gold over his blue academic robe to make me wonder whether the diploma included territory. I learned he was sixty. His gray beard was beautifully trimmed, his voice deep, and by the time he reached my outstretched hand, he was crying.

“At last,” Mercer said.

Tank leaned toward my ear. “What the fuck was in that booklet?”

I watched Mercer take my hand between both of his. “Apparently me.”

The principal shook it without letting go. “Your work changed this college.”

I looked toward Halcyon. “Which part?”

Mercer opened a folder. “The final page.”

I looked at the copy inside. My handwriting, crowded around a grease mark. “Blank one?”

Mercer nodded, still wet around the eyes. “You corrected the examination.”

I recognized a line I'd crossed out. “Had a mistake.”

Mercer held the folder higher. “Six. We'd found two in twenty years.”

That sounded like something I might have done while waiting for dessert. I asked if that was the original paper. He told me they'd framed it. This was a copy for handling. Somewhere in a college building, a grease spot from my chicken had achieved archival protection.

Halcyon stepped beside him. “The scholarship board accepted your independent work in place of the standard course sequence. Four departments circulated your essays. Your music analysis corrected the conservatory examination. Professor Lorne has taught your final solution since last spring.”

I glanced toward the men behind her. “Hope Lorne's got a nice voice.”

A man lifted one hand. “Do my best.”

He straightened his collar after saying it. I smiled at him before he could start apologizing to my own work on my behalf.

Shawna caught my sleeve. Her eyes were bright.

“Didn't tell me any of this,” Shawna said.

I looked from her to the carefully protected grease mark. “Didn't know they kept it.”

The graduates nearby laughed. One took off his cap and rubbed his hair.

“Came every night for three years,” the graduate said.

I turned toward him. “You here, ain't you?”

He looked down at the cap in his hand, then back at me. “Yeah.”

I nodded toward the building. “Come on, then. Graduating.”

He put his cap back on and joined us as Halcyon led the way. She stopped long enough to settle the robe over my jacket and put a cap on my head. I held still while she adjusted the tassel. It was the first part of the morning that felt like somebody had expected me to be a student.

On the way to the auditorium, Mercer insisted we go through the science hall. He wanted to show me a wall. Lorne went ahead while the principal stopped us outside.

Gold letters above the doors read AIDEN HALL.

I stood beneath them. Tank took a picture. Shawna laughed behind her hand, and I tilted my head until I could see my whole name at once.

“Got a hall,” I told her.

Mercer moved beside me. “Several.”

Rosa put a hand on his arm. “Don't tell him. Start naming doors.”

Inside, three professors stood around a table covered with papers. I heard one insist a number couldn't be right just before he saw us. The others stopped talking at different moments, leaving one last offended syllable to hang in the room by itself.

Lorne picked up a page. “Perhaps you could settle something.”

Halcyon made a little sound through her nose. “He has a ceremony.”

Lorne held one finger up. “One minute.”

I approached the table. “Everybody needs one minute till I get there. Show me.”

He turned the sheet around. A drawing of the auditorium roof. Lines, numbers, a span circled several times in red. I looked at it, then through the window at the building across the courtyard.

Lorne touched the circled part. “The model gives two possible answers.”

I looked out of the window again. “Old roof.”

Lorne bent over the drawing. “The dimensions—”

I pointed across the courtyard. “Moved the stage. Changed that end. Eight panels in the window now. You got seven.”

All three professors turned toward the real building. I took the pencil lying beside the page, moved the line to match what we could see, and followed the load across to where it belonged.

“There,” I told Lorne. “Now those numbers stop fighting.”

His mouth moved without a sound. The professor beside him found another sheet, compared the two, and sat down so suddenly his wheeled chair rolled back into the wall.

“He's right,” the seated professor said.

I put the pencil down. “Say it like you enjoyed finding out.”

The professor looked up, smiling this time. “He's right!”

The room laughed. Mercer began crying again. I looked at Shawna while Halcyon searched her pockets for something to give him.

“Your man got a roof,” I told Shawna.

She hooked two fingers through my robe. “Better get under it before they name another building.”

As we left, Lorne touched my arm gently.

“What do you do for a living?” Lorne asked.

Tank answered over my shoulder. “Improves transportation.”

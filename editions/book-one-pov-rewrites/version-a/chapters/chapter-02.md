## 2. First Night in Vegas

The night I met Shawna, cocaine had settled in the fold of my collar and somebody else’s blood had worked its way inside my watch. It had dried beneath the rim of the glass. Every time I checked the hour, there it was: a little red reminder that somewhere in this city I either owed a man an apology or needed to finish the job.

I was twenty-four, and I thought damage was a way of keeping score.

My last car had burned outside my ex-boyfriend’s apartment. He told me that meant I wanted him back. I had reached the point in my life where even my insurance claims were being interpreted romantically. That afternoon I had eaten a gas-station sandwich in a borrowed car. Before the sun came up again, I would be carrying three million dollars, running with a woman half of Vegas wanted delivered to them, and bleeding around a bullet I would keep for the rest of my life.

But all that was still ahead of me. Behind the nightclub, under the yellow security light, twelve men were preparing to beat my ass, and I wanted them to like me when they finished.

Big T had walked me out with his hand on my shoulder. He kept it there while the door closed and the music became a deep thump on the other side of the brick. The rear lot smelled of hot engines and spilled beer. Cars stood at angles around an open patch of concrete. Men leaned against the hoods or rolled their shoulders beside the doors, blue rags showing from pockets, chains flashing when they moved beneath the light.

I knew some of them. Two had borrowed money from me. One still owed me ten dollars and had apparently decided paying it back would be easier if I lost the ability to remember numbers.

T drew me into the middle, his fingers pressing through my shirt.

“Once a nigga in, that nigga in,” he told me. “You understand what I'm saying?”

I told him I understood. He watched my face anyway. Summer ended, he said; a body dropped; suddenly somebody had somewhere else to be. He wanted to know whether I would be one of those men.

“I got nowhere else to be,” I said. “That’s why I’m standing here instead of enjoying the fucking music.”

A laugh escaped from behind a Cadillac. T didn’t join it. He asked whether I thought this was funny, and I looked around the ring of men who had been assembled to determine my sincerity through orthopedic damage.

“I think twelve is a committee,” I told T. “Which one of you takes the minutes?”

T drove me backward into the Cadillac. My spine hit the edge of the hood and the breath jumped out of me.

That answered it. T took the minutes.

The other eleven came in with amendments.

A fist split my lip. Something hard caught behind my ear before I had turned toward the first blow. I tried to get my elbows in, but a kick knocked my leg out and I hit the ground on my palms. Grit bit into the skin. Shoes filled the little space I could see beneath the cars.

I got up because I had come there to get up. That was about the size of my thinking at twenty-four. I had spent too long being somebody’s temporary guest, somebody’s good time, somebody’s useful man until the useful part was over. T was offering me a place where my name would stay after I left the room. I was willing to pay for that place with whatever these motherfuckers could reach.

Blood ran into one eye. I swung toward a shoulder with the eye that still worked, felt my knuckles connect, and heard somebody tell me to stay down.

“Y’all just told me to stay IN,” I shouted. “Pick an instruction!”

Then Tank hit me in the ribs.

He was twenty-eight—I learned that later—almost twice my size, and broad enough that his shirt had given up pretending the buttons were a permanent arrangement. The punch bent me around his arm. I caught a fistful of that failing shirt before he could draw back and drove with my legs, taking him into the next car.

The alarm went off beneath us.

“Not the paint!” somebody yelled.

I hit Tank again. “You should’ve thought about the paint before you started throwing people at it!”

He tried to turn me off his chest, but I was too close and too angry to assist him. I kept my fist in his shirt and used the other hand wherever I could find room. He called for somebody to get me off him. T laughed. The others watched. Through the alarm I heard Tank shout T’s name. He kept looking past my shoulder toward him while nobody moved to help. I wasn't the only nigga finding the induction process disappointing.

“You said he was little,” T reminded him.

Tank caught another blow on his forearm. “Nigga, I have new information!”

T finally hauled me away by the shoulders. I stood in the open with my fists still up. My legs shook. My mouth tasted of pennies and whiskey, and I couldn't uncurl my fingers even after the men stopped coming.

“Enough,” T said. He grinned as he looked around at them. “Twelve niggas. Look at him. Still shopping.”

I told him I was done. When he asked whether I was sure, I looked at the fists I was apparently still offering to the room.

“I’m waiting for my hands to hear it,” I told him.

T laughed, and several of the others joined him. Then he took a gold chain out of his pocket, opened it wide, and lowered it over my head. I bowed automatically to let him. A small cross dropped against the sore place above my ribs.

The men went quiet before I did.

“My brother wore that,” T said. “Real nigga.”

I lifted the cross. It was warm from his pocket. I asked where his brother was so I could thank him.

“Dead,” T said.

The car alarm chose that moment to stop. I heard T draw a breath through his nose, and because he was so close I could see the dampness at the edge of his eye.

“Don’t make me bury you too,” T told me, keeping one hand on my shoulder.

A minute earlier, he had been hitting me. Now he had found a way to make me stand there trying not to cry. I closed my hand over the gold as if accepting it required me to keep it warm.

“Not this nigga,” I promised.

T kissed my forehead. Then he turned me toward the club and called for the men to make room for his son.

I remember believing him. I won't improve the story by pretending I was cleverer than I was. He had hurt me, stopped the hurting, and called the space after it love. At the time, that felt enough like a home for me to walk through the door.

Tank held it open. Out of some bruised instinct toward manners, I reached to help him. He moved his shoulder away from my hand.

“Walk through, nigga,” Tank said, holding the door away from my hand. “I can still defeat a door.”

Inside, blue light lay across the smoke. The back room was warmer than the lot, crowded around a glass table buried in money and white bricks. Men separated bills into stacks. Bottles of Hennessy stood wherever the cash left room. Three women in diamonds drank beside a man with two pistols tucked into one belt, as if his trousers had enemies on both sides.

One of the women looked at my face and asked whether I had survived a war.

“Yeah,” I said, taking the chair somebody pushed toward me. “And I won.”

They cheered. A bottle arrived in one hand, a burning blunt in the other. I drank, smoked, and wiped my mouth against my wrist before I remembered what was already on the watch. The pile of cash beside my elbow was bigger than any amount I had ever been trusted to sit near.

The woman wanted to know if I was the one who had beaten Tank. T said I was. She folded a thousand-dollar bill into my shirt pocket. Ninety seconds back inside and somebody was paying me to be handsome with a broken face. I began to think the committee’s compensation package deserved another look.

Tank came in holding ice against his jaw. He took the bottle from me, drank, and gave it back. I waited to find out whether the next thing between us would be another punch.

Tank pressed the ice against his jaw. “Who taught you to fight dirty like that?”

“Same nigga taught you to fight unconscious,” I told him, keeping the bottle ready in case this resumed.

The room broke open. Tank stared at me while everybody else laughed. Then his face changed. He began laughing too, blood showing between his teeth, and dragged a chair up beside mine.

We drank out of the same bottle. I counted that as the first brick in the house we would build between us. I passed it over; he passed it back. I stopped watching his free hand quite so closely. Later, I would have reasons to doubt him. That night I would have handed him my heart to hold while I fought with both hands.

T tossed a set of keys across the table. I caught them against the bottle.

“Black Mercedes,” he said. “That’s yours.”

I turned the keys over, feeling the little badge beneath my thumb. An hour earlier, I had wanted a place in his crew. Now he was telling me the west side needed a man and the west side had me.

I asked whether he was serious. He nodded toward the keys, as if there was no point discussing an answer already in my hand.

That is how quickly a young man can mistake being given a thing for having earned the world. I sat taller. My ribs objected. I ignored them. The woman’s thousand dollars crackled in my pocket whenever I moved, adding its own applause.

Then T showed me a photograph on his phone.

The woman in it stood beside a pool at night. The Strip burned behind her. She wore black, diamonds at her ears, dark hair over one shoulder. She wasn't smiling for whoever held the camera. I looked at her face and forgot to admire the city.

“Shawna,” T said. “Twenty-six. Works for Red.”

I asked what sort of work. Whatever Red wanted, he told me, and moved on before I could decide how much I disliked the answer. She would be at the Saint, in the penthouse. At eleven she would bring down a case. I was to take her and the money to T’s house in Henderson.

Across the table, Tank watched me looking at the photograph.

I asked why she was leaving Red. T said she had finally got smart. I asked whether Red knew about this little family pickup. T said that if he knew, we wouldn't be having the conversation.

Then he took the back of my neck and drew me close enough that his next words were only for me.

“Don't fall in love with her,” T said close to my ear.

I laughed into his face. He kept his hand on my neck until I looked at him properly.

“Laugh when the job's done,” T told me. “I'm serious.”

“Look at me, nigga,” I said. “Do I look like the nigga falls in love?”

He released my neck. I sat back with the keys and believed every word I had just said. I had managed to become wrong about my whole future before I had even learned the radio in my new car.

The Saint rose over a gold fountain and a line of waiting vehicles. Men I knew from the clubs had told me how Red ran it: he owned the penthouse, the nightclub beneath it, and enough people between those floors that a man could disappear into a service elevator without anybody at the desk looking up. At the top, bulletproof windows reflected a city that was encouraged to admire from outside.

I parked the Mercedes at the entrance. The valet saw the drying blood on my collar and asked whether I needed a doctor.

“My car needs you first,” I told him, putting a hundred in his hand. “Nothing happens to it.”

The car still had all its windows when I said that. I like to remember we had those few minutes together.

Upstairs, the penthouse opened into music, glass, and more money being treated as if it had grown there. Men gambled with bank-wrapped bundles. Cocaine dusted tables between drinks. Women in dresses worth more than the cars outside laughed at men who introduced themselves with names I wasn't about to verify.

A man in a white fur coat bent over a trophy to do a line. It was September in Vegas. He had brought both the heat problem and the trophy himself. When somebody reached past him for a drink, he hugged the award to his chest. I never learned what he had won, but he was having a hell of a time retaining the title.

Mercedes stood near the bar. She was thirty, and we already possessed a little history: she had once left me naked in a hotel room and taken my shoes after I called her by her sister’s name. She looked down at the car keys in my hand and smiled before she looked at the damage to my face.

“You finally got a Mercedes you can afford, baby?” Mercedes asked, touching the key ring with one finger.

I invited her to come see it. She reminded me that I owed her flowers.

I counted five hundred out of my new money and put it in her hand. “Buy the whole fucking garden.”

She kissed my cheek, cautiously avoiding the cut, and told me I was bleeding on the good side of my face.

“Both sides good,” I told Mercedes, turning my face so she could check.

She laughed. I left her beside the bar, pleased with myself, a dead man’s gold on my chest and another man’s gift in my fist. I had been wanted by men and women across that city and thought I knew the forms trouble liked to take when it dressed beautifully.

Then I saw Shawna at the window.

The silver case stood beside her ankle. She held a cigarette low enough that its smoke curled past her bare arm. A little scar marked the skin above one eyebrow. A man was talking beside her, but she wasn't following his mouth. Her eyes had found me across the room.

I went to her. My lip hurt. My ribs hurt. The air seemed to have become a thing I needed to move through deliberately.

“T sent me,” I told Shawna.

“I know,” she said. Her eyes had already dropped to the cross on my chest.

I asked how she knew. Her cigarette stopped halfway to her mouth.

“Where'd you get that?” Shawna asked, pointing at the cross.

“T gave it to me,” I said.

“Tonight?” she asked. She hadn't looked back up at my face.

I nodded. She reached out and turned it over without asking.

Three tiny letters had been engraved in the back: D. B. M. I hadn't noticed them. Shawna’s fingers closed around the cross, pulling the chain gently against the back of my neck.

“You know what those mean?” she asked.

I told her no.

She let it fall. “Drive before you find out.”

I lifted the case. She took my free hand and led us away from the window. The man beside her stopped talking. As we crossed the party, I watched the small changes travel ahead of us: a smile lost, a phone raised, a man turning his shoulder to speak into somebody else’s ear. We were leaving a room that had not given permission to lose her.

At the elevator, one of Red’s men stepped across the doors. He asked where the fuck I was taking her. I told him she was coming with me. Did he need a picture?

“I’m asking her,” he said, reaching toward the front of his jacket.

I hit him in the throat before his hand reached the inside of his jacket. He folded into the wall. I shoved him lower and stepped across his shoes as Shawna pressed the elevator button.

Another man came around the corner. Shawna brought a small silver pistol out of her purse and aimed it at the middle of his chest.

“You get one chance,” Shawna told the man coming around the corner.

He took it by standing perfectly still.

The doors shut. She kept the pistol in her hand all the way down. In the quiet box, with the music shrinking above us, I could hear how carefully she was breathing.

“Niggas chasing you out of every party,” I asked, “or I got the special one?”

She watched the floor numbers descend. “Only the ones I survive.”

I wanted to make her laugh. I wanted it badly enough that the desire frightened me before I recognized what it was.

The valet had the Mercedes waiting. I put the case on Shawna’s lap, got behind the wheel, and pulled away from the entrance. I had barely started learning where the controls lived when she looked in her mirror and said my name.

“Don't look,” Shawna told me, holding her eyes on the mirror.

Naturally I looked. I had owned this Mercedes for eleven minutes. I was inspecting everything that approached it.

A black Charger followed with its lights off. Two men sat in front. In the back, a third leaned across the seat with something long in his hands.

The rear window burst.

Shawna folded over the case. I ducked, jerked the wheel, and clipped the edge of a fountain with the right fender. Water rose over the hood and washed across three men in suits standing with their chests out at the entrance. Their posture became considerably less formal. We struck the street still moving. A horn screamed beneath our own engine.

I shouted to ask whether the men were hers.

“You think I'd ride with you if those were my niggas?” Shawna shouted back.

“You met me tonight!” I reminded her, dragging the wheel straight.

“I'm trying to meet you tomorrow!” She pointed at the open road. “Drive!”

A yellow Ferrari slid out of the next street and joined the Charger. It came level on my side. The driver smiled at me while his passenger raised a pistol. That smile bothered me. There should be a limit to how comfortable a man gets while doing this shit.

Shawna had braced one hand against the center of the wheel when the glass broke. I pointed at it.

“Baby, move your hand,” I told her. “The horn. You're leaning on the horn.”

She lifted her wrist. The screaming stopped. In the sudden space it left, I heard the Ferrari’s engine climbing beside mine.

I turned toward it.

The gunman pulled his pistol up against his chest. Beside him, the driver's hands jerked on the wheel. Yellow metal scraped our side, caught the street pole, and spun away across a valet entrance. Sparks flew beneath the car. Men in expensive shoes climbed onto the backs of other men in expensive shoes to get out of its path.

Shawna stared after it. “That’s a Ferrari!”

“That’s one Ferrari,” I told her. “Keep up.”

The Charger still filled the mirror. The man in the back shifted his long gun into another position, and before I could decide how much road I had to work with, a red convertible burst out of a hotel drive between us.

Mercedes stood in its passenger seat, holding her heels together in one hand.

“I told you about my fucking flowers, Aiden!” Mercedes shouted from the red convertible.

Porsche drove. She had told me she was twenty-nine at the party where we met. Now I saw dark red hair, a red dress, and eyes so fixed on the lane ahead that the car seemed to be following an argument she had already won. I had met her once at a party where a rich man bought her a sports car and cried when she actually left in it. She kept the car. Later, she showed me the apology he sent. She had kept that too.

Porsche shouted for Mercedes to sit down. Mercedes shouted that I owed her.

“He owes the whole street right now!” Porsche answered, steering across the Charger’s nose.

The black car braked. Shawna turned to me with her mouth open.

“Your friends?” she asked.

“Good people,” I told her.

“Your good people are going to get us killed,” Shawna said.

“Look behind us,” I told her, keeping the Mercedes ahead of the convertible. Porsche was keeping the Charger from passing. Whenever its nose appeared on one side, the convertible moved across it. Mercedes had dropped into the seat, but her arm kept appearing above the door with a shoe in it, contributing what she could to traffic management.

Shawna laughed. One bright sound, short enough that I turned to make sure I'd heard it. I can still hear it more clearly than the gunshots that came before.

Then the Charger hit the convertible’s rear bumper.

The red car swung toward the wall. Mercedes grabbed the windshield frame. One silver heel left her hand, caught the streetlight in a flash, and disappeared into somebody’s outdoor dinner.

I braked hard enough to drag Shawna forward against her belt.

“Not leaving them,” I said before she could finish my name.

The Charger surged toward us. I saw the driver properly now: a white scar over his eyebrow, teeth showing, his head turning between the two cars.

“Choose me, nigga,” I said, looking at him in the mirror.

I put the Mercedes behind the red convertible. The Charger hit us instead.

The impact drove my head into the seat. Metal folded at the rear. Shawna caught my arm, and for an instant the world narrowed to the grip of her fingers and the work of keeping the wheel from being torn out of mine.

Porsche recovered before she reached the wall. She lifted one finger as she pulled clear. I took it as something stronger than a thank-you.

Ahead, the boulevard opened across an intersection. A transport truck moved through it carrying a silver speedboat over the front of its long trailer, with the back portion empty. Beside the intersection, a closed hotel’s delivery ramp climbed out of the street. I saw the rising concrete, the boat crossing beyond it, and the patch of road on the far side.

Shawna looked from my face to the ramp.

“No,” she said, catching the door handle.

“You don't know what I'm thinking,” I told her.

“I know exactly what you're thinking,” Shawna said. “Your hands already voted.”

I brought the car toward the foot of the ramp. “Then hold the case.”

We hit the incline with the engine roaring. The hood lifted. For a moment the tires hammered over the ridges in the concrete, and then that sound stopped completely.

The car left the ramp.

My stomach rose against my ribs. Through the windshield the Strip spread out below us, bright and impossible, while the boat’s mast and flag slid beneath the nose. Shawna screamed my name. I heard terror in it and hoped that somewhere inside the terror there was affection. Whatever else it contained, the volume would have put an ordinary marriage ceremony out of business.

We cleared the boat by less than the height of its flag.

The landing drove my teeth together. A tire burst. The Mercedes lurched, leaned, and found enough of the street to continue. A collision behind us reached me through every missing window. I glanced in the mirror and saw the Charger sideways against the bare rear of the trailer.

Shawna looked back too. In the mirror, a black wheel rolled across the intersection by itself.

Our pursuers had become a collection of separate transportation problems.

I slowed at the next light. The steering pulled against my hands, and my ribs were beginning to remind me that I had started this adventure with twelve men’s worth of damage already installed.

Porsche came alongside. Mercedes still held the surviving shoe. She looked as if she intended to find whoever had invented heels and make him build her a replacement under supervision.

“You jumped a fucking boat!” Porsche shouted.

“Had to get over it somehow,” I called back to Porsche.

She leaned across the seat toward my window. “With a car, Aiden!”

On the sidewalk, a man began clapping. The couple beside him joined in. Then the taxi line took it up, people lowering suitcases to applaud. I could already imagine how badly I was going to hear that story repeated. I raised my hand to accept what the city had finally recognized.

Shawna caught it and returned it to the wheel.

“They can admire you moving,” Shawna told me, keeping my hand on the wheel until I took hold again.

Mercedes pointed her remaining shoe at Shawna. “Keep him. He’s expensive, but he comes with a show.”

The light changed. Porsche kissed the air toward me and turned away. The two women left together, carrying one shoe. I watched them go and knew I owed them more than a replacement heel. I drove toward the desert on a damaged tire and the sort of pride that will keep a young man from hearing the noises his car is making.

Shawna touched my cheek. She told me I could have kept going when the Charger hit the women.

“Wouldn't have been me,” I told Shawna, keeping my eyes on the road ahead.

She watched me for a moment, then rested her hand on my leg. I felt that small weight more powerfully than anything the men outside the club had done to me.

We passed the last casinos before she asked me to pull over.

I said T’s house was farther out. Her hand left my leg.

“I know where his fucking house is,” Shawna told me. “Pull over.”

I turned off behind a shuttered wedding chapel. Half the sign had burned out. Gravel rasped beneath the ruined tire as I took us into the empty lot, out of sight of the main road. For the first time since the Saint, there was no engine chasing ours.

Shawna opened the case on her lap.

Money filled it. Tight bundles, edge to edge. Three million dollars, she told me. I stared at it and started to repeat what T had said.

“T says a lot,” Shawna said, lifting an envelope out from beneath the cash.

She opened the envelope. The photograph inside had a crease across its middle. It showed her standing in front of this same chapel three years earlier, wearing a white dress. A man stood behind her with his arms around her waist.

The cross against his chest was the one against mine.

“Dante,” she said. “His brother. My husband.”

I looked at the chapel, then the photograph, then at her. The whole ride changed shape behind us. Her fingers closing around the cross at the window. The way her eyes had gone somewhere else when I said T had given it to me. I had been carrying her dead husband through the night and talking as if the world began wherever I happened to park.

She put a cigarette between her lips. The lighter struck twice without catching. I took it gently, lit it, and held the flame steady while she leaned forward.

She told me how she had come to Vegas at twenty-three. A suitcase, eight hundred dollars, and a number a girl in Phoenix had given her. She wanted to sing. The number belonged to Valentino.

I knew Valentino's name from the clubs. I had seen his white suits and the diamond in his tooth, watched men laugh around him and women keep their eyes on his hands. What he had done to Shawna was new to me.

Shawna told me he had bought her clothes and found her a room. He had promised introductions to people in music. By the time she understood what he demanded in return, she said, her clothes, her room, and the life she had started building in them belonged to him.

She blew smoke toward the broken side of the windshield and kept the photograph flat against her knee.

“Dante came into the club on my birthday,” Shawna told me. “Everybody else wanted something. He asked what kind of cake I liked.”

One corner of her mouth rose. She looked down at the photograph before continuing.

“He came back with a whole carrot cake,” she said. “Not a slice. A whole fucking cake. Put it between the bottles like we had all night.”

She told me Valentino had laughed at him. Dante had knocked the diamond tooth out of his mouth.

“My nigga,” I said before I remembered whose story I was hearing.

Shawna looked up from the photograph. “That’s what I said. Exactly what I fucking said.”

She wiped hard beneath one eye, then told me they had married here six weeks later. T stood beside his brother. Red paid for the room. Everybody smiled in the pictures.

I asked what happened. She watched the chapel while she answered.

Dante had taken three million out of a deal. He said they were leaving. He said he had a brother, so he didn't have to be afraid.

I lowered my eyes to the cross. The back had turned outward against my shirt.

Shawna turned the photograph over. Written there were the words behind those initials: Damn, boy. Magnificent.

Shawna described Dante looking at himself in the chapel mirror after their wedding, saying those words as if God had just completed the finishing work. She told me she had nearly dropped her flowers laughing. The next morning she had the words put on his cross.

“Nigga was right about you,” I told her.

“He was talking about himself,” Shawna said, looking up at me.

I looked down at the gold. “Then he finally got it on the right neck.”

The laugh escaped her so suddenly she covered her mouth. For a second the woman from the photograph looked out through the woman beside me. I didn't mistake that for a victory. I was grateful to have seen her.

Then she held the picture to her chest and told me they had left Dante in the desert. Valentino brought her his watch. He said Dante had died begging for somebody to take care of her.

I wanted to kill a man I hadn't met. By then Valentino was already dead, but anger doesn't always concern itself with the schedule.

Shawna told me she had waited eight months. She returned to Valentino and let him assume she had nowhere else to go. By the end, she said, he was telling her she loved him.

“Last Tuesday, I shot him in his car,” she told me. “He was talking when I did it. Kept talking after the first one. So I shot him again.”

Then, she said, she took Dante's watch out of his glove compartment.

She held her wrist where I could see it. The watch was too large. It had turned toward the side of her arm, and she pushed it upright with her thumb.

She told me Red had taken her in afterward and promised protection. Tonight, she said, she had taken the three million back.

I asked where T fitted into this. He had promised to get her out of Vegas, she said.

“You believe him?” I asked Shawna.

Shawna slid the photograph inside her dress. “I believed his brother.”

She closed the case. Then she leaned across the space between our seats and kissed me.

I felt her hand against my face and remembered the man in the photograph. I was wearing his cross. I had just repeated his joke. Part of me wondered how much of that kiss belonged to what she remembered and how much belonged to the man sitting beside her. I hoped she had seen something in me when I went back for Mercedes and Porsche. I didn't ask. Her fingers stayed against my cheek. I wanted her so badly I couldn't make myself pull back.

I tasted smoke. I forgot about the fortune on her lap.

When she pulled back, her fingers stayed against my cheek.

“Don't take me to that house,” Shawna said, still holding my face.

“Then this nigga ain’t taking you,” I said. “Give me somewhere else.”

My first night in the family, and I had already found something I was willing to lose it for.

I took her to a motel outside town. It had an outdoor stairway, six rooms on the upper floor, and a blue sign that blinked as if electricity was being rationed by a man with a personal grievance. I paid for all six rooms and told the clerk nobody was to come upstairs.

He checked the money twice before asking whether I really meant all six. I told him yes. He pushed the keys across the counter without attempting further hospitality.

In our room, Shawna set the case on the second bed and put her purse and pistol on the nightstand. She took Dante's photograph from her dress and laid it beside the lamp. I locked the door and set my pistol on the other side of the bed. Trucks passed on the highway beyond the lot. Downstairs, a couple were arguing beside a car, their ordinary fury rising through the window while the rest of our night waited outside it.

Shawna unbuttoned my shirt to look at my ribs. The bruising had darkened. She touched beside it and said my name softly, without a joke in it.

“I'm good,” I told her.

“You're bleeding,” Shawna said, touching the dark edge of a bruise.

“I've been bleeding all night,” I explained. That seemed a useful distinction to me. She went into the bathroom and came back with a wet towel, which was a fairly complete review of my argument.

She cleaned my face. Her hand steadied my jaw. Whenever the cloth caught the split in my lip, she apologized. I kept telling her not to. It had been a long time since somebody's touch made a hurt easier to carry.

When she finished, I lifted the chain over my head and put the cross in her hand.

She looked down at it. Her thumb found the back. Then she began to cry, bending over the little cross until I moved close enough for her to lean against me.

I held her while she cried. I didn't tell her Dante would want this or that. I hadn't known him. I didn't promise the pain would become useful. I knew how to stay on the edge of the bed and keep my arms around her without making her explain why she was shaking. For once, that was enough for me to do.

After a while she lifted her head.

“Stay,” Shawna said.

“I'm here, baby,” I told her, keeping an arm around her.

“Tonight,” she said. “I mean tonight.”

“I'm right fucking here,” I promised. She pulled me down to kiss her.

She laid the cross beside the photograph and turned the lamp off herself.

We spent what remained of that little piece of night together, with the money on the other bed and our guns close enough to reach. Afterward, she rested against my chest while the blue light from the motel sign moved over the ceiling. There was nothing elegant about the room. The air conditioner rattled. A spring complained whenever I shifted. I remember it as one of the first places where I wanted morning to find me exactly where I was.

Shawna asked what I wanted. I told her I wanted her.

“You don't know me,” she said, her head still against my chest.

“Then tell me more,” I answered.

So she did. She talked about the songs she had wanted to sing. She said her mother in Phoenix still believed she worked in a restaurant. She told me Dante had wanted a house near the water, though neither of them knew how to swim.

I told her we would buy it. I told her we would go wherever she wanted.

I had known her less than three hours. A wiser man might have offered something smaller. At twenty-four, with her head on my chest, I would have tried to carry a fucking coastline into the room if she had said she liked the view.

My phone lit up on the nightstand.

T’s name filled the screen. I let it ring. A moment later a photograph arrived: the Mercedes parked below us, taken from close enough to show the damage along its side.

Under the picture, he had written: Come down, son.

I put my clothes on and slipped my pistol back into my waistband. Shawna sat up behind me. Before I opened the door, I saw her put the photograph and cross inside her purse.

The empty swimming pool lay beyond a metal fence at the far side of the motel lot. T waited beside it with Tank and two men I didn't know. He wore the same shirt he had worn behind the nightclub. My blood still marked one cuff.

I came down with my hands visible. Behind me, our room door closed. I was aware of that sound in a way I wasn't aware of the trucks or the light buzzing over the stairs.

T asked where the money was. Upstairs, I told him. He asked about Shawna. Upstairs too.

T nodded slowly. He looked me up and down before speaking again.

“One thing,” he said. “I gave you one fucking thing to do.”

I told him she had spoken about Dante.

Tank looked away. That frightened me before T answered. My new brother couldn't look at me while I asked about the dead one.

“Dante stole from his family,” T said.

“He was your brother,” I said to T.

“Then the nigga should've known better,” T answered.

I had brought a little hope downstairs with me. It was embarrassing how little I required of him: a denial, another name, anything I could hold between the man who had kissed my forehead and the man who could let his brother die. He did not even bother giving me a lie.

He blamed Shawna instead. Said she had put Dante in the ground and now I was ready to follow.

“You put him in the ground,” I said.

T stepped close. I smelled the same cologne that had been against my face when he gave me the cross. He asked who had made me. Who had given me the car, the money, the name people would now say across the west side.

“You think she would've looked at you without all that?” T asked, pointing toward the car he had given me.

I didn't answer. It hurt because I had spent the first part of the evening believing the gifts had made me worth looking at.

“She’s upstairs with my three million,” he said. “That’s what she’s in love with.”

Tank shifted behind him. I looked toward the sound; T moved closer, filling my sight again, and gave me one last order: go get her.

Above us, the room door opened.

Shawna stood at the balcony rail holding the case. She had come out barefoot. The blue motel light moved across her face while she looked down at T.

“You want your money, T?” Shawna called from the balcony.

He looked up.

She swung the case over the rail and let go.

It struck the empty pool below us. The latches gave, and cash burst across the cracked blue tiles. Bundles split on the slope toward the drain. A few loose bills fluttered long enough for everybody to watch them land.

“There,” Shawna said. “Now tell him what I love.”

T drew his gun toward the balcony.

I struck his arm as he fired. The shot broke a window above the stairs. Tank caught me from behind, locking one arm across my chest. One of the other men came toward me with his fist up. I drove my forehead into his face and felt a bright red pain spread through my own skull.

Tank’s hold loosened. I twisted, drove him into the pool fence, and heard the wire rattle behind his back.

Shawna fired from above. The man beside T dropped.

T shoved his pistol against my side.

The shot felt like an enormous hand closing inside me. Sound came after it, or seemed to. I couldn't take a breath. My knees wanted to fold, and the front of T’s shirt filled my sight because I had fallen against him without letting go.

“Should’ve listened,” he said.

I caught his gun wrist with both hands. Blood ran beneath my shirt. I could feel it moving, could feel my strength trying to follow it out. But the balcony was above his shoulder, and Shawna was on it, and I would not give him that angle again.

Tank hit me. The blow reached me from somewhere far away.

I drove a knee into T, pulled one hand free, and got the pistol from my waistband. Even then, I wanted him to stop. I wanted the man who had called me son to look down at what he had done and become somebody else before I had to answer it.

He kept holding the gun.

I fired. He struck the fence. I fired again, and he went down with his hand still reaching toward me.

The wire shook after he stopped moving.

Tank stared at him, then at the gun in my hand. He began to say my name.

“Back up.” I could barely get the breath behind it. “Tank, back the fuck up.”

He moved away. I tried to remain standing long enough to understand what I had done.

Shawna ran down the stairs with the pistol in her hand and her purse slung over one shoulder. When she reached me, she put the pistol inside the purse and pressed both palms against the blood spreading over my shirt. I heard her tell me to look at her. I tried to tell her I was good. My mouth made the shape; nothing useful came out.

She turned on Tank. “Get the fucking car!”

He looked at T one more time. Then he ran toward the Mercedes.

That was how the west side became mine. Not with the keys landing in my hand, not with T announcing it over the money. With a man I had wanted for a father dead beside the pool and a woman still wearing another man’s watch trying to hold my life inside my body.

We left the cash where it had fallen. I remember that much of it: blue tiles, money everywhere, nobody bending to pick it up while Shawna got me into the car. There wasn't a dollar in that pool that could buy me another minute standing beside it.

She drove. One hand on the wheel, the other holding a towel against my side whenever the road allowed. The sky had begun to lighten over the desert. Behind us, the casinos kept shining, indifferent as a room full of people who hadn't heard their names called yet.

“Stay with me,” Shawna said.

“I'm here,” I told her, forcing enough air into my chest to make it audible.

“Then keep talking,” Shawna said. “Tell me about that house.”

So I talked about the house I had promised at the motel. I told her there would be a room just for her music. We would learn to swim. She asked where the windows would face, and I told her the sunrise. I was bleeding through a motel towel and selling waterfront property to keep the woman driving from hearing how hard I was working for each breath.

She kept asking about the windows. I kept giving her more of them.

At the edge of town, she pulled over. I thought something had gone wrong with the car until she took out the cross. She put the chain around my neck and settled the gold against my chest.

“Don’t,” I said. “That’s his.”

“I know,” Shawna said.

She closed my fingers around it. Then she kissed me and returned to the wheel. The meaning of that would take us longer than one drive to understand. At the time, I held on because she had asked me to.

The sun rose beside her face. My phone kept ringing. Some numbers I recognized from T's people or Red's. Others meant nothing to me. On the third call, I answered with my name.

There was a silence at the other end. Then a man asked what I wanted them to do.

I looked at Shawna. She watched the road. Dante’s watch had slipped around her wrist again, but she couldn't spare a hand to turn it back.

“Some nigga better find me a doctor,” I said. “And tell everybody the girl is with me.”

I had started the night looking for somewhere to belong. By sunrise, belonging had stopped meaning the men who would let me stand beside them. It meant the woman beside me, the people we had stopped for, and whatever I would have to stand through now that I had chosen them.

Ten toes down. All the way to the end.

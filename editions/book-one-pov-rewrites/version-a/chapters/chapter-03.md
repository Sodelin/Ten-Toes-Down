## 3. A King with a Hole in Him

At the hospital, a doctor asked Shawna who she was to me.

“His wife,” she said.

I heard that. I had missed pieces of the drive: whole stretches of road, questions I must have answered because she stopped asking them. My mother’s kitchen kept appearing where the dashboard ought to be. Then I would see the empty swimming pool again, and hear T calling me son, and want to tell him he had lost the fucking right.

But wife came through clean.

I tried to live harder when she said it. Whatever blood I had left in me received a promotion.

The doctor asked for a name. Shawna gave her own.

“His name,” the doctor repeated.

“Aiden,” Shawna said. “His name is Aiden.”

They had brought a bed alongside the one from the entrance. Hands reached beneath me to move me across, but Shawna had both fists caught in my shirt. My blood had dried brown over the front of her black dress. One earring was missing. Her bare feet stood on a floor polished enough to show her reflection, in a hospital that smelled of flowers and probably charged extra for letting them die naturally.

The doctor told her to let go.

I managed to say, “Baby.”

Shawna looked down at me. I wanted to tell her something that would stay if I didn't. Instead, I felt the chain against my neck and saw the scissors in somebody’s hand.

“Don't let them cut my chain,” I told Shawna, trying to lift my hand toward it.

She opened her fists. Her fingers shook while she lifted the gold over my head, but she did it herself. She kissed the cross, put it around her own neck, and stepped back just far enough for the waiting hands to take me.

The ceiling moved. Then it went away.

When it returned, a woman in a white coat stood beside the bed studying a chart. I had a tube in my arm, a dry mouth, and the impression that somebody had removed the middle of my body and returned it without consulting the instructions.

“Dr. Marisol Vega,” she said when she saw my eyes follow her. “You’re alive.”

I asked for Shawna. Vega pointed toward the window.

She was curled in a chair with my bloody shirt gathered against her stomach. Somebody had put hospital socks on her feet. The cross lay against her collarbone, and Dante’s watch had slipped down over the narrow part of her wrist. In the morning light, I could see the darkened skin beneath her eyes. At the Saint she had held her back straight even while she stood still. Asleep, she curled around the shirt. I wanted to keep the room quiet around her.

I told Vega not to wake her.

“She’ll wake herself,” the doctor said. “She has been doing it every eleven minutes.”

I tried to sit up. Pain drove through my side so hard the room lost its edges. I fell back against the pillow and said the only word I had ready.

“Fuck,” I said into the pillow.

“Yes,” Vega agreed. “That. You will be doing quite a lot of that if you continue.”

She waited until I stopped gripping the sheet, then explained what I needed to know. They had treated the damage. The bullet was still inside me; taking it out would create risks I didn't need. What I required now was time, which was inconvenient because I had never considered time a serious participant in my plans.

I stared at the ceiling. T was dead and had still found a way to move in with me.

“How much time?” I asked Vega.

“More than you want,” she said, holding one hand beside the bed until I stopped trying to rise.

I told her there were men waiting downstairs and a whole west side I couldn't run horizontally. She said they could keep waiting. She checked the dressing, looked at the monitor, and continued talking to me as if owning territory did not exempt a man from the ordinary problems of having organs.

I couldn't lift my head without making myself dizzy. When she helped me drink, I needed her to hold the cup. That is something I remember whenever people describe power as a thing a man simply possesses. I could have owned every building I saw through that window and still needed a woman to tilt a fucking straw toward my mouth.

I tried to take the cup from her. My hand slipped. Water hit the sheet; the cup struck the floor.

Shawna came out of the chair before it stopped rolling.

Shawna said my name and reached for me, touching my face, my shoulders, the hair above my ear. Her eyes passed over the bandages without stopping. I held still under her hands.

“Right here,” I told her.

“You scared me,” Shawna said, pressing her forehead briefly against mine.

I apologized. She kissed my forehead, slapped my arm, and told me not to be sorry—to stop doing it. Then she kissed me again. Her tears reached my mouth. I could taste them when I tried to speak.

Vega set a fresh cup within reach and left us alone.

I watched Shawna wipe her face with the back of her hand. The cross moved when she breathed.

“You told them you're my wife,” I said to Shawna.

“They weren't going to let me in,” she told me.

“Could've said sister,” I suggested, watching her wipe her cheek.

She looked at me over her wet knuckles. “I wanted them to let me in. I didn’t want to make it weird when I kissed you.”

I laughed and regretted it physically. The joke was excellent. My side was simply a hater.

I asked her to say it again. She asked which part. I told her I meant what she had told the doctor.

She sat on the edge of the bed, careful of the tubes.

“Nigga, you trying to die married?” Shawna asked.

“Trying to live that way,” I told her.

Her hand stopped against my cheek. Her eyes widened, and she didn't answer. I wondered whether the question frightened her. I was asking for a life before we had finished surviving the night that introduced us. I meant it. Meaning it didn't make it a small thing to ask.

She lowered her head onto the pillow beside mine.

“Let me get you home first,” Shawna whispered.

That afternoon, Tank appeared in the doorway with flowers.

The suit was fresh. His shoes had dirt around the soles. A bruise marked his forehead, and he held the bouquet out from his body with the uncertainty of a man who could lift an engine but hadn't been briefed on stems.

“These for her,” he said.

Shawna stood beside my bed. She didn't reach for them.

“Put the flowers down,” I told him, “before we both start feeling strange.”

He set them on the table by the door. I watched his hands come away empty. Those hands had held me while T tried to kill me. They had also gone for the car when Shawna told him to. I wanted to hit him again. I wanted him close. I wanted a man that size between this room and the people looking for us, and I couldn't yet tell whether he was one of them.

I asked whether he had come alone. He said the men downstairs were waiting. T’s men, or what had been T’s men. They wanted to know whose men they were now.

Before he answered that part, he glanced at Shawna.

“I’m not leaving,” she said.

“Didn't ask you to,” Tank told Shawna.

“You looked,” she said, keeping herself between his flowers and my bed.

Tank spread his empty hands. “I got eyes.”

I held out my hand to her. She took it without taking her eyes off him.

T had gone to the funeral people, Tank told me. Of the two other men at the pool, one was alive and one wasn't. Shawna looked down. I felt her fingers stiffen around mine and squeezed them gently. There would be time for what she needed to say about the man she had shot. I wasn't going to decide for her whether that meant relief, guilt, or a silence we sat through together.

She asked about the money.

Tank studied the foot of my bed before answering. “Pool was empty when I got back.”

“Three million doesn't walk,” Shawna told him.

“Plenty people got legs,” Tank answered, still watching the foot of the bed.

She let my hand go.

“Look at me when you say it,” Shawna told Tank.

He did. He told her he had looked, that people moved fast when nobody was watching, that the money wasn't there.

I believed him then. There was still room in me for that particular kind of stupidity. I wanted the man who had brought the car to be the man I could keep, and I was lying in a bed without enough blood to investigate every wish I had.

“Leave it,” I said. “She threw it away to save me. It saved me. That’s money spent.”

Tank looked from me to the monitors.

“You always this rich?” Tank asked me.

“I woke up,” I told him.

He stayed quiet for a moment. Then he nodded. “Yeah. You did.”

Tank told me what was happening outside. Suppliers were waiting with questions. Men who owed the club money had started asking whether their debts died with T. Red wasn't answering calls from the west side, and T's office remained locked. I could imagine what that looked like to men I knew: respect for the dead until somebody found a way to make it pay.

T had announced my place publicly. Keeping it was another matter.

“You keeping me there?” I asked Tank.

He worked his jaw and reminded me that he had driven the car.

“That ain't what I asked,” I said to Tank. “I'm asking about now.”

Shawna moved to the window. Sunlight caught Dante’s watch. Tank looked at it longer than he needed to, then looked back at me.

“I ain't putting another man in the desert,” Tank told me.

He went downstairs to bring the others up. I watched the doorway after he had gone. It wasn't the promise I had asked for, but it was the one he had managed to give me.

Vega returned to find nine men in my room, two in the hallway, and a duffel bag occupying the visitor’s chair. Shawna had stepped into the adjoining bathroom. I could hear the tap running. The men stood around my bed. Holsters showed when their jackets opened, and their faces stayed blank in a way I had learned to be careful around.

“Absolutely not,” Vega said.

The older man holding a stack of papers stopped halfway through a sentence. Nobody else moved.

Vega pointed at the door. “Two visitors. The rest leave.”

They looked at me. I could feel the little test taking shape: whose authority counted when the woman in the white coat contradicted the men in expensive shoes?

“She said move,” I told them. “You want the woman who kept me alive having to step over you?”

They moved so fast the last two reached the doorway together. Each stopped to let the other through, then stepped forward again. Vega pointed once more. They abandoned manners and fitted themselves through sideways.

Tank stayed, along with the older man from T’s club. The papers came onto the bed beside my uninjured hip. Money would arrive Friday, he explained, but people needed paying today.

I asked what was in the bag.

“Yesterday,” the older man said, nodding toward the duffel.

Tank opened it. Cash reached the zipper. Less than a day before, I had been counting what I could spend on food. Now men were describing a fortune by the day of the fucking week.

I told the older man to pay the people who had worked. He wanted to know whether I meant from this money.

“Unless you got some magic beans,” I told the older man. “They worked. Pay them.”

He made a mark on the paper. His pen sounded absurdly small in the room.

Then he said Red wanted a meeting. Face to face. I told him Red could call, because if he looked at my face he might notice I was busy trying to keep it attached to the rest of my life.

The man swallowed. Tank closed the duffel.

For a moment they both waited. I had seen T sit silent until the men around him stopped talking and began looking at their hands. I didn't know how to be him. I was twenty-four, leaking under a dressing, with Shawna’s lip gloss on my forehead. All I knew was that the chair T had given me came with people attached to it, and I wasn't giving him the last word about how they were treated.

“Nobody touches Shawna,” I said. “Nobody says her name to Red. Anybody got a problem can leave the keys on the bed.”

The older man looked at Tank. Tank looked at me.

“You heard him,” Tank told the older man.

The keys stayed in the older man’s pocket.

After he left, I sank back against the pillows. The conversation had emptied me. I could feel dampness beneath the dressing and couldn't hide the effort it took to draw a full breath.

Tank moved the bag closer and offered to count it.

“I want you watching that door like your brother in here,” I told Tank.

“Who am I watching for?” he asked, his hand on the duffel.

“Every fucking nigga,” I answered.

He carried the chair into the hall. That was the first night he guarded me.

Before it got dark, I heard a woman beyond the door. Then I saw the red velvet coat, the bags, and a face I would come to associate with some of Shawna's oldest stories.

“Where the fuck is she?” a woman shouted outside my door. A moment later I would learn her name was Rosa.

Shawna said her name and crossed the room. Rosa dropped what she was carrying and caught her. They held each other in the narrow space between my bed and the window while Tank quietly brought the abandoned bags inside.

Rosa had copper hair and a broken-heart tattoo beneath her ear. I came to know the rest through the two women in that room: she was thirty-four, sang at the Saint on weekends, and took paying men upstairs when the singing didn't cover what life demanded. She had known Shawna when the suitcase from Phoenix still had its tags. Watching them hold each other, I was seeing a piece of Shawna's life I had only begun to hear about.

She scolded into Shawna’s hair. Why hadn't she answered her phone? When Shawna said she had broken it, Rosa told her to borrow one, send a pigeon, do anything except let Rosa hear she was dead from some man in a rhinestone hat.

“I’m here,” Shawna said.

“I see you,” Rosa told Shawna. “I'm mad anyway.”

Rosa held her at arm’s length and checked her face. Only then did she turn toward me. She asked whether I was the one who had shot T.

I said yes.

“Good,” Rosa said.

She came to the bed and kissed my forehead, putting another mark over the one Shawna had left.

“Stay alive,” Rosa told me. “She doesn't have another black dress in her.”

Shawna’s face crumpled. Rosa turned back and brought her close before I could see the tears properly.

The duffel sat beside me with more money than I had known what to do with. I couldn't make it do a single useful thing for either woman in front of me. It couldn't return a phone call from the past or make one night’s frightened waiting unhappen. I lay there learning the size of what cash failed to reach.

Rosa unpacked clothes, food, and an old songbook. She told us she had kept it after Dante died. Shawna opened it in the middle, looked down at the page, and shut it fast enough for the paper to snap.

“Later,” Shawna said, keeping her hand on the cover.

Rosa put it on the table where she could reach it. “Whenever.”

That evening Rosa painted Shawna’s nails by the window. Tank ate in the hallway, where I could hear him negotiating with a plastic fork. More money came from downstairs, and I told them to leave it there. My room held two women talking softly, the smell of real food, and a man outside who kept shifting and making the chair creak but hadn't left it. I wanted to keep that arrangement for a while.

Later, Shawna fell asleep against me. Her hand rested near the bandage without touching it.

She whispered Dante’s name.

I heard it. Of course I heard it. I had been listening to every breath she took.

I lay still until morning. I didn't know yet how to make room for a man who was gone without feeling him take space from me. The only decent thing I knew to do was let her sleep.

On the third afternoon, somebody delivered a coffin.

The nurse called from the private entrance. She sounded offended, which I respected. She worked in an expensive hospital, and someone had rolled a direct criticism of the establishment through the doors.

She said there was an item for me. I asked whether it was flowers. Larger than flowers, she said. I tried piano next.

“It is a coffin, Mr. Aiden,” the nurse said over the phone.

Across the room, Tank stood so fast his food hit the floor.

I told the nurse not to open it.

“I wasn't planning to try it on,” the nurse told me.

Shawna took the phone from me and began telling her to move everyone away. I reached for my trousers. Tank told me no. I told him to get my shoes. He pointed out that I already had hospital socks.

“Then get shoes worthy of the fucking socks,” I told Tank.

Mercedes and Porsche were visiting. They had brought fruit, champagne nobody was allowed to open, and an enlarged photograph of the Mercedes in the air above the boat. Somebody on the sidewalk had caught the jump. You could see Shawna screaming and me concentrating with tremendous visual appeal.

Mercedes went to the window. I pulled myself upright and joined her, one hand against the wall. Through the glass entrance below us, I could see two men beside a black lacquer coffin on a trolley. Silver handles gleamed along the sides. My name crossed the lid in letters big enough to make dying seem like a sponsorship opportunity.

“Even your enemies know you’re expensive,” she said.

Porsche stood beside her counting the men near two white sedans across the private drive.

“Four more by the cars,” Porsche said. “Those two aren't delivering shit.”

I looked beyond the coffin toward the white sedans. The men outside kept watching the entrance instead of the street. One adjusted his jacket without taking his eyes off it. The men by the coffin reached beneath theirs.

Tank dragged me back as the first shot broke glass below us.

The hospital changed in a breath. Alarms started. Somebody screamed beneath the balcony. A man ordered people onto the floor. In the hall outside our room, the brisk sounds of ordinary work became running feet.

I took my pistol from the clothes piled beside Tank's chair. I told Shawna to take Rosa into the bathroom and lock the door.

She started to argue. I caught her eyes.

“Be alive when I come back so you can cuss me out,” I told Shawna.

She pressed her lips together, caught Rosa's hand, and went into the bathroom. Mercedes followed them with a heavy vase in both hands, taking a piece of the hospital’s expensive taste into the defensive plan.

Porsche stayed by the door and pointed at the wheelchair.

“The man can't walk fast,” Porsche told Tank. “He can roll.”

I had spent most of the argument defending my feet. Porsche had found me wheels. I appreciated that.

Tank helped me into the chair and pulled my shoes on over the socks. Porsche pushed me into the hallway while he checked the corner ahead. A nurse was struggling with a bed farther along. The patient had one leg in a cast and was trying to hold the back of his gown closed while she steered. The bed kept catching the wall because every time he reached for the rail, his ass became a public matter.

I told Porsche to help them. She wanted to know who would push me.

“I got hands,” I told Porsche, setting them on the wheel rims.

She caught the foot of the bed before it struck the corner. I laid the pistol across my lap, gripped the wheel rims, and followed Tank. Each turn pulled at my side. The chair traveled about as fast as a man pretending the pain wasn't deciding the route. Whenever Tank stopped, I took the gun up again.

A gunman came through the stairwell door at the far end. He stopped facing Tank. Then his eyes dropped to my chair, and he smiled.

“That's the king?” the gunman asked, pointing at me.

“That’s the nigga,” Tank said.

The man lifted his gun. Tank threw himself against the wall, leaving a clear line between me and the weapon. I leaned past the arm of the chair and fired. The gunman fell backward through the stairwell door, and his weapon skidded across the polished floor. More feet sounded on the stairs behind him.

Porsche and the nurse got the bed around the corner. The old man had both hands clamped over his gown. I told him not to look down.

“Wasn't planning to, son,” the old man told me, gripping his gown.

“Good,” I answered. “Your nigga's got this.”

A shot struck the sprinkler above us. Water burst across the ceiling and fell over the corridor. Tank seized the back of my chair and ran.

The wheels rattled over seams. Water hit my face. I held my wounded side with one hand and kept the pistol up with the other. I twisted in the chair and saw two men come around the corner behind us. The man I had shot carried one arm low; the other held his pistol out in both hands. Their shoes slid as they tried to chase across the wet floor.

I shouted Tank’s name. He said he saw them.

“Then quit sightseeing and push!” I shouted up at Tank.

We struck the swinging doors to the atrium and went through onto an upper gallery overlooking the entrance. The nurse and Porsche brought the bed after us. To our left, stairs curved down beside the wall; a service lift stood in a recessed opening beyond them. Both routes emerged near the main floor’s far exit. Below, the gunmen at the coffin controlled that stretch of open floor.

The coffin and its trolley were now in the shallow fountain beneath the gallery. One delivery man crouched behind it. The other stood in the water with his gun aimed up at us.

I fired before he did. His raised arm dropped, and he fell against the fountain's rim. His gun hit the water beyond his reach. The man behind the coffin shot back. Tank pulled my chair away from the edge as the shots struck the tiles and cracked a section of the rail. The chair tipped. I caught the arm, went over with it, and landed on one knee. My lungs emptied so completely I couldn't make a sound about what my side was doing.

Tank fired back through the gallery doors, forcing the men pursuing us into the corridor. Porsche stood in front of the nurse and her patient with a chair raised across her chest. The lift was only a few yards away, but I could see the gunman below watching its doors. He could catch them when they reached the ground floor. Staying here meant waiting for the men behind us to come through again.

I looked down at the coffin with my name on it.

“Who sent it?” I shouted.

The man using it for cover looked up. He actually answered.

“Who you think?” the gunman below called back.

“I think your taste is fucking awful,” I told him.

I kicked the overturned wheelchair toward the broken section of rail. It scraped across the wet tile, tipped into space, and fell into the fountain. The frame hit the coffin and shoved it sideways through the water. Its trolley buckled. The gunman sprang out from behind it, and Tank fired from above, driving him behind a marble column.

That opened the route to the lift. It didn't keep it open. The man could come back around the column before the bed reached the floor, and the gunmen in the corridor were still trying to get through behind us.

I got my feet under me and stood. The pain stood too. That motherfucker was committed.

Tank followed my gaze over the broken rail.

“Don't you dare,” he warned me.

“Get them down,” I told him. “Catch up.”

I went over.

The coffin lid took my feet in the middle of my own engraved name. Black wood split. Water burst over the sides. My legs bent farther than I wanted, and a white shock ran up through the wound. I stayed upright by putting a hand against the broken lid and refusing to consider what the other option would cost.

People lay behind furniture all across the lobby. They stared at me standing in a coffin in a fountain with a gun. I had come to this hospital for rest. The reviews were going to be complicated.

The man behind the column charged.

He didn't fire. I never learned whether his gun was empty or he had simply decided the wounded man ought to accept delivery. I tore a loose length of the lid upward and swung it into his shoulder.

The wood cracked. He fell against me. We went into the fountain together, his hands reaching for my throat while the water closed over my mouth. My side opened into a kind of pain I couldn't separate from the struggle. I drove him away with my forearm, came up coughing, and brought the pistol close enough to his face for him to stop.

I asked whether he was finished. He nodded, lying half against the broken coffin.

“Use your words,” I told the man in the fountain.

“Finished,” he said, keeping his hands where I could see them.

“Then stay fucking finished,” I told him.

Tank came down the stairs while Porsche and the nurse used the service lift with the bed. He reached the fountain first, kept his gun on the man, and gave me an arm thick enough to trust my full weight to. I had no strength left for making the climb look good.

I put my pistol back in my waistband while Tank held me upright.

The lift opened across the lobby. Porsche checked the way before the nurse pushed the bed toward the exit. The old man lifted a fist at me as they passed.

“That's my boy!” the old man shouted from the bed.

I had never seen him before that morning. I heard pride in his shout and took it anyway. I was collecting fathers faster than the bad ones could die.

Through the broken entrance I saw one of the men from the upstairs corridor burst out of a side door and run to the second white sedan. Another man already had the first sedan running. Both tried to leave. The lead car reached the gate just as a police cruiser struck it broadside. The second stopped behind it with nowhere to go. A woman came out of the cruiser with a long gun. Her dark glasses stayed exactly where they belonged while she shouted for the men to show their hands.

Every hand in those cars appeared with remarkable enthusiasm.

Chief Gloria Knox came through the broken entrance a couple of minutes later. She was fifty-three, as I learned afterward, with silver at her temples and a blue uniform so crisp it looked personally disappointed in every wet surface around her. She checked the fountain, the coffin, the broken gallery rail, and finally me.

She asked whether I had jumped from there.

“Nigga was in my way,” I told Knox.

“And that solved it?” she asked, looking from me to the broken coffin.

The man in the fountain groaned behind Tank.

Knox looked at him. “Apparently.”

I heard an officer call that the corridor was clear. Then Vega arrived with Shawna, Rosa, and Mercedes. My doctor saw my wet trousers and the blood spreading beneath my shirt. She said my name in a tone that made the bullet feel almost conversational.

I told her everybody around us was alive.

“You were in bed,” Vega said to me.

“Didn't have wheels,” I explained, leaning harder on Tank.

Porsche raised a hand. “I tried a chair.”

Vega shut her eyes. When she opened them again, she looked past me at the nurse beside the bed. The nurse was shaking now that the movement had stopped.

“You saved my nurse,” Vega said.

“She saved my ass first,” I told Vega, nodding toward the nurse.

The nurse began crying. The old man demanded to shake my hand. I offered the one that wasn't braced against Tank and tried not to let him see how little grip remained in it.

Knox put a card where I could take it.

“When you're tired of people sending coffins, come see me,” Knox said, holding out the card. “I'd rather give you a badge.”

Tank stared at her. “This nigga?”

“Especially this nigga,” Knox told Tank.

Before I could decide what to say to that, Shawna got her arms around me. She laughed, called me an impossible motherfucker, and kissed my face in front of the chief of police. Her hands moved carefully around the dressing, though she kept telling me what she ought to do to my ass. I stood with fountain water running out of my clothes while the people around us watched. I felt as if I had arrived from somewhere higher than the gallery.

They put me in another chair. Vega supervised that part personally.

That evening, I watched from my window as the broken coffin left on the back of a truck. Two of the captured attackers sat beside it in handcuffs. The man from the fountain kept looking up at my window. I raised my hand in a small wave. He looked away before I had finished it.

Then I let Vega put me back in bed. I had run out of arguments and, more seriously, out of the ability to remain upright while making them.

The days that followed did not resemble a victory montage. I slept. I woke sore. I learned that crossing the room and sitting down again could constitute a full morning’s work. By the time five days had passed in the hospital, Vega allowed me to leave. She made it clear that leaving meant she trusted my people to keep me reasonably still. It did not mean my body had forgiven me.

“No heroics,” she said at the door.

Tank waited behind my wheelchair with two duffel bags and Rosa’s red coat. Before we left the room, Shawna lifted Dante’s chain off her own neck and settled it around mine again, easing the cross flat against the clean shirt. Then she promised Vega I would behave.

Vega turned to her. “You either.”

Shawna nodded. Vega held her eyes until she nodded again. I watched their agreement take shape and felt several of my preferred methods leave the building without me.

Downstairs, six black cars waited under the entrance canopy. Men straightened beside them when Tank rolled me out. One opened the Mercedes door and welcomed me back.

Back. As if I had possessed a kingdom before I had a woman. As if these men hadn't learned my name while I bled into somebody else’s sheets. The word carried expectations I wasn't strong enough to lift, so I concentrated on the smaller task directly in front of me.

I stood up.

I wanted Shawna to see me do it. When my knees trembled, she took my arm and held it firmly against her side. I hoped the men by the cars would mistake the help for simple affection.

“Where we going?” Shawna asked, keeping hold of my arm.

Beyond the canopy, the city moved in bright afternoon traffic. We had people, cars, money, enemies, and a bullet that would apparently be joining all future plans. The one ordinary thing we did not possess was somewhere that belonged to both of us.

I looked down at her.

“Home, baby,” I told her. “We just gotta get one.”

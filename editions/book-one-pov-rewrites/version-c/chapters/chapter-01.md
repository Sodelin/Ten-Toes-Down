## 1. The Funeral Had a Two-Drink Minimum

“Nigga, it’s my motherfucking funeral,” Aiden told the man behind the folding table.

The man kept his hand out. A cash box stood between his knees. On the table, a stack of memorial programs pinned down the corner of a sign: FORTY DOLLARS. Behind him, a second sign announced a two-drink minimum. The first few bars of a trumpet exercise came through the ballroom curtains and stopped on a note that made a woman in line put a finger in her ear.

“Forty dollars,” the ticket man said.

Aiden pointed to the six-foot photograph beside the entrance. His photographed face hovered above a white robe and a pair of angel wings. One shoulder still bore a police officer’s hand. Below that hand, the image ended at a gold border before it reached the missing handcuffs.

“I’m Aiden,” he said.

The ticket man looked from the photograph to the man pointing at it. He leaned sideways to examine the picture without the reflection of the chandelier across its face.

“You look better in the picture,” he told Aiden.

“Nigga, I’m alive,” Aiden said.

The man tapped the cash box. “Then you can pay.”

Aiden took his phone out. A waiter pushed through the curtains carrying an empty tray, and warm air rolled past him into the lobby. Butter, perfume, cigar smoke caught in somebody’s coat. Inside the ballroom, Tank stood beside a table with a plate raised almost to his chin. His phone vibrated against the cloth. He looked at the name, took another shrimp, and answered.

“Why there a cover charge at my funeral?” Aiden asked. “We get home to Vegas and y’all start selling tickets to my problems?”

“The shrimp was expensive,” Tank said into his phone.

“Why do I have shrimp?” Aiden asked.

Tank lifted one by the tail and inspected it. “You died successful.”

Aiden moved the phone away from his ear and looked directly through the opening. “Nigga, are you eating?”

“Grieving,” Tank said. He put the shrimp in his mouth.

The waiter let the curtain fall between them. Aiden ended the call, took two twenties from his wallet, and laid them on the table. The ticket man checked the bills under the light. Then he caught Aiden’s wrist and brought a rubber stamp down against it.

DECEASED appeared above Aiden’s watch in purple letters. The ticket man blew across the wet ink before releasing him.

“That gets you one well drink,” he said.

Aiden pointed at the other sign. “One? Nigga, the sign says two-drink minimum.”

“Second one’s your responsibility,” the ticket man said, straightening the bills inside his box.

Behind Aiden, a man in a black shirt leaned around him. He looked at the photograph, then at the stamped wrist, then back at the photograph. His companion had already begun opening the camera on her phone.

“Hold up,” the man said. “Ain’t that the deceased?”

The ticket man glanced at Aiden’s wrist. “Paid deceased.”

The man stepped forward and offered Aiden his phone. “Can I get a picture?”

“Of me alive?” Aiden asked.

“People gonna think I knew you personally,” the man said.

Aiden spread his free hand between them. “Nigga, you meeting me personally.”

The man moved closer to the portrait. “Yeah, but the funeral picture hit different.”

His companion raised her phone. Aiden walked between them and through the curtains as the shutter clicked. The picture caught half his face, the full photograph, and the man holding an arm around several inches of empty air.

Inside, round tables crowded a central aisle leading to a low stage. Two shallow steps rose at its left end. A closed coffin stood on the platform in front of the band, flowers banked around its wheeled stand. Behind it waited two keyboards, a piano, drums, and three brass players with their instruments resting across their knees. Black cloth had been wound around the white pillars on either side. Where a strip came loose, a waiter pushed it back into place with the handle of a serving spoon.

A mirrored wall ran above the bar on the right. On the left, near a chocolate fountain, a round window in the swinging kitchen door flashed with the movement of cooks and waiters. Farther along the stage, beyond the piano, another doorway opened into the backstage passage.

Aiden stopped at the chocolate fountain and unfolded his program. He turned it over, turned it back, and held it toward Shawna as she approached.

“Why this say I’m survived by eleven children?” he asked.

Shawna put her finger under the small heading above the list. “Those are sponsors.”

Aiden read the first line aloud. “Jeremiah.”

“Jeremiah’s Collision Repair,” Shawna said. She opened the fold he had bent over the rest of the name.

His wife wore a black dress cut high along one leg, a small hat tilted above her eyebrow, and a diamond at her throat. Aiden lowered the program and slipped his arm around her waist. She set one hand on his chest, clear of the wet stamp when he brought his other hand up.

“You enjoying widowhood?” he asked.

“The parking is excellent,” Shawna said.

He drew her closer. She kissed the edge of his mouth, then put two fingers against it. Her eyes moved to the stage. Mercedes stood beside the steps in a red suit, pressing her thumbnail into the middle of her other palm. Porsche waited near the backstage doorway with a silver case at her feet.

“Eight minutes,” Shawna said to Aiden. “Kitchen. Stay there until we get the case.”

Aiden looked at the kitchen door, then back at her. “Soon as Silas walks in, I could take it.”

“And his men could start shooting into everybody who paid to eat shrimp,” Shawna said.

A waiter passed behind her with two plates. Aiden watched a shrimp slide toward the rim of the nearer one and caught it with his program before it fell. The waiter thanked him and continued down the aisle. A spot of butter spread across Jeremiah’s name.

“Apparently excellent shrimp,” Aiden said.

Shawna took the program from him. “Aiden.”

He lowered his hands. Mercedes had crossed from the stage and stopped beside them. She reached up to straighten his collar. The skin in the middle of her palm held a pale crescent where her nail had been.

“Don’t do the thing,” Mercedes told him.

“Which thing?” Aiden asked.

Mercedes turned toward the giant portrait visible between the open curtains. “The thing where this becomes about you.”

Aiden followed her look. “My photograph is six feet tall.”

“Exactly,” Mercedes said. “The nigga has enough publicity.”

He stopped smiling. Mercedes smoothed his collar once more and let it go.

“He’s really bringing the originals?” Aiden asked her.

“The drive’s in his case,” Mercedes said. “He showed me the folders on the call. Then he started naming people who wanted to buy them.”

Aiden looked toward Porsche’s case. “And we leave him that one.”

Porsche came over from the stage, keeping the case in her hand. “Mercedes checks his. I make the switch. You stay where the knives belong to somebody else.”

Aiden glanced back at the kitchen. “Nigga, I can behave around potatoes.”

“Start there,” Porsche said.

Mercedes rubbed her palm against her trouser leg. Aiden reached toward the hand and stopped short of taking it.

“You can sing every one of those songs again,” he said. “If he’s done anything to that drive—”

Mercedes shook her head. “He hasn’t. It’s worth more to him whole. And I can sing the songs, Aiden. I can’t sing that night again.”

The trumpet player tested another note. Mercedes waited for it to end before continuing.

“That studio’s gone,” she said. “Half the band lives somewhere else. On the last chorus, my voice cracks. You remember?”

Aiden nodded. “You told them to leave it.”

“It sounded like me,” Mercedes said. She closed her hand, opened it, and held it flat against her thigh. “I want that back. I don’t want to stand here explaining what he took after you put it through a wall.”

“You’ll get your music,” Aiden told her.

Mercedes squeezed his shoulder. “I know. I want to enjoy getting it.”

She returned to the stage with Porsche. Shawna watched them go, then put the folded program on the table.

“He’s coming for the auction,” she told Aiden. “Your watch, the cars, the collection. He thinks Mercedes and I are settling everything after the memorial.”

Aiden looked down at his watch. “That nigga steals my boat, now he gets to shop at my funeral.”

“It got him out from behind his bulletproof glass,” Shawna said. “Let him get all the way into the room.”

She kissed Aiden once and turned him toward the kitchen.

“Kitchen,” she said.

Aiden went through the swinging door. “Y’all got one word for me tonight.”

The door swung back behind him. A cook looked up from a tray of potatoes, saw the purple stamp on Aiden’s wrist, and moved a hot pan away from the edge of the counter.

“Don’t lean there,” the cook said. “We’ve got enough going on.”

***

Tank climbed the two stage steps with his plate and took the microphone. A waiter approached the plate with an empty hand. Tank shifted it to the other side of his body. The waiter withdrew.

“We gathered here because my brother would want these niggas supporting the bar,” Tank said.

Glasses rose across the ballroom. At one table, a man raised two. His neighbor reached up and took the second one back. Tank waited until the laughter and the clinking had settled.

“Aiden was generous,” he said. “Nigga gave me the shirt off his back.”

In the kitchen, Aiden pushed the door open with his shoulder and leaned through the gap.

“Police was looking for a man in a red shirt,” Aiden called.

A woman near the stage slapped the table. Laughter spread down the aisle. Aiden came another half step through the doorway. Shawna lifted one finger without turning in her chair. He backed into the kitchen again, and the cook drew his potato tray away from Aiden’s elbow.

Tank lowered his plate to a stand beside the microphone.

“But my nigga came back,” Tank said.

Several people stopped laughing. Tank rubbed the side of his thumb against the microphone handle. In the kitchen, Aiden placed his palm flat against the door.

“Came to the station,” Tank said. “Told them I hadn’t done shit. Paid everything. Waited six hours. Bought me breakfast.”

The drummer lowered both sticks to his knees. At the nearest table, a woman set her glass down without letting its base strike the wood.

Tank sniffed into the microphone. “Then the nigga asked for his shirt.”

The drummer rolled beneath the laugh. Tank picked his plate back up. In the kitchen, Aiden bent his head, grinning, while the door moved gently against his palm.

A boo came from the entrance.

Silas Gold walked between the curtains wearing a white fur coat. Four men followed him. The smallest carried a silver case. The largest turned sideways to pass between two chairs and still struck both of them with his knees. Neither seated guest objected. They moved their chairs after he had gone by.

Silas raised a bottle toward Tank. “Miss the good part?”

Tank looked at the closed coffin. “Depends how this speech goes.”

Silas laughed. His men joined him a beat later. He went to the steps, where Shawna rose to meet him. She held out her hand. When he bent to kiss it, she turned her wrist slightly. His lips closed against the thumb of the hand he had wrapped around hers.

Shawna withdrew her hand. “The recordings.”

“After my tribute,” Silas said.

“Mercedes inspects them first,” Shawna said. “We agreed.”

Silas extended his bottle toward the crowded tables. “Darling, look at the room. Can’t buy timing.”

He climbed onto the platform and took the microphone from Tank. The small guard followed with the case. At a motion from Silas, the largest man went through the musicians and stopped in the backstage doorway, directly in front of Porsche.

Silas set his bottle on the coffin. Tank picked it up and handed it back.

Silas put it down again. Tank returned it again. The drummer tapped a small tick against the rim of his snare each time the bottle changed hands. Three guests near the stage began keeping count on their fingers.

“You got somewhere to be?” Silas asked Tank.

“Nigga, eventually,” Tank said.

Silas tucked the bottle against his side and faced the audience.

“Aiden and I had differences,” he said.

At the kitchen window, Aiden’s mouth moved against the glass. The cook glanced at him and continued turning potatoes.

“But he was a man of appetite,” Silas said. He looked down at Shawna. “Appetite leaves things unfinished. People unprovided for. I would never presume—”

“Then don’t,” Shawna said.

A woman at the front table struck the cloth with her palm. “That nigga got instructions!”

Silas stood still while the room laughed. A little of the bottle’s contents ran over his thumb. He wiped it against the fur before lifting the microphone again.

“Mercedes,” he said. “Up here.”

She remained at the foot of the steps.

Silas beckoned the small guard closer. The guard held the case against the side of the coffin and opened both catches. Silas put his bottle down, took out the hard drive, and raised it between two fingers. He nodded the guard back down to the floor with the empty case.

“Perhaps our little lady could sing a tribute,” Silas said. “And apologize for some things she’s said about me.”

Mercedes put a foot on the bottom step. “You promised an inspection.”

“Sing first,” Silas said.

Behind the band, Porsche moved left. The large guard moved left with her. She shifted the duplicate case to her other hand and looked past him toward Mercedes.

Silas held out the microphone. Mercedes reached toward it. He pulled it back before her fingers closed.

“Ask nicely,” Silas said.

In the kitchen, Aiden took off his jacket. The cook stopped with the spoon above the potatoes.

“You want me to hold that?” the cook asked.

“Please,” Aiden said.

The cook took it by the collar and hung it on a hook away from the stove. “You coming back?”

Aiden looked through the round window. Silas still held the microphone beyond Mercedes’s hand.

“Apparently I do,” Aiden said.

He lifted a tray of shrimp from the service counter. The cook moved the little cup of sauce toward its center before Aiden carried it out.

***

Shawna turned toward the kitchen as Aiden came through the door. Her eyes moved from the tray to the platform. She slid her chair clear of the aisle and stood behind it. Tank had returned to a table below the stage. He lowered his plate with both hands.

A woman near the aisle looked at Aiden, then at the coffin.

“Lord,” she said.

The man beside her crossed himself.

“Don’t do that,” the woman told him. “He got shrimp.”

“Could still be spiritual,” the man said, his hand paused against his chest.

The woman caught the back of his chair. “Nigga, move.”

He scraped it inward. Aiden passed between their table and the next without tipping the tray. The drummer lifted a stick above the snare and held it there as Aiden approached the platform.

The smallest guard stood beside the steps. Aiden stopped in front of him and offered the tray.

“Shrimp?” Aiden asked.

The guard took one with his right hand. The open case hung from his left.

Aiden tipped the tray slightly toward the cup. “Sauce?”

The guard nodded and dipped. A drop landed on the empty case lining. He looked down at it. Aiden went up the two shallow steps while the guard pulled the tail from the shrimp.

Silas turned at the sound of Aiden’s voice. His fur collar struck his mouth. He stared at Aiden, then at the coffin beside them. The microphone gave a small squeal when his hand tightened around it.

Aiden set the tray level between them. “Nigga, it’s my motherfucking funeral.”

Silas jammed the microphone into its stand and reached beneath his coat.

Aiden drove the tray upward under Silas’s wrist as the pistol cleared the fur. The gun fired into the ceiling. Plaster burst above the piano. The pistol flew from Silas’s hand, struck the platform, and skidded under the instrument.

Shrimp scattered over the coffin. One dropped into the trumpet player’s open case. The musician folded over his trumpet and went behind his chair. Guests ducked between tables. Tank caught two people around their shoulders and pulled them down behind one, keeping his glass raised above all three heads until he found a place to set it.

Aiden caught Silas’s coat in his free hand.

“Nigga, everybody else brought flowers!” Aiden shouted.

Silas seized the bottle from the coffin and swung it against Aiden’s shoulder. Glass broke. Aiden’s fingers loosened in the fur, then closed again. Liquid ran down his sleeve; a dark line spread through it from the shoulder.

“Motherfucker,” Aiden said through his teeth.

He struck Silas across the face with the tray. Its rim bent. The drive flew out of Silas’s other hand. Mercedes stepped onto the platform and caught it against her chest, her heels sliding until her back touched the coffin stand.

“Got it!” Mercedes shouted.

The largest guard left the backstage doorway and came along the platform. Porsche followed through the opening with the duplicate case. He reached the top of the steps and planted a foot to turn toward Aiden. Porsche swung the case into that ankle.

His shoe stopped. His body went down the steps.

Shawna lifted her chair and met him at the bottom. The chair legs broke across his face. A tooth struck the edge of a table and fell into a saucer. The guard landed sitting among the splinters, one hand against his mouth.

“Stay,” Shawna told him.

He lowered the hand, looked at the blood in it, and remained on the floor.

In the mirror above the bar, another guard’s pistol rose behind Aiden. Mercedes pushed the recovered drive inside her jacket. She caught the microphone stand in both hands and swung it across the platform.

“Down, Aiden!” Mercedes shouted.

Aiden ducked. The stand passed over him and struck the guard across the chest. The man staggered backward into the drum kit. A cymbal buckled beneath his shoulder. The bass drum rolled into a keyboard player’s shin, and the musician caught it before it went off the platform.

The drummer stood. He picked up the bent cymbal by its edge and held it over the man trying to crawl out of the kit.

“Nigga, that’s vintage,” the drummer said.

He set the cymbal down, took up his stool, and brought the padded seat across the guard’s shoulder and back. The man folded forward with his arms over his head. The drummer raised the stool again and waited. The guard stopped crawling.

Silas pulled free of his coat. Aiden’s hand remained wrapped in fur as Silas ran toward the steps. Tank came up them from the ballroom and caught him around the waist. With his free hand, Tank flung open the coffin lid and tipped Silas inside.

The stand rocked. The lid banged down.

A high squeal came from the coffin.

Tank stopped with both hands against it. “Shit.”

The lid flew up. A little man in a gold tracksuit sat upright beneath Silas. He wore sunglasses like the pair hanging from Aiden’s shirt; one arm had slipped off his ear. Silas lay across his legs.

“NIGGA, I TOLD YOU NINE-FIFTEEN!” the little man shouted at Tank.

Aiden let the fur coat fall. He looked at the man in the coffin, then at Tank.

“You hired a corpse?” Aiden asked.

“Resurrection package,” Tank said. He looked down at his shoes.

Aiden kept looking at him. “Nigga.”

Tank raised a hand toward the coffin. “It included the dance.”

“And fog,” the performer said, pushing Silas’s shoulder out of his way.

Tank pointed at the plaster dust hanging in the stage lights. “Nigga, we got atmosphere.”

The performer followed his finger. “That’s ceiling.”

“Work with the venue,” Tank said.

The little man climbed over Silas’s stomach, stood on the edge of the coffin, and stepped onto the platform. He straightened his tracksuit and set the sunglasses squarely on his nose.

“I do the surprise,” the performer told Tank. “I do the dance. I leave. Ain’t no nigga mentioned wrestling.”

At the far end of the ballroom, Silas’s fourth guard moved toward the entrance. Tank pointed at him over Aiden’s shoulder.

“Sit your ass down,” Tank said. “The nigga hasn’t danced.”

The guard stopped, pulled out a chair at an empty table, and sat with both palms on the cloth.

The smallest guard still stood beside the steps. He held a shrimp tail in his right hand and the case in his left. He lowered the case to the floor without letting either catch strike the wood.

“I’m with catering,” he said.

The cook leaned through the kitchen door. “No the fuck you are not.”

***

Tank checked the time on his phone. Seven minutes later, he checked it again while tightening the last binding around a guard’s wrists. Porsche had put the collected guns behind the sound desk. A waiter crawled beneath the piano with a towel and emerged holding Silas’s pistol by the wrapped grip. Porsche took it and laid it beside the others.

The bartender stood up, ran a hand along the bottles on the back shelf, and switched on the light above the register.

“Bar’s open,” he called.

A man holding a broken chair leg put it down and joined the line. Two guests righted their table. One retrieved a drink from beneath it, held it up to the light, and set it back down without tasting it.

Silas tried to sit upright in the coffin. The performer put a gold sneaker against his forehead and pushed him back into the satin.

“Stop touching my face,” Silas said.

“Stop being in my office, nigga,” the performer told him.

Mercedes went to the sound desk and took the drive from her jacket. The technician handed her a cable. She plugged it in, moved the mouse, and watched folders fill the screen. Porsche stood at her shoulder.

Aiden started toward them. Shawna hooked two fingers through his belt and held him beside her. He looked back at her, then at Mercedes. He stayed where he was. A drop of blood traveled from his sleeve to his fingertips and fell onto the carpet.

Mercedes put on the headphones and selected a file. She closed her eyes. Porsche reached for her hand beneath the edge of the table. Mercedes’s lips moved with no sound, stopped, and moved again. After a while she opened another file, listened, then scrolled through the remaining folders.

She removed the headphones and wiped beneath one eye with her knuckle.

“All here,” Mercedes said.

Tank breathed out. He put both hands on the back of a chair and leaned against it for a moment.

“Then play one, my nigga,” Tank said.

Mercedes looked from the screen to the platform. Broken flowers lay across the steps. A waiter crouched between two bound guards, holding a memorial napkin against each man's face. The giant portrait had been moved inside and leaned crookedly near the entrance, its angel wings rising behind an abandoned tray of shrimp.

She went up the steps. The drummer was tightening a replacement cymbal onto a stand.

“You good?” Mercedes asked him.

He turned the wing nut once more. “You doing something slow?”

“No,” Mercedes said.

The drummer sat down. “Then yeah.”

Mercedes gave the technician a track number. He nodded and put his hand on the controls. She pointed across the room at Aiden’s portrait.

“Turn that nigga around,” Mercedes said. “I need the room.”

Aiden went to it. He caught the frame with his good arm, lifted the lower corner with the other, and stopped with his teeth clenched. Shawna reached him before he tried again. Together they turned the photograph to the wall. She let go only when the plain backing rested securely against it.

A hiss came through the speakers. Then saxophone, low and rough at the start of the note. The live bass player listened with his fingers resting on the strings. On the next bar he joined in. The drummer laid a beat beneath him. Mercedes took the microphone from its bent stand and held it close.

She sang low at first. The people at the nearest tables stopped talking. A waiter paused with a tray against his hip, then lowered it to an empty chair. At the chorus, Mercedes planted one heel on the monitor and lifted her face. Her voice rose above the recorded saxophone. The trumpet player stood and brought his instrument to his mouth.

People began moving between the tables. A woman caught her companion by both wrists and pulled him out of his chair. Two men carried the broken chair pieces to the wall, then returned with drinks. The bar line shortened as guests turned toward the platform.

The performer closed the coffin and climbed onto the lid.

“Get off!” Silas shouted from inside.

The little man checked Tank’s phone, held up beside the platform. “Nine-fifteen, bitch!”

He danced. His gold trouser legs flashed above the flowers, and the coffin stand rolled several inches before Tank planted a shoe against one wheel. The performer kept time without stepping down.

Tank raised his free hand. “That nigga earned his deposit!”

Porsche laughed with her head tilted toward the stage. The cook came out carrying another tray, Aiden’s jacket over his shoulder. A woman took the tray from him and put it on a table, then caught both his hands.

“Nigga, I’m working,” the cook said, looking toward the kitchen.

“Not anymore,” the woman told him. She drew him between the tables as a waiter pushed through the door behind them.

Shawna slipped her fingers into Aiden’s unhurt hand. She touched the blood-dark cloth at his shoulder with her other fingertips. He flinched.

“You’re bleeding,” Shawna said.

Aiden looked down at his sleeve. “Little bit.”

She pressed a clean napkin against it. “We’ll get it looked at.”

“After the song,” Aiden said.

Shawna stayed against his good side, holding the napkin in place. On the last chorus, a second voice came through the speakers alongside Mercedes’s live voice. The recording caught for half a breath. Mercedes continued singing beside it, smiling. Porsche stopped moving and stood with both hands around her glass.

Shawna leaned close to Aiden’s ear. “Eleven sponsors is a lot of mouths to feed.”

He laughed, bent toward his hurt shoulder, and pressed her hand against him more firmly. She eased the napkin back into place without letting go of his other hand.

At the entrance, the ticket man opened the curtains for a new group.

“Sixty dollars,” he told them.

Aiden pointed across the room. “Nigga, it was forty!”

The ticket man indicated Mercedes, the performer on the coffin, and Aiden standing beside Shawna.

“Live show now,” the ticket man said.

***

By the time officers led Silas and all four guards out, the band had put away two instruments and taken them out again while the drummer discussed overtime with Tank. The performer stood beside the empty coffin counting his payment. He took off the sunglasses and gave them to Aiden, who folded them over the pair already hanging from his shirt.

At the buffet, Tank held an empty foil pan while a cook measured the remaining shrimp into it. Each time the spoon stopped, Tank tilted the pan toward the tray. The cook added one more spoonful, covered the food, and pressed the lid down with both hands.

Aiden sat near the sound desk. Shawna had taped a fresh dressing over his shoulder. The discarded napkins lay inside a folded memorial program on the table. She sat on his other side with his jacket across her knees.

The technician approached with two clean glasses. His headphones rested around his neck. He set the glasses down, looked at Shawna’s ring, then at the purple stamp on Aiden’s wrist.

“Can I ask something?” the technician said.

“Nigga, apparently you can charge for it,” Aiden told him.

The technician smiled. “How’d you two meet?”

Shawna laughed before Aiden answered.

“Let me tell it,” she said.

Aiden turned toward her. “Baby, I was there.”

“So was I,” Shawna said. “One of us remembers who saved whom.”

The technician pulled a chair toward their table. Tank came back carrying his pan beneath one arm.

“Somebody get this nigga a plate,” Tank called.

The technician touched the headphones. “I’m on the clock.”

“So am I,” Tank told him. “I organized a death.”

Mercedes brought a plate and a napkin. Porsche moved the bottle to the center of the table. The technician sat and loosened the cord around his headphones so it no longer caught on the chair. Aiden watched him do it.

“How old are you?” Aiden asked.

“Twenty-three,” the technician said.

Tank opened the pan. “Old enough for whatever version these two agree on.”

Shawna took one of the clean glasses. Aiden leaned back carefully, leaving his injured shoulder clear of the chair. Beyond them, somebody swept plaster into a dustpan while the ticket man counted bills at the entrance.

“First night in Vegas,” Aiden said to the technician. “You want the whole thing?”

The young man nodded.

Shawna settled beside Aiden. “Start before you became reasonable.”

“Nigga, we still waiting on that chapter,” Aiden said.

Tank laughed into his shrimp. Mercedes pulled her chair closer. The room grew quieter as the musicians carried their cases out through the curtains.

Aiden turned his glass once against the table and set it down.

“Big T had his hand on my shoulder,” he began. “Bass shaking the back door. Twelve men standing around the cars, stretching like somebody told them there was gonna be exercise.”

Shawna laid her hand beside his on the table. The cross beneath his open collar touched the edge of the glass when he leaned forward.

“That night,” Aiden said, “I thought all the trouble was already standing where I could see it.”

## 6. Forty Million Reasons to Die

The morning after T's funeral, Aiden rode to the Saint with Red's note open on his knee. The sentence had been written in red ink. He smoothed a crease beneath Dante's name, folded the card, and opened it again before Tank reached the next light.

A diamond pin covered a small hole in the front of Aiden's coat. Tank glanced from the pin to his face.

“Nigga, you slept at all?” Tank asked.

Aiden kept the card on his knee. “Nigga, I can dream about firing you with my eyes open.”

Tank reached into the console and handed him the little bottle Vega had left at the house.

“Take what she said to take,” Tank told him.

“Nigga, I heard the doctor,” Aiden said.

Tank returned his hand to the wheel. “Then listen to her.”

Aiden put the bottle in his pocket. A cut crossed Tank's knuckles above the steering wheel. The cuff of his black suit moved over it whenever he turned.

At the Saint, two men approached Aiden's door before the valet could reach it. Tank stopped the Mercedes and remained seated until Aiden opened his side. Aiden stepped out with the note in his hand.

“Your boss sent for me,” Aiden told the nearer man.

The guard looked at the pistol beneath Aiden's open coat and held out his hand.

“Nigga, you can have the note,” Aiden said.

Beyond them, Red stood inside the glass entrance in a cream suit. The guard turned toward him. Red raised one finger. Both men stepped away from the Mercedes, leaving space for Tank to join Aiden.

“Aiden,” Red said when they reached the doors. “Glad you came.”

Aiden held up the note. “You would've been sad if I didn't?”

“I had breakfast made,” Red told him.

“Then feed a nigga before the conversation gets hungry,” Aiden said.

Red put an arm around his shoulders and brought him through the entrance. His hand squeezed Aiden's upper arm. Aiden looked down at it, then back at Red's face without moving away. Tank followed close behind them.

Before they went upstairs, Red led them past a white piano. Flowers stood on its polished lid. Above it hung a photograph of Shawna singing, one hand raised beside a microphone. Red stopped beneath the picture.

Aiden looked up. His hand tightened on the folded note.

“You play?” Red asked.

Aiden turned toward him. “Nigga, is breakfast behind the piano?”

Red rested two fingers on its lid. “I wondered what you brought to a woman who already sings.”

Tank closed his eyes.

“Please,” Tank said.

Aiden looked back at him. “You want a request?”

“I want eggs before the niggas serving them retire,” Tank told him.

Aiden pulled out the bench and sat. Sheet music stood open on the piano, Shawna's name written across its top. He turned the first page, looked at the next, then returned it to the beginning.

“That's a difficult arrangement,” Red said.

Aiden put the note beside the score. “Nigga, we haven't heard your kitchen yet. Everything difficult until somebody does it.”

He set both hands above the keys. His first chord stopped a waiter carrying coffee toward the lift. The waiter held the pot level while Aiden moved into the introduction.

Low notes passed beneath a series of quick figures in his right hand. He took the first page without stopping the bass, laid it flat, and continued. The melody emerged above the chords. Then the left hand changed, striking a heavier rhythm beneath it while the right kept the tune intact.

Tank opened his eyes.

“Oh, this nigga,” Tank said.

Red looked at him. “Something wrong?”

“With him? All morning,” Tank said. He nodded toward the keyboard. “With that? Listen.”

The waiter shifted his weight in time. He stopped when Red looked toward him, but the toe of one shoe continued moving against the tile.

“You just read that?” Red asked Aiden.

Aiden's hands kept moving. “Nigga, you left it open.”

Red glanced at Shawna's photograph. “She never said—”

“Nigga, you want me to play or collect witness statements?” Aiden asked.

The man beside the entrance laughed. Red turned. The man straightened and lowered his face, but Aiden's mouth widened as his fingers carried the melody into its last phrase.

He finished with both hands spread across the keys. The final chord rang into the room after he lifted them away.

One waiter clapped once. He looked at Red, then at Aiden, and clapped again. The others joined him. Tank spread his hands toward the piano.

“You ordered that,” Tank told Red. “Nigga asked him what he brought. He unpacked.”

Red kept his face toward the photograph. “She never mentioned you played.”

“I keep giving her things to find out,” Aiden said.

He collected the note and closed the keyboard cover gently. Red watched his hand leave the white surface.

“Where'd you learn?” Red asked.

“Church,” Aiden said. “Old woman kept making me lift my wrists. Stayed after, played through supper, got fed.”

Tank looked at the covered keyboard. “Nigga, you been hiding a food job?”

Aiden held out a hand toward him. “Help me up and I'll explain it over eggs.”

Tank caught his elbow before he stood. Aiden rose slowly, pressing his other hand against the bench until he was upright. Red moved toward the lift.

“Breakfast,” Tank said.

“Nigga, you hungry?” Aiden asked him.

Tank guided him away from the bench. “I've lived two years since we parked.”

The penthouse had been cleared of the party. No dice lay on the tables; no trophy stood beside the bar. Red took them through to a terrace where six waiters stood around a table set for three. Crystal vases held white roses. The only red rose stood in front of Aiden's place.

Tank picked it up by the stem as Red pulled out a chair.

“Don't eat that,” Aiden told him.

Tank looked at the rose. “I wasn't going to eat it.”

Red laughed, dimples showing beside his mouth. Aiden lowered himself into the chair, stopped, and shifted the cushion away from his injured side. Red watched him settle before taking his own seat.

“T always did know where to put a bullet,” Red said.

Aiden put the note beside his water glass. “Nigga, he didn't know where to keep his own.”

The waiter pouring coffee stopped with the pot tilted. Red lifted his orange juice.

“To our absent friend,” Red said.

Aiden drank water. Tank set the rose down and left his glass untouched.

The food came in covered dishes. Red lifted one lid, offered Tank bacon, and held the plate toward him. Tank took two pieces. He looked at them on his plate, then at Red, before setting down his fork.

“Where's Shawna?” Red asked.

“With people who like her alive,” Aiden said.

Red cut into his breakfast. “I've always liked her alive.”

“You got an expensive way of showing it,” Aiden told him.

Red put his fork down. “I bought her the dress she was wearing when you met her.”

Aiden leaned forward. His hand closed on the edge of the table, and Tank looked toward the movement.

“You want me to send you the motherfucking dress?” Aiden asked.

“I want you to understand how little of this you understand,” Red said.

Aiden held his position. “Nigga, that's the first time you said anything all morning.”

Red smiled. He smoothed the cloth beside his plate with two fingers.

“I gave her a room when Valentino died,” Red said. “I protected her from people who would've cut her life short over what she did to him. Then my partner arranges to take her and three million dollars out of my house.”

He moved one finger toward Aiden's plate.

“My partner dies,” Red continued. “You inherit his business, his car, his necklace, and the woman. That's a very fortunate week.”

“You forgot the bullet,” Aiden said.

Red looked at the hand against the table. “No. I can see you haven't.”

Tank's chair scraped backward. Aiden raised his free hand without turning toward him. Tank stopped with his knees beneath the table.

“You think I planned that shit?” Aiden asked Red.

“I think you're young enough to be honest when it profits you,” Red said.

Aiden lowered his raised hand. “Then here's some honesty. Send another nigga after her, I'll come back without an invitation.”

Red wiped his mouth. Behind him, a waiter moved toward the juice glass. Red lifted a finger without looking back, and the man stopped.

“You can keep T's streets,” Red told Aiden. “That's a generous beginning.”

Aiden leaned back. “I can keep what's already mine? Damn. Put a bow on it.”

“The money comes back,” Red said. “Shawna comes home. We stop putting people in the ground.”

“Which people you want me to bring back?” Aiden asked. “T? Dante? Valentino? You got a favorite?”

Red's smile stopped at Dante's name. Aiden watched his face and moved the folded note into the center of the table.

“You wrote me a note,” Aiden said. “Finish it.”

Red looked toward the waiters. They collected nothing. One by one they went inside and closed the terrace doors behind them. Red waited until the last latch clicked.

“Eight years ago, I bought something extraordinary from a man who didn't appreciate the opportunity he'd been given,” Red said.

“What man?” Aiden asked.

“Marcellus Vale,” Red said. “A jeweler. Violent when disappointed.”

Tank's hand closed around his glass. Red looked at it, then continued speaking to Aiden.

“His masterpiece was called Blue Mercy,” Red said. “Large blue diamond. Depending on who's asking, somewhere around forty million dollars.”

Aiden sat still. Then he leaned forward again, less quickly this time.

“Nigga, forty million for one rock?” he asked.

“One,” Red said.

Aiden spread both hands. “Nigga, for forty million that bitch better call me when I'm lonely.”

“It has made quite a few men lonely,” Red told him.

Aiden put his hands down. “What does Dante got to do with it?”

“Dante met Vale in prison,” Red said. “Came home with some unfortunate ideas. Decided the money in my accounts belonged to a woman he'd never met.”

“Which woman got that nigga reaching into your accounts?” Aiden asked.

Red picked up his glass. “Vale's dead wife.”

Tank's glass remained on the table inside his closed hand.

“Men do peculiar things when they love dead women,” Red continued. “It's why I advise you to avoid the habit.”

Aiden stood. His chair struck the terrace tile behind him. Tank rose beside him, leaving his breakfast untouched except for the two pieces of bacon moved to its edge.

“Nigga, you can tell me what you know about Dante,” Aiden said, “or you can keep talking about my woman until this breakfast gets real fucking expensive.”

Red stayed seated. “Shawna knows the story.”

“No,” Aiden said.

“Ask her,” Red told him.

Aiden put a hand flat on the table. “I fucking did.”

Red pushed an envelope toward that hand. “Then ask her again.”

Aiden opened it. Two invitations lay inside, heavy blue paper edged in silver. Shawna's name appeared before his.

“The diamond will be the centerpiece of my gala in three nights,” Red said. “I thought Shawna might like to see what her husband died chasing.”

Aiden held the invitations by their edges.

“You printed these before the funeral?” he asked.

“I print a lot of invitations,” Red said.

Aiden put them into his coat. “Nigga, print one for yours.”

Tank touched his elbow. Aiden pulled it clear and turned toward the terrace doors. They had almost reached them when Red spoke again.

“Tell her I still have the piano,” Red said.

Aiden turned back. Red had picked up his knife and was cutting into his breakfast.

“The white one,” Red added. “She'll know.”

Aiden crossed the terrace to Red's side of the table. He picked up the vase in front of him, pulled one rose free, and flung the vase over the railing.

It struck the red Aston Martin parked below. Glass and water burst across the car's roof. A woman near the entrance screamed and jumped back against the wall. The valet ran toward the car with both hands raised.

Red came halfway out of his chair.

“That's my fucking car,” Red said.

Aiden snapped the rose stem between his fingers and put the wet end on Red's plate.

“Tell it I still have the vase,” Aiden said.

He left the terrace with Tank behind him. The rose remained across Red's breakfast, water spreading from its stem into the food.

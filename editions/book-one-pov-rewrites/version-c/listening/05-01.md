## 5. Flowers for the Man I Killed

Big T's coffin shone candy blue beneath the chapel lights. Gold handles ran along its sides, and a six-foot photograph stood behind it between two banks of flowers. In the photograph, T smiled with one hand extended toward someone outside the frame. The hand wore the ring that had split Aiden's mouth.

A funeral worker carried an invoice down the empty aisle before the guests arrived. Flowers, cars, musicians, lunch. Each item had been marked paid. Aiden's name stood beside the signature at the bottom.

At the house by the lake, Aiden faced the bedroom mirror with one cuff undone. He tried to pass its small fastener through the hole, missed, and tried again. The sleeve shifted over his watch. Beneath his dark suit, the bandage made a raised line across his side.

Tank stopped in the doorway wearing black.

“Nigga, you ain't gotta go,” Tank told him.

Aiden kept working at the cuff. “Nigga, I killed him.”

“That's why you ain't gotta go,” Tank said.

Aiden lowered his hands. For a moment the loose sleeve hung over his knuckles.

“He had nobody else?” Aiden asked.

“Plenty people,” Tank said.

Aiden turned from the mirror. “People that loved him.”

Tank looked toward the window. A bird passed low across the water beyond it. Aiden followed his look, then faced him again.

“You did,” Aiden said.

Tank's jaw moved. He said nothing.

“Nigga, you can say it,” Aiden told him.

Tank kept his face toward the glass. “He gave me work when I came out.”

“Nigga gave me a car,” Aiden said.

“Ain't the same,” Tank told him.

Aiden nodded once. “I know.”

Tank came over, took the cuff, and passed the fastener through it. Aiden held his wrist still until Tank let it go. Then they went downstairs together.

Shawna reached the last stair as they entered the foyer. Her dress and coat were white. White gloves hung from one hand; Dante's watch shone on the other wrist. She had pinned her dark hair up, leaving her face clear beneath the chandelier.

Tank stopped.

“Nigga, oh, hell no,” he said.

Shawna looked at him. “What?”

Tank pointed toward her coat. “Nigga's funeral, Shawna.”

“I know whose funeral it is,” she told him.

“Nigga, you can't wear that,” Tank said.

Shawna turned toward Aiden. He looked from her coat to the gloves and then back at Tank.

“Nigga, get the car before she decides on a veil,” Aiden told him.

***

Outside the chapel, a man waiting with the pallbearers raised his hand to greet Aiden. Gold cuff links caught the morning light as he straightened his sleeve.

“Rico,” the man said. “We met at the club.”

Aiden took his hand. “I remember.”

Rico held up the cuff. “My daughter sent these. Lives out of state now. Told me I better wear them where people could see.”

Aiden turned the man's wrist enough to catch the design in the light. “Girl got taste.”

Rico smiled. “That's what I told her.”

He stepped aside to let them pass. Shawna's hand slipped into Aiden's. Tank followed them through the chapel doors.

Several people stood as they entered. Others remained seated with their shoulders pressed against the backs of the pews. Near the front, a woman bent over a folded handkerchief and began crying louder. Aiden continued down the aisle without turning toward her.

Behind him, a man spoke close to someone in the back row.

“Shouldn't have come,” the man said.

Aiden stopped long enough that Shawna's arm straightened beside his.

“Which nigga said it?” Aiden asked the room. “Say it loud so I know who to look at.”

No answer came. A woman in the next pew lowered her eyes to her program. Aiden resumed walking and sat with Shawna in the second row. Tank took the place beside them.

At the front, the preacher spoke about generosity. He named families T had helped, bills he had paid, men he had put to work. A man across the aisle wiped his face with his sleeve. Another nodded at each account, his hands folded between his knees.

“He loved his family,” the preacher said.

Shawna let go of Aiden's hand.

He looked at her. She stared at T's photograph, one white glove crushed in her fist. The seam along a finger parted beneath her thumb. Aiden put his hand on her knee. She did not move it or look down.

Tank rose. He stepped into the aisle with a folded sheet of paper in one hand and walked to the microphone. The preacher moved aside. Tank opened the paper, looked at it, and folded it back into his pocket.

“T knew my mother,” Tank said.

The microphone carried the first words too loudly. He moved his mouth farther from it.

“Knew she worried about me,” he continued. “Every time I came by, nigga made me call her first. Didn't matter what we was doing. Call your mother, Tank. Tell her you ate.”

A woman near the aisle laughed through her tears. Tank looked toward the sound, then at the blue coffin beside him.

“She died while I was inside,” Tank said. “T paid for everything. Told me don't owe him for that.”

He rubbed a hand down the front of his suit. The paper in his breast pocket crackled beneath his palm.

“I owed him for it anyway,” Tank said.

The room stayed quiet. Shawna drew a breath that could be heard from the aisle. Aiden's hand remained on her knee.

“He did some shit,” Tank said.

The preacher shifted beside him. Tank kept his face toward the coffin.

“Some shit I can't stand up here and fix for him,” he continued. “I ain't gonna try. But he picked me up when nobody was answering. And I remember that.”

Tank stepped away from the microphone. On his way back to the pew, he looked at Aiden. Aiden nodded. Tank sat with his hands open on his knees until the preacher began again.

***

Red's flowers stood taller than the coffin. White roses crowded a frame wound with blue ribbon. His name had been printed on a card large enough to read from the aisle. The seat near the front reserved in his name remained empty.

Each time the chapel door opened, Aiden turned toward it. An usher brought in a late couple. A funeral worker came to speak with the preacher. A man carried fresh water to the musicians. Aiden faced forward again after each one.

When the guests began going up to the coffin, Shawna stayed in the pew.

“You don't have to,” Aiden told her.

She looked at the front. “I know.”

Then she stood. He got up beside her, using the pew's back to push himself upright. They went forward together.

T lay in a dark suit with his hands arranged against his waist. The ring remained on his finger. Aiden looked at it and touched the healing cut at his own mouth. Shawna took a folded photograph from inside her coat.

She opened it over T's chest. The wedding chapel showed in the background. Dante stood with his arms around her waist; at the edge of this print, T still appeared beside them with one hand on his brother's shoulder.

Shawna laid the picture against the dead man's suit.

“You can take your fucking picture with you,” she whispered.

Her hand stayed above it. Then she bent closer, one glove caught between her fingers.

“He loved you,” Shawna told T. Her voice broke on the word. “You knew he did.”

Aiden turned his face toward the flowers. Behind them, the preacher cleared his throat. Aiden looked back at him, holding the look until the man stepped away from the aisle.

Shawna straightened. Aiden touched T's cuff once with two fingers. He did not take the hand beneath it.

“Nigga, all you had to do was let us go,” Aiden said.

He drew his hand back and took Shawna's. They walked out together while the next guest approached the coffin.

***

Rosa waited beside the cars in black sunglasses. The wind pulled at her coat, and she caught its collar beneath her chin. When an arriving guest asked why she had come, she pointed toward Shawna in the chapel doorway.

“For her,” Rosa said.

She had given the same answer to two other people. Aiden stopped beside her as Shawna came down the last step.

“I ain't crying for that nigga,” Rosa told him.

“Nobody asked you to,” Aiden said.

Rosa looked toward the chapel doors. “I know. I just want the dead to be clear.”

Shawna leaned against her. Rosa opened the coat and wrapped it around both of them.

Mercedes and Porsche stood near the parked Mercedes with a tray of sandwiches. Beyond them waited another white Bentley. A card from Leon rested against its windshield beneath a wiper. Porsche took it out and handed it to Tank.

He read it while taking two sandwiches from the tray.

“Seeing the last one die changed my feelings about ownership,” Tank read aloud. He looked at the new car. “That's love.”

Porsche took the card back. “That's a nigga with insurance.”

The pallbearers brought T down the chapel steps. Rico walked at the front with his gold cuff links showing beneath the handle. The hearse driver opened the rear door and stepped aside while the six men slid the coffin onto its rollers and secured it inside.

At the corner of the chapel, a black SUV moved into view. Another followed it. Dark windows showed the pale shapes of faces leaning toward the glass. Aiden looked past the hearse as the first SUV slowed.

Tank stopped chewing.

“Down,” Tank said.

The first shot broke the tray in Porsche's hands. Sandwiches and pieces of its rim scattered across the pavement. Aiden caught Shawna around the waist and pulled her behind the Mercedes. Rosa dropped beside them as bullets struck the chapel doors.

Rico fell next to the hearse. One arm lay extended from his body, the cuff link catching the light above his open hand. He did not move when a pallbearer crawled toward him and caught his shoulder.

Tank fired over the Mercedes's hood. Porsche dragged Mercedes down behind the rear wheel. Another burst took the mirror off the car, throwing it across the space where they had stood.

“Everybody inside!” Aiden shouted.

An elderly couple remained halfway down the chapel steps. The man pulled at his wife's hand, but she stood facing Rico with her other hand against her mouth. Bullets broke the flowers from a tall wreath behind them.

Aiden pointed toward the steps. “Nigga, get her!”

Tank looked back at him. “You stay here!”

“I heard you the first time!” Aiden shouted.

Tank ran low across the drive. He caught the older man beneath one arm and pushed the woman ahead of him through the chapel doors. An usher reached for her. Tank turned at once toward the entrance again, pistol raised.

The first SUV came farther into the lot. A passenger leaned out with his gun. Aiden fired from beside the Mercedes's wheel. The man jerked back into the SUV. Its driver cut across the drive, clipped the fountain, and struck the empty wheelchair waiting at the bottom of the chapel steps.

The chair folded beneath its front corner. A wheel rolled free and struck a flowerpot.

Aiden stared at it. “That nigga just hit my chair.”

Shawna fired twice toward the second SUV and drew back behind the car.

“You weren't using it,” she said.

“Still mine,” Aiden told her.

Across the drive, the hearse began moving. Its engine was running; the driver's door remained open. The driver crouched behind it with one hand pressed against his upper arm as the car rolled away down the slope. T's coffin gleamed through the rear glass.

The first SUV turned behind it. One of the men opened a door and jumped out, running toward the hearse's empty driver's seat.

Rosa lifted her face above the Mercedes's fender.

“They're taking him,” Rosa said.

Aiden turned toward her. “Taking who?”

“T!” Rosa told him.

Shawna caught Aiden's jacket as he began to rise.

“Aiden,” she said.

He looked at the man running toward the hearse. “They don't get him.”

“Baby, he's dead,” Shawna told him.

“Then he ain't driving himself home,” Aiden said.

He kissed her once. “Stay with the car,” Aiden told her. Then he pulled his jacket free and went across the drive. His steps shortened as he reached the slope. One hand pressed against his ribs; the other kept his pistol down beside his leg.

Someone at the chapel doors shouted his name.

“Look at that nigga!” another man called from behind a car.

Aiden reached the hearse as the gunman caught its steering wheel. He shoved his own pistol into his waistband, seized the man's jacket, and drove him against the open door. The man's gun fired into the air. Their weight pushed the door against its stop as the hearse kept rolling.

The man's elbow struck Aiden across the throat. Aiden lost one hand from the jacket, caught the doorframe, and drove his forehead into the man's nose. Blood struck the edge of the glass. The man staggered backward, and Aiden shoved him clear of the moving car.

Aiden fell into the driver's seat. He pulled the wheel around as the hearse hit the curb with both front tires. The coffin thumped against its restraints behind him.

“You can shut up too,” Aiden told the coffin.

The first SUV accelerated after him. Aiden took the hearse across the road and through the open cemetery gate. Gravel burst beneath the tires as he entered the broad center drive. White stones passed on either side, then rows of trees and narrow paths branching away from the road.

Behind him, the SUV's men fired through their own windshield. The hearse's rear glass burst. Glass scattered along the coffin and over the back of the front seat. One gold handle showed beneath a fresh scratch in the blue finish.

Aiden glanced into the mirror.

“Nigga, they're scratching your box,” he said to T.

At the center of the cemetery, the drive curved around a wide stone circle. Aiden turned the hearse hard around it. The car leaned; the coffin stayed held in its restraints. The SUV entered the curve too quickly, sliding outward across the gravel before its driver brought it back onto the drive.

At the second gate, a groundskeeper stood beside a watering truck with a hose in his hand. Aiden raised two fingers as the hearse passed. The man dropped the hose and lifted two fingers back. Water spread over his shoes while he watched the SUV follow.

The hearse came out onto a service road. Aiden straightened the wheel. In the mirror, the SUV showed a bent bumper and one broken headlight.

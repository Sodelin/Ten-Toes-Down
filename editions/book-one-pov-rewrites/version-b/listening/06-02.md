Downstairs, the valet stood beside a red Aston Martin with a towel in his hands. The vase had gone through its roof. Roses and glass lay inside the cabin. Aiden couldn't see a promising use for the towel.

“Mr. Red's going to be upset,” the valet told him.

“Got a head start upstairs,” Aiden said. “Where's mine?”

Tank went toward the Mercedes. Its glass had been replaced, its rear repaired, the body polished until Aiden could see himself approaching. He liked the car a little more every time it survived him.

A man carrying a helmet came out of the next hotel. He looked at the ruined Aston, then at Aiden.

“You Aiden?” the man asked.

“Depends who's hoping,” Aiden said.

The man grinned. “You flew over a boat.”

“Word's getting around,” Aiden told him.

The man had a scar down his chin and a leather jacket with a silver eagle across its back. He pointed toward a black motorcycle.

“Saint,” he said. “Little Saint. I ride.”

“Good for you,” Aiden said.

Saint shook his head, still smiling. “I mean I ride. Thought you ought to know.”

Tank opened the Mercedes door.

“Everybody's got something this man ought to know,” Tank said.

Saint looked beyond them. His smile disappeared.

“Know those two cars aren't valets,” he said.

A gray sports car had stopped across the exit. Behind it, a black SUV rolled forward with its windows lowering. The passenger held a pistol against his chest. Aiden couldn't see anything in the man's clothes or face that told him whether this was about Red, T, or a sum of money.

“Inside,” Tank told Aiden.

The first bullet struck the Mercedes door while Tank's hand was still on it.

Aiden grabbed the valet by his collar and threw him behind the car. Glass burst from the hotel's revolving door. People dropped behind luggage. Saint ran toward his motorcycle, pulling his helmet on as he went.

Tank fired from beside Aiden's shoulder. Aiden got into the passenger seat. Tank went around the hood, slid behind the wheel, and put the car in reverse before his door was fully closed.

The Mercedes hit the gray car and moved it sideways. The other driver shouted through his window. Tank hit him again. The gap opened, and they scraped through with sparks beneath Aiden's door.

“Somebody really hates breakfast,” Aiden said.

“Head down,” Tank ordered.

“I need it where I can see,” Aiden told him.

“See lower,” Tank said.

The gray car followed with a fender hanging against its wheel. The SUV came behind it, its passenger firing through the open window. A shot broke the Mercedes's new rear glass. Aiden hunched against the dashboard as pieces struck the seat behind him.

Tank took the first corner too fast. A taxi braked in their path. He missed its bumper by inches and turned into a narrow street lined with delivery trucks. The gray car followed. Behind it, the SUV struck a streetlight and kept coming, dragging something metal beneath it.

Little Saint drew alongside on his motorcycle. Through his helmet visor, Aiden saw him grinning. Saint pointed ahead.

A tour bus stood across the next intersection. Its driver argued with a man in a yellow vest. Neither was looking toward the approaching cars.

“Tank!” Aiden said.

“I see it!” Tank answered.

“Keep hearing that before we hit something,” Aiden told him.

Tank braked. The Mercedes slid. The gray car couldn't stop and hit their rear quarter, spinning them halfway around.

Now Aiden looked directly at its driver. He pulled his pistol from his waistband, raised it toward the broken rear window, and fired. The man's windshield whitened. He ducked, lost the turn, and drove into the tour bus's empty luggage compartment.

The bus driver abandoned his argument and ran.

Tank straightened the Mercedes and pushed into the gap beside the bus. Their door scraped along it. Aiden watched a pink suitcase shake behind a little window each time the car struck another panel.

Then they came onto open road. Saint was still beside them. He raised a fist. Aiden lifted his own.

The SUV hit the motorcycle's rear wheel.

The bike snapped sideways. Saint flew across the hood of a parked car and rolled into the road. His helmet struck the pavement.

Tank swore and braked. Aiden had his door open before they stopped.

“Where the fuck—” Tank began.

“Get him!” Aiden shouted back.

He ran across the road. The SUV turned toward him. Its driver was smiling behind the windshield. Aiden could see the wheels change angle, lining up with him and the man struggling on the asphalt.

Saint had got himself onto one elbow. His helmet shell was cracked, blood running down his cheek beneath it.

“Leg works?” Aiden asked, catching his arm.

“Works,” Saint said.

“Work it with me,” Aiden told him.

He pulled Saint up. They stumbled toward the parked trucks while the SUV accelerated at their backs.

Tank drove the Mercedes across its path.

The crash filled the street. Aiden pushed Saint behind a truck and turned with his pistol raised. The SUV had stopped against the Mercedes. Its driver tried to open his door. Tank got out, slammed it back on him, then reached through the open window and hauled him out that way instead.

Aiden aimed at the passenger. The man dropped his pistol and put his hands on the dashboard.

“You can keep breathing,” Aiden told him. “That's all you're getting today.”

Saint stood behind him, jacket torn at one shoulder. He looked from Tank to Aiden and the smoking cars.

“You came back,” Saint said.

“You said you ride,” Aiden told him. “Haven't seen enough yet.”

Saint's grin returned slowly.

“Any road,” he said. “Any fucking road, Aiden. You call, I'm there.”

A crowd had formed at the corner. Someone called Aiden's name. A woman raised her phone and asked him to look toward her. He turned his unbloodied side toward it. If everyone insisted on recording him, they could at least be useful about the angle.

Knox's officers arrived while smoke still rose from the engines. One looked over the wrecked SUV and shook his head.

“Chief said you were something,” the officer told Aiden.

“Chief pays attention,” Aiden said.

Tank called for another car to collect Saint. Aiden waited beside the motorcycle rider until it came, then returned to the Mercedes. The engine still ran. Tank tied one damaged door shut with a belt.

They drove away with people watching from both sides of the street.

Before the highway, Aiden called Shawna.

“Baby, you want lunch?” he asked.

“Why are there sirens behind you?” she demanded.

“People wanted breakfast,” Aiden said. “I'm thinking ahead.”

***

At the house, Shawna stood barefoot on a little platform while a woman pinned a silver dress around her hips. Aiden stopped in the doorway. For a moment, he wanted everybody else out so he could look at her without an audience.

Then the watch slid down her wrist.

Dante's watch. The song. Now a diamond worth forty million and a prison story nobody had told him. Aiden had bought the water and bled getting her here, but it felt as though the dead man kept arriving ahead of him.

Rosa looked up from a chair.

“Why you got that face?” she asked.

“What face?” Aiden said.

“The one that's about to make the dress cost more,” Rosa told him.

Aiden held up the invitations.

“Blue Mercy,” he said to Shawna.

She stopped moving. The dressmaker kept pinning until Rosa put a hand on her shoulder.

“Let's get coffee,” Rosa told the woman.

“I don't drink coffee,” the dressmaker said.

“Good day to start,” Rosa answered, helping her up.

They left. Shawna stepped down from the platform. A pin fell between her and Aiden, clicking against the floor.

“What did he tell you?” she asked.

Aiden laughed once. It sounded ugly in the quiet room.

“Everybody asks what somebody else told me,” he said. “You knew about forty million dollars?”

Shawna drew back a little. “I knew about the stone.”

He saw the movement, disliked causing it, and kept going.

“Didn't seem worth mentioning?” he asked.

“You were shot,” Shawna said. “Then people shot at us yesterday. I was thinking about getting you through it.”

Aiden gestured toward the platform. “Time to choose a dress, though.”

Her face changed.

“I wanted one night where you looked at me without seeing somebody bleed,” she said.

He lowered his hand. He hadn't prepared an answer for that.

Shawna took the invitation from him. Her thumb moved over Red's name.

“He mentioned the piano?” she asked.

“Hard to miss it,” Aiden said. “White one. Apparently I'm meant to know what that means.”

“I broke the keys with a bottle the night I left,” Shawna told him. “He bought another so he could pretend I was coming back.”

Aiden looked toward the windows, then back at her.

“Said you knew what Dante died for,” he told her.

“I know what Dante told me,” Shawna said.

“Tell me, then,” Aiden asked.

She watched his face. “It starts before me.”

“Everything in this house starts before me,” he said.

Her gaze dropped to the cross at his collar.

“Want me to take that back?” Shawna asked.

Aiden's hand closed around it. “No.”

“Sit down,” she told him.

He sat in the chair Rosa had left. Shawna sat beside him with the pinned silver cloth gathered carefully over her knees.

“Dante told me after we married,” she said. “In the room Red paid for. I thought we'd go downstairs and drink with everybody, but he locked the door.”

Aiden felt his jaw tighten. He made himself leave it there without speaking.

“He said I ought to know something before deciding what kind of man I'd married,” Shawna continued.

She took his hand away from the cross and held it between both of hers.

“Then he told me about the old man,” she said.

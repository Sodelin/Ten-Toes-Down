## 13. Everybody Bring Your Heart

At nine forty, the Mercedes stopped outside the lake house.

Aiden got out with Dante's folder under one arm and the photograph against his chest. The blue box stayed on the seat. He walked up the path, saw the lights shining through the glass front door, and struck the door with his fist.

The pane broke inward. Pain opened across his knuckles. He drew his hand back to hit the remaining glass, and Tank caught his wrist.

“Aiden,” Tank said.

Aiden swung at him with the other hand. The folder fell. Tank's head turned with the blow, but his grip stayed closed.

“Let go,” Aiden ordered.

“No,” Tank said.

“Had the money this whole time.”

“Yeah,” Tank answered.

Aiden struck him again. His side pulled so sharply that his knees bent beneath him. He tried to drag Tank toward the broken glass. Tank turned, put his body between Aiden and the door, and held him against the wall instead.

Tank was too heavy to move. Aiden hated knowing it, hated that Tank could feel how little strength the last blow had left him.

“Let me walk in there thinking I knew what was mine,” Aiden said. “Let him laugh at me.”

Tank looked past him into the house.

“Heard him,” Tank said. “Was standing right there.”

Aiden shoved once more. Tank released his wrist when his fist stopped trying to reach the glass.

Inside, the front room still wore the remains of a party. Champagne chilled in silver buckets. A red heel lay beneath the piano. On a side table, somebody had tipped a little white pile onto a photograph of Aiden and Shawna, choosing her face as the available surface.

He swept the table clear.

Glass and silver hit the floor. Four men rose from the couches before he spoke.

“Everybody out,” Aiden told them.

One man had a woman's handbag over his shoulder. He looked down at it, turned toward the hall to find its owner, then saw Aiden's face and decided the bag could notify her itself.

They left through the broken door. Tank picked up Dante's folder from the path, carried it inside, and pushed the damaged door as far shut as it would go.

“Me too?” Tank asked.

Aiden looked at his torn jacket, his bleeding mouth, the place a cuff link had come loose. His hands carried blood from people Aiden had watched him lift. Nothing on him gave a simple answer anymore.

“Get the money,” Aiden said.

Tank went upstairs.

Aiden sat on the piano bench because it was closest and his legs had started shaking. That afternoon, Shawna had sat here and missed a note. She'd blamed the piano. He had offered to buy another.

“Every time I play something wrong?” she'd asked him, laughing over her shoulder.

He had told her yes. Meant it too. The house would have been a fucking orchestra by Christmas.

Her perfume remained near the bench. He put his head down and remembered her dancing with Red. They had planned it together. He'd said he could take what she needed to do. He'd discovered his actual capacity in front of several hundred witnesses.

A heavy thump came from upstairs.

“Don't drop her money,” Aiden shouted.

“Got it,” Tank called back.

“You been saying that,” Aiden said.

Tank came down with two bags. No silver case. The case had stayed broken at the motel while the money came here, past the door Aiden had believed belonged to the three of them.

Tank opened both bags on the dining table. Cash filled the space between the candles Shawna had bought because, according to her, the house smelled like men lying to each other.

Aiden looked at the candles. She had bought them before she knew.

“All of it,” Tank told him.

“Counted?” Aiden asked.

“More than once.”

“Feel good each time?” Aiden asked.

Tank shook his head.

Aiden took a bundle and threw it at his chest. The band broke. Bills spread across the floor.

“Your tip,” Aiden said.

Tank watched the money settle.

“Look at me,” Aiden demanded. “What did you think was going to happen?”

Tank looked up.

“Thought you were going to die,” he said.

The refrigerator hummed in the next room. Aiden hadn't heard it until then.

Tank rubbed both palms over his head, leaving a streak of blood near his temple.

“That morning,” Tank said. “Before she drove. You kept talking about water and a house. Room for her to sing. She kept telling you to look at her. Half the time you weren't answering what she asked.”

Aiden could remember her voice in the car. The words themselves had broken apart. He didn't want Tank arranging them for him.

“So you went shopping,” Aiden said.

“Went back,” Tank answered. His voice rose, then steadied. “T was there. Money was there. Ten years I stood behind that man. Every room. Every car. Somebody said his name wrong, I was who got up.”

“Loved him,” Aiden said.

“Yeah.” Tank kept his eyes on him.

“Say it again,” Aiden told him.

“Loved him,” Tank repeated. “Want a lie about that too?”

Blood had reached his chin. He let it stay.

“You knew him one night,” Tank continued. “I knew him ten years. Know what he was. Give you something with one hand, keep the other round your neck. Know all of it. Still got to that pool and wanted him to stand up.”

Aiden looked down at the bills around Tank's shoes.

“When he didn't?” Aiden asked.

Tank swallowed. “Took what was left.”

Aiden's anger stayed. But for the first time he could see a man inside the explanation, standing in a place that had emptied around him.

“Red called after,” Aiden said.

Tank nodded. “Said he knew. Said the money bought him whatever he asked.”

“Believed that?” Aiden asked.

“For a while,” Tank said.

“Until when?”

Tank looked at Aiden's chest. “Until he put his hand on her and you stopped breathing.”

Aiden felt the room tilt again.

“Don't tell me what I looked like,” he said.

“Like you did in that car,” Tank answered.

Aiden picked up one of the candles and hurled it into the empty fireplace. Glass burst against brick. The small flame went out.

“Not dying in another fucking car,” Aiden said.

Tank stayed beside the table.

“Then let me drive,” he told Aiden. “Let me get one thing right.”

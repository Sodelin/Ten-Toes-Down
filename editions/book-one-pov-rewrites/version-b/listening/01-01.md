## 1. The Funeral Had a Two-Drink Minimum

“Nigga, it's my motherfucking funeral,” Aiden told the man at the door.

The man kept his hand out. Behind that hand stood a cardboard sign announcing FORTY DOLLARS, a second sign announcing a two-drink minimum, and a portrait of Aiden in a gold frame taller than both of them. An electric candle shivered beside a bowl of breath mints. The candle's plastic flame had been bent toward the money.

The ticket man looked from Aiden to the portrait.

“You look better in the picture,” he said.

“I'm alive,” Aiden reminded him.

“Then you can pay.” The man gave his open palm a little lift.

Aiden leaned around him for a better look inside. Every time the door swung open, butter and perfume came out together in a breath of warm air. A bass player kept trying the same three notes. Somebody was laughing loud enough to offend a real dead person. Aiden had scarcely got home before his own people found a way to charge him for existing.

He inspected the portrait again. They had used his arrest photograph. The cuffs were gone, replaced by angel wings behind his shoulders, but nobody had removed the officer's hand from his arm. Apparently heaven required a police escort. Underneath his expression of profound legal concern, silver letters instructed him to REST EASY.

He called Tank.

“Why is there a cover charge at my funeral?” Aiden asked when his friend answered.

“Shrimp was expensive,” Tank said through a mouthful of something.

Aiden watched a waiter pass the open door. “Why do I have shrimp?”

“You died successful,” Tank told him.

There was chewing in the phone. There was also chewing inside the building, in the same rhythm. The big motherfucker couldn't have been thirty feet away.

“Are you eating right now?” Aiden asked.

“Grieving,” Tank said.

Aiden ended the call and paid two twenties. The ticket man stamped the inside of his wrist: DECEASED, in purple letters. The ink felt cold. One edge of the last D smeared across his skin.

“One well drink,” the man said.

Aiden pointed at the sign. “That says two.”

“Second one's your responsibility,” the man told him, already reaching for the next person's money.

A guest in a black shirt had been watching. He lowered his program and held up his phone.

“Ain't that the deceased?” the guest asked the ticket man.

The ticket man checked Aiden's wrist. “Paid deceased.”

The guest wanted a picture. Aiden asked whether he meant a picture with him alive, and the man nodded eagerly. It would look like they knew each other personally.

“You're meeting me personally, nigga,” Aiden pointed out.

“Yeah, but a funeral picture hit different,” the guest explained, putting an arm around his shoulder.

Aiden held the memorial program while the man aimed the phone at their faces. He considered charging for the photograph. He'd been inside Tank's business model less than a minute and was already finding room for expansion.

***

The room still had a wedding beneath its funeral. A brass arch above the stage had been draped in black, but white petals poked through the folds. Pale rectangles marked the carpet where different tables usually stood. The chandeliers hadn't changed sides at all; they were shining on whatever paid.

Aiden stopped beside a chocolate fountain and took in the room. Tables crowded both sides of a central aisle. On the left, waiters pushed through a kitchen door with a round window; on the right, mirrors multiplied the candles and everybody looking at them. The low stage faced the entrance. Three shallow steps led to it. His coffin stood on the platform immediately in front of the band, its polished lid close enough to the steps for a speaker to rest a hand on it. The drummer had arranged himself behind the box like death was opening for him.

Shawna found Aiden trying to read the program without sticking it to the wet stamp on his wrist.

“Why am I survived by eleven children?” he asked her.

His wife leaned close to inspect the list. “Those are sponsors.”

Aiden put a finger beneath one name. “This one's Jeremiah.”

“Jeremiah's Collision Repair,” Shawna said.

She was wearing a black dress split along one leg, a little hat tilted over one eye, and a diamond at her throat. Aiden slipped his hand around her waist. The cloth felt cool; the skin beneath it did not. She had dressed like an expensive widow with no intention of reducing her standard of living.

“You enjoying widowhood?” he asked.

“The parking is excellent,” Shawna said.

He pulled her closer. She put two fingers against his mouth and looked past his shoulder toward the entrance.

“Eight minutes,” she told him. “Kitchen. Stay there until we get the case.”

Mercedes stood beside the stage steps in a red suit. Aiden could see her thumbnail pressing into her opposite palm. She stopped doing it, smoothed the hand against her thigh, then began again.

The hard drive in that case held the original recordings of her first album. Silas Gold had stolen it. He was offering people the chance to buy what she had made before any of them thought she was worth buying.

Aiden had heard her explain why another recording wouldn't do. The studio was gone. The musicians were older. A voice could sing the same words without getting back to the same night. On one chorus, her younger voice broke; she had refused to smooth it away. It sounded like her, she'd said. Silas had put a price on the break along with everything else.

Getting him outside his bulletproof rooms had required a funeral. Shawna had promised a private estate auction after the memorial: Aiden's watch, cars, collection. Silas could bring Mercedes's recordings and settle the deal while shopping through a dead man's life. He would get the microphone, the widow's attention, the sight of the coffin. Apparently that was enough to make him leave the glass.

Mercedes would inspect the drive. Porsche would switch the case backstage. Aiden's contribution involved a door and eight minutes of leaving it shut.

“I could take it when he gets here,” he told Shawna.

Her fingers left his mouth and closed around his wrist. “And his men could shoot into all these people who paid to eat shrimp.”

Aiden looked down the aisle. An older woman was cutting food for the man beside her. A waiter balanced a tray over three guests trying to fit another chair into a space already losing the argument with two.

“Apparently excellent shrimp,” he said.

“Aiden.” Shawna held his eyes until he stopped smiling.

Mercedes came over and straightened his collar. The little crescent in her palm was white against her skin. Her hands did not shake on his shirt.

“Don't do the thing,” she said.

“Which thing?” Aiden asked.

Mercedes looked around the memorial. “The thing where this becomes about you.”

He nodded toward his portrait by the entrance. “My photograph is six feet tall.”

“Exactly,” Mercedes said. “The nigga has enough publicity.”

Aiden's answer caught behind his teeth. He'd been preparing something about getting her music back, something large and reassuring. She was standing close enough for him to see what her thumbnail had done to her hand. A large promise wasn't the hard part. Standing still while somebody else got what she came for might be.

“You'll get it,” he said.

Mercedes squeezed his shoulder. “I know. I want to enjoy getting it.”

She returned to the stage. Shawna kissed Aiden gently, then turned him by the shoulders.

“Kitchen,” she said.

“Y'all got one word for me tonight,” Aiden complained as her palm gave his back a final push.

He went through the swinging door into steam.

***

The cook stood over a tray of potatoes with a spoon broad enough to dig a grave. Sweat had soaked the collar of his shirt. A fan rattled above him, swallowing the top of the music whenever the door fell shut. Through a speaker over the service shelf, the microphone squealed.

Aiden put his face to the door's round window. Tank was onstage with a plate in one hand and the microphone in the other.

“We gathered here because my brother would want these niggas supporting the bar,” Tank announced.

Glasses went up. Aiden watched a man take the instruction seriously enough to empty his. Tank waited, cleared his throat, and began describing Aiden's generosity. The speech reached the part about giving a brother the shirt off his back.

Aiden leaned into the service opening before he could stop himself.

“Police was looking for a man in a red shirt!” he called.

Laughter broke across the tables. Aiden started pushing the door open with his shoulder. Shawna, seated by the aisle with her back to him, lifted one finger.

He eased back into the kitchen. His hip struck the potato tray. The cook saved it with the spoon and gave him a look that suggested he would personally make this funeral accurate.

“But my nigga came back,” Tank said into the microphone.

Aiden's hand stopped on the door. Tank's voice had changed. He was telling them about the station now: Aiden coming to get him, arguing that he hadn't done anything, paying what needed paying, sitting six hours on a hard bench. Taking him to breakfast afterward.

Through the glass, Aiden could see Tank looking down into his plate. No chairs moved. A woman had a fork halfway to her mouth and seemed to have forgotten why.

Tank sniffed once into the microphone.

“And the nigga asked for his shirt.”

The drummer rolled the laughter out with two quick strokes. Aiden shook his head, smiling into the glass. That was a fucking eulogy.

A noise near the entrance made him look up.

Silas Gold had arrived in white fur. Four men spread around him. The smallest carried the silver case close to his leg. The biggest turned sideways to get past a chair; his neck was wider than Aiden's thigh. Silas raised a bottle toward the stage.

“Miss the good part?” he asked.

Tank glanced at the coffin. “Depends how this speech goes.”

Silas laughed. His guards joined in a little late. Their shoes tracked through a spill of ice as they followed him along the aisle.

Shawna got up to meet him. He bent over the hand she offered. She turned her fingers just before his mouth reached them, and he kissed his own thumb.

Aiden pressed his lips together behind the glass. Beautiful. So little movement, so much accomplished.

“The recordings,” Shawna said to Silas.

“After my tribute,” he told her.

She left her hand at her side. “Mercedes inspects them first. We agreed.”

Silas opened an arm toward the tables. “Can't buy timing, darling.”

He took the microphone from Tank and set his bottle on Aiden's coffin. Tank picked it up and handed it back. Silas set it down again. Tank returned it again. The drummer tapped the rim of his snare once, then again, supplying a clock for the exchange.

“You got somewhere to be?” Silas asked Tank.

“Eventually, nigga,” Tank said.

Silas turned to the guests and began discussing his differences with Aiden. In the kitchen, Aiden silently supplied the difference about his stolen boat. Silas preferred to speak about appetite and unfinished business. He looked toward Shawna when he mentioned people left unprovided for.

“I would never presume—” Silas began.

“Then don't,” Shawna said from beside the steps.

A woman slapped her table hard enough to jump a spoon.

“That nigga got instructions!” she cried.

Silas stood through the laughter with his teeth showing. Aiden watched him rub one thumb along the microphone. Then Silas crooked a finger at Mercedes.

Mercedes stayed where she was.

The smallest guard carried the case up the steps and held it open. Silas finally put his bottle on the coffin without Tank returning it. He lifted the drive from the case, displayed it to Mercedes, and waved the guard away. The man went back down to the foot of the steps with the empty case open in his left hand.

“A tribute,” Silas said. “Our little lady can sing. And apologize for some things she's said about me.”

Mercedes moved to the bottom step. “You promised an inspection.”

“Sing first,” Silas told her.

Behind him, the biggest guard filled the backstage doorway. Aiden could see Porsche beyond the man's shoulder with the duplicate case. The guard shifted just enough to close the remaining gap.

Silas offered Mercedes the microphone. When she reached for it, he pulled it away.

“Ask nicely,” he said.

Aiden took off his jacket.

The cook's spoon stopped above the potatoes. “Want me to hold that?”

“Please,” Aiden said, handing it over.

The cook hung the jacket away from the stove. “You coming back?”

Aiden looked through the window at Silas standing beside his coffin.

“Apparently I do,” he said.

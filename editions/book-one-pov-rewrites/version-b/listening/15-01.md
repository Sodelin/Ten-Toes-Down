## 15. Ten Toes, Thirty Engines

By eleven, headlights filled the street outside the lake house.

Aiden stood just inside the broken door and watched the men come up the path. Leather jackets. Silk shirts. Coats borrowed in a hurry from people with different shoulders. One man had shaving foam behind his ear. Another wore pajamas patterned with little white moons, a gold chain caught between the buttons.

Tank looked down at the pajamas.

“Asleep?” Tank asked.

“Participating in my relationship,” the man replied.

“Relationship's open at the bottom,” Tank told him.

The man buttoned his shirt without stopping.

“Where she at?” he asked Aiden.

“Red has her,” Aiden said.

The man's hands stopped on the buttons.

“Aight,” he said. “What we need?”

Aiden moved aside to let him in.

He couldn't know why every man had come. A few had worked for T long enough to prefer another person standing in Aiden's place. Some might have answered Red's call just as quickly. There were men coming through the door who couldn't be trusted with an unlocked refrigerator.

But they had come.

The man with shaving foam stopped over the broken glass.

“Red do that?” he asked.

“I did,” Aiden said.

The man nodded. “About to get separately mad.”

“Stay with the original mad,” Aiden told him. “Deadline.”

The man wiped his ear on his sleeve and went inside. Aiden watched someone he'd once fought move a chair so Marcellus could sit. Another man picked glass out of Rosa's path before her shoe reached it.

Better men could arrive in the morning and explain their schedules. These were here.

Tank waited beside the dining table until the room had filled. The bags stood open in front of him.

“Money from the motel,” Tank told the gathering. “I took it.”

Conversations stopped. A man beside the piano leaned forward.

“You did what?” the man asked.

Tank kept his eyes on the room.

“All of it,” Tank said. “Let Aiden believe it was gone. Red found out. Tried to use it against him tonight.”

“Tried?” a man near the windows asked.

“Tried,” Tank repeated.

The man by the piano looked toward Aiden.

“Still breathing?” he asked, pointing at Tank.

Aiden stepped between them.

“Look at me when you say that,” Aiden told him.

The man lowered his hand.

“Tank stands with me,” Aiden said.

“Just saying—” the man began.

“Say he stole,” Aiden interrupted. “Did. Say he lied. Did that too. Say you're touching him tonight, find out how fast I forget your name.”

The room went still. Behind him, Tank didn't speak.

The man by the piano lifted both hands.

“Your house,” he said.

“Yeah,” Aiden answered. “My house. Money on my table. My girl with Red. Any of that bother you, door's already broke. Won't hurt its feelings leaving.”

Nobody left.

Rosa stood beside the stairs in the borrowed coat, studying faces. Marcellus sat behind her. The papers were inside his jacket; the closed blue box rested on his knees. He looked tired enough that Aiden wanted to offer him a bed, but the old man caught his eye and shook his head before he could.

Aiden put his good hand on the money.

“Some of you owe me,” he told the room. “Some think Red owes you. Some heard there'd be money.”

“All three,” a man at the back said.

Aiden looked at him. The man stood by his answer.

“Good,” Aiden said. “Knows why he came.”

He began putting stacks into hands.

He didn't throw them. Each man had to come to the table, take the weight, and look at him. The woman they were going to bring home had carried this money out before any of these people arrived to speak about courage. He wanted them to remember whose absence had opened the bags.

Cash went into coats and trouser pockets. The man in pajamas packed bundles inside his shirt until the little moons strained against their buttons.

“Get a bag,” Tank told him.

“Good,” the man said.

“Look pregnant,” Tank replied.

The man held the shirt closed. “Good.”

Aiden kept paying. Some men counted at once. Some laughed. One only held the stack between his palms. Aiden recognized him from a hospital corridor, sitting with his head in his hands. Tonight he kissed the money before putting it away.

Aiden didn't ask what waited at home for it. He needed a woman brought back to his own house. There could be room for more than one desperate thing on the table.

He and Tank had separated the bundles by their bands. When two million lay distributed or set aside for the people in the room, Aiden stopped. The last million remained together inside one bag.

“Hers,” Aiden told them, pulling the zipper shut.

No one argued.

Tank hadn't taken his allotted share. Aiden picked it up from the money already set aside and held it toward him.

Tank shook his head. “No.”

“Take it before I love you louder,” Aiden told him.

“Aiden,” Tank said.

“Poor and guilty?” Aiden asked. “Giving you one bad feeling. Pick.”

Tank glanced at the watching men.

“Don't want it,” Tank said.

“Spend it on somebody who does,” Aiden answered.

He pressed it against Tank's chest until the big man took it. Tank looked down, then placed the money beside the closed bag.

“For her,” Tank said.

Aiden left it there. He wasn't going to count the gesture back out while everybody watched.

Rosa stepped away from the staircase.

“Before you start singing about brotherhood,” Rosa told the room, “she's my friend.”

Several men straightened. The one holding his pajama shirt stopped smiling.

“See her, bring her,” Rosa said. “Don't stop to tell Red how hard you are. Don't explain a grudge from 2018. Bring her.”

The man who'd arrived with shaving foam nodded.

“Got you, Rosa,” he said.

“Better have her,” Rosa answered.

Aiden pushed Rosa's share across the table. It came from the two million already separated, not the bag he had closed.

She looked down.

“What is that?” Rosa asked him.

“Yours,” Aiden said.

“For what?”

“Whatever you want,” he told her.

Her face tightened. “Paying me to stay home?”

“Because she loves you,” Aiden said.

Rosa looked up sharply.

“Don't do that right now,” she told him.

“When she gets out that car,” Aiden said, “want somebody here she knew before every room had me in it.”

Rosa closed one hand around the borrowed coat. He could see how tired she was now. One earring gone. Dress marked by the gala. Her face held still with visible effort.

“Bring her,” Rosa said.

Aiden nodded.

She slapped his cheek. The sound stopped a whisper at the back of the room.

“With your mouth, Aiden,” Rosa told him.

He swallowed.

“Bringing her,” Aiden said.

Rosa kissed the same cheek.

“Go, baby,” she told him.

Aiden turned toward the men.

“Anybody think Red owns Vegas?” he asked.

“Fuck Red,” someone near the door answered.

“Anybody think he owns my wife?” Aiden asked.

Rosa made a sound behind him. He turned halfway back.

“What?” Aiden asked her.

“Asked her yet?” Rosa said.

“Going to.”

“Quit inviting these men before me,” Rosa told him.

Laughter moved through the room. Men called out wedding advice, named people who couldn't be allowed near the bar, argued about suits for a ceremony nobody had agreed to hold. Aiden let them have a few seconds. The future had become large enough to stand among them.

Then he raised his hand.

“Listen,” Aiden said.

They quieted.

“Not asking you to die because I fell in love,” he told them. “Asking you to stand with me because he thinks seeing something makes it his. Took a man's wife. Took another man's stone. Had Dante taken into the desert and left there. Tonight, took Shawna.”

He closed his fingers around the cross through his shirt.

“Keeps calling this dead man's jewelry,” Aiden said. “Fine. Let him look at it while I get my girl.”

Tank came to stand beside him.

“Ten toes,” Tank called.

“Down,” the room answered.

Tank raised his voice. “Ten toes.”

The men answered louder. “Down.”

The damaged door rattled in its frame. Aiden could feel the voices against his chest. He wanted them to reach every room Red owned.

He brought the cross to his mouth, kissed it, and went outside.

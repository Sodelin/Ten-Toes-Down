## 9. Our Valedictorian Has a Gun

Aiden was deciding which pistol went with breakfast when Tank brought the cream envelope into the kitchen.

A gold crest shone above his name. Somebody had given the capital A more attention than Aiden had given certain relationships.

“School,” Tank said, laying it beside the plate.

“Whose?” Aiden asked.

“Yours,” Tank told him.

Aiden put one pistol down. “I got a school?”

Shawna stopped pouring coffee. Rosa turned from the counter. Mercedes, still occupying the guest room and now occupying its owner's breakfast, lowered her toast.

“Las Vegas College,” Tank read.

“Oh,” Aiden said. “Those people.”

Shawna set the pot down. “You went to college?”

“Went to the building,” Aiden told her.

“When?” she asked.

“Twenty-two,” he said. “Free lunch. Excellent chicken.”

Tank opened the second page.

“Says valedictorian,” he told Aiden.

Aiden nodded. “Paying attention.”

Rosa reached for the pages. “Give me that.”

“Don't wrinkle my achievement,” Aiden warned her.

“Looking for the payment demand,” Rosa said. “Mercedes, check whether he's on a milk carton too. Been somewhere two years and forgot.”

Mercedes leaned over Rosa's shoulder.

“Highest marks in the college's history,” she read aloud.

Tank looked up. “Marks in what?”

“History,” Aiden told him. “She just said.”

Shawna turned his face gently toward hers.

“Baby, what did you do to these people?” she asked.

He thought about the chicken.

Two years earlier, a friend told him the college's adult scholarship day served food. The program took people twenty-two and older. Aiden was exactly twenty-two and hungry enough to have a future if it came with a plate.

The registration woman told him lunch was for applicants. He became one before she finished the word. She gave him a booklet: mathematics, reading, music with something wrong on the page, a question about two promises that couldn't both be kept.

He remembered eating and writing. Correcting the music while waiting for another piece of chicken. Taking two rolls when he handed everything in. He explained this to the kitchen.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

“You ate a degree,” Tank said.

“Thought while I chewed,” Aiden told him. “Acting like it's forbidden technology.”

Mercedes turned the invitation over. “They want you there at eleven.”

Shawna checked the clock. Nine twenty.

“We need clothes,” she said.

“Got plans,” Aiden reminded her.

Porsche entered carrying a garment bag, sunglasses still on.

“Your plans just got a hat,” she said. “You're going.”

Aiden tapped the blue gala invitation beside his plate. “Red.”

“Two nights away,” Porsche told him. “Graduation's in a hundred minutes. You can count that far.”

Tank lifted a finger. “He has the highest—”

“Don't help,” Rosa told him.

Shawna kissed Aiden. “I want to see my man graduate.”

He looked at her smiling above him. She could have asked him to graduate from the moon with that face.

“Get dressed,” he said, standing carefully. “We're going to college.”

Tank looked at his plate.

“Bring it,” Aiden told him. “Apparently that's the entrance exam.”

***

Tank drove the Mercedes. In the mirror, Aiden watched Porsche follow in Leon's newest Bentley, dark green this time. Leon had sent a note explaining that white brought back memories. Mercedes held a camera in the passenger seat. Behind them came Rosa in yellow bright enough to underfund the campus flowers, and Marcellus wearing the clean shirt she'd helped choose a tie for.

Shawna wore white again. Aiden preferred this occasion for it. He'd chosen black trousers and a jacket that covered the bandages, his shirt open enough for the cross. The pistol sat beneath the jacket, clear of the wound.

Graduates in blue gowns crowded the campus gates. Grown people carried bouquets and checked their phones, trying to arrange themselves for photographs before their relatives arranged them worse.

As Tank stopped near the fountain, Aiden heard a man point him out.

“That's him,” the man said to his friend. “Mercedes nigga.”

His friend looked up from a program. “It's a college, Marcus. Seventeen Mercedes in the parking lot.”

“Flying Mercedes,” Marcus clarified.

The friend looked properly, then looked back at the program and struck Marcus's shoulder.

“Our valedictorian?” he asked.

“That's what I'm saying,” Marcus told him.

By the time Aiden got out, people around the fountain were turning. A woman came down the steps with a blue robe folded over her arms. Silver glasses, dark skin, hair pinned with a precision that made Aiden straighten his own collar.

“Professor Yvonne Halcyon,” she said, reaching him. “We met at your admission.”

“You had the chicken,” Aiden told her.

Her smile widened. “You remember.”

“Don't forget a good plate,” he said.

She gave him the robe. “We've been looking for you.”

“Been around,” Aiden said.

Halcyon glanced at the Mercedes. “We've seen.”

Principal Percival Mercer came down behind her in academic blue with enough gold to make Aiden wonder whether a crown came with it. He had a gray beard and a deep voice that failed him on the first attempt at Aiden's name. His eyes filled before they shook hands.

“At last,” Mercer said.

Tank leaned close to Aiden. “What the fuck was in that booklet?”

“Apparently me,” Aiden said.

Mercer took his hand in both of his.

“Your work changed this college,” the principal told him.

“Which part?” Aiden asked.

“The final page,” Mercer said.

Aiden tried to remember. “Blank one?”

“You corrected the examination on it,” Mercer explained.

“There was a mistake,” Aiden said.

“Six,” Mercer told him. “We'd found two in twenty years.”

He opened a folder. A copy of Aiden's impatient handwriting lay inside, complete with the grease mark from lunch. Mercer said they'd framed the original.

Halcyon explained that the scholarship board had accepted Aiden's independent work in place of the standard course sequence. His essays had circulated through four departments. The music analysis had repaired the conservatory's examination. Professor Lorne had taught his final solution since the previous spring.

“Hope Lorne has a nice voice,” Aiden said.

A man behind Halcyon raised a hand. “I do my best.”

Shawna turned to Aiden, her eyes bright.

“You didn't tell me any of this,” she said.

“Didn't know they kept it,” he told her.

A graduate nearby took off his cap and laughed into it.

“Came every night for three years,” the graduate said.

Aiden looked at him. “You're here, aren't you?”

The man straightened. “Yeah.”

“Come on, then,” Aiden told him. “We're graduating.”

The graduate put his cap back on and joined them walking toward the buildings.

***

Mercer insisted on taking Aiden through the science hall on the way to the auditorium. Gold letters above the entrance announced AIDEN HALL. Aiden stopped beneath them while Tank raised his phone.

“Got a hall,” Aiden told Shawna.

“Several,” Mercer corrected him.

Rosa turned on the principal. “Don't tell him. He'll name the doors.”

Inside, three professors stood over a long table covered in papers. Their discussion stopped in pieces as the visitors entered. Lorne picked up a drawing and looked hopefully at Aiden.

“Perhaps you could settle something,” he said.

“He has a ceremony,” Halcyon reminded him.

“One minute,” Lorne promised.

Aiden came to the table. “Everybody needs one minute until I get there. Show me.”

It was a drawing of the auditorium roof. Lines crossed beneath columns of figures. Someone had circled the same span three times in red. Lorne explained that the model produced two different answers.

Aiden looked through the window at the auditorium, then back at the paper.

“Old roof,” he said.

Lorne touched a line. “The dimensions—”

“Changed when they moved the stage,” Aiden told him. “That window's eight panels wide. You've drawn seven.”

All three professors looked outside.

Aiden took the pencil. He moved the line, followed the load across the corrected drawing, and changed the two numbers that no longer needed to fight.

Lorne read what he'd written. The professor beside him compared it with another sheet and sat so suddenly his chair rolled backward.

“He's right,” the seated man said.

“Say it like you enjoyed finding out,” Aiden told him.

The professor looked up. “He's right!”

The room laughed. Mercer began crying again.

Aiden turned to Shawna. “Your man got a roof.”

She caught the edge of his robe. “Get under it before they name another building.”

At the door, Lorne touched Aiden's arm gently.

“What do you do for a living?” Lorne asked.

“Improves transportation,” Tank answered before Aiden could.

## 13. Everybody Bring Your Heart

At nine forty, the Mercedes stopped outside the lake house.

Aiden got out with Dante's folder under one arm and the photograph against his chest. The blue box stayed on the seat. He walked up the path, saw the lights shining through the glass front door, and struck the door with his fist.

The pane broke inward. Pain opened across his knuckles. He drew his hand back to hit the remaining glass, and Tank caught his wrist.

“Aiden,” Tank said.

Aiden swung at him with the other hand. The folder fell. Tank's head turned with the blow, but his grip stayed closed.

“Let go,” Aiden ordered.

“No,” Tank said.

“Had the money this whole time.”

“Yeah,” Tank answered.

Aiden struck him again. His side pulled so sharply that his knees bent beneath him. He tried to drag Tank toward the broken glass. Tank turned, put his body between Aiden and the door, and held him against the wall instead.

Tank was too heavy to move. Aiden hated knowing it, hated that Tank could feel how little strength the last blow had left him.

“Let me walk in there thinking I knew what was mine,” Aiden said. “Let him laugh at me.”

Tank looked past him into the house.

“Heard him,” Tank said. “Was standing right there.”

Aiden shoved once more. Tank released his wrist when his fist stopped trying to reach the glass.

Inside, the front room still wore the remains of a party. Champagne chilled in silver buckets. A red heel lay beneath the piano. On a side table, somebody had tipped a little white pile onto a photograph of Aiden and Shawna, choosing her face as the available surface.

He swept the table clear.

Glass and silver hit the floor. Four men rose from the couches before he spoke.

“Everybody out,” Aiden told them.

One man had a woman's handbag over his shoulder. He looked down at it, turned toward the hall to find its owner, then saw Aiden's face and decided the bag could notify her itself.

They left through the broken door. Tank picked up Dante's folder from the path, carried it inside, and pushed the damaged door as far shut as it would go.

“Me too?” Tank asked.

Aiden looked at his torn jacket, his bleeding mouth, the place a cuff link had come loose. His hands carried blood from people Aiden had watched him lift. Nothing on him gave a simple answer anymore.

“Get the money,” Aiden said.

Tank went upstairs.

Aiden sat on the piano bench because it was closest and his legs had started shaking. That afternoon, Shawna had sat here and missed a note. She'd blamed the piano. He had offered to buy another.

“Every time I play something wrong?” she'd asked him, laughing over her shoulder.

He had told her yes. Meant it too. The house would have been a fucking orchestra by Christmas.

Her perfume remained near the bench. He put his head down and remembered her dancing with Red. They had planned it together. He'd said he could take what she needed to do. He'd discovered his actual capacity in front of several hundred witnesses.

A heavy thump came from upstairs.

“Don't drop her money,” Aiden shouted.

“Got it,” Tank called back.

“You been saying that,” Aiden said.

Tank came down with two bags. No silver case. The case had stayed broken at the motel while the money came here, past the door Aiden had believed belonged to the three of them.

Tank opened both bags on the dining table. Cash filled the space between the candles Shawna had bought because, according to her, the house smelled like men lying to each other.

Aiden looked at the candles. She had bought them before she knew.

“All of it,” Tank told him.

“Counted?” Aiden asked.

“More than once.”

“Feel good each time?” Aiden asked.

Tank shook his head.

Aiden took a bundle and threw it at his chest. The band broke. Bills spread across the floor.

“Your tip,” Aiden said.

Tank watched the money settle.

“Look at me,” Aiden demanded. “What did you think was going to happen?”

Tank looked up.

“Thought you were going to die,” he said.

The refrigerator hummed in the next room. Aiden hadn't heard it until then.

Tank rubbed both palms over his head, leaving a streak of blood near his temple.

“That morning,” Tank said. “Before she drove. You kept talking about water and a house. Room for her to sing. She kept telling you to look at her. Half the time you weren't answering what she asked.”

Aiden could remember her voice in the car. The words themselves had broken apart. He didn't want Tank arranging them for him.

“So you went shopping,” Aiden said.

“Went back,” Tank answered. His voice rose, then steadied. “T was there. Money was there. Ten years I stood behind that man. Every room. Every car. Somebody said his name wrong, I was who got up.”

“Loved him,” Aiden said.

“Yeah.” Tank kept his eyes on him.

“Say it again,” Aiden told him.

“Loved him,” Tank repeated. “Want a lie about that too?”

Blood had reached his chin. He let it stay.

“You knew him one night,” Tank continued. “I knew him ten years. Know what he was. Give you something with one hand, keep the other round your neck. Know all of it. Still got to that pool and wanted him to stand up.”

Aiden looked down at the bills around Tank's shoes.

“When he didn't?” Aiden asked.

Tank swallowed. “Took what was left.”

Aiden's anger stayed. But for the first time he could see a man inside the explanation, standing in a place that had emptied around him.

“Red called after,” Aiden said.

Tank nodded. “Said he knew. Said the money bought him whatever he asked.”

“Believed that?” Aiden asked.

“For a while,” Tank said.

“Until when?”

Tank looked at Aiden's chest. “Until he put his hand on her and you stopped breathing.”

Aiden felt the room tilt again.

“Don't tell me what I looked like,” he said.

“Like you did in that car,” Tank answered.

Aiden picked up one of the candles and hurled it into the empty fireplace. Glass burst against brick. The small flame went out.

“Not dying in another fucking car,” Aiden said.

Tank stayed beside the table.

“Then let me drive,” he told Aiden. “Let me get one thing right.”

***

Blue light moved across the windows.

Aiden was on his feet before the cars stopped. For one foolish instant he thought they had brought Shawna home.

Tank looked through the front window.

“Not ours,” Tank said.

A silver-haired captain came through the damaged door without knocking. Two younger officers followed him. The nameplate on the captain's chest read FROST. Aiden recognized the Black woman behind him: Bell, from the station when he'd brought in the robbers yesterday.

All three saw the open bags. Bell's eyes moved from them to Aiden's cut hand. Frost looked at the badge inside his ruined jacket.

“Take it off,” Frost ordered.

“Selling shirts?” Aiden asked.

“The badge,” Frost said.

Tank stepped between the captain and the money.

“Got an appointment?” Tank asked.

“Move,” Frost told him.

“House,” Tank said, pointing down. “Ask like you walked into one.”

Frost rested his hand on his gun. Bell glanced at the other officer. Neither came farther into the room.

Aiden moved around Tank.

“What do you want?” Aiden asked Frost.

“Redmond filed a complaint,” Frost said. “Theft. Assault. Armed men on his property. A woman held against her will.”

Aiden's face went cold.

“Last part's accurate,” he said.

“We're recovering the money,” Frost told him. “Your authority is suspended pending an investigation.”

“Who sent you?” Aiden asked.

“You don't question me.”

“Doing it beautifully,” Aiden said. “Who?”

Frost unfolded a paper from his pocket and held it out. Aiden left it hanging between them.

“Knox signed that?” Aiden asked.

“Chief has larger concerns,” Frost replied.

“Bought me a Lamborghini two days ago,” Aiden said. “Thinking about me plenty.”

Bell looked more closely at the paper. Aiden could remember her taking a plate at the lunch he'd bought the station. She looked less certain about the meal this visit was supposed to provide.

“Bell,” Frost said. “Collect the bags.”

She didn't move.

Frost turned toward her. “That was an order.”

Aiden set his phone on the table and called Knox.

Frost reached toward it. Tank caught his wrist.

“Let the telephone ring,” Tank told him.

Aiden picked up the phone when Knox answered on the second ring.

“Tell me you have her,” Knox said.

Aiden couldn't answer immediately. She knew. Her first question wasn't about the money, the fire, or the badge on his chest.

“Not yet,” he managed. “Send Frost?”

A silence. Then Knox spoke differently.

“Speaker,” she told him.

Aiden pressed it and returned the phone to the table.

“Wade,” Knox said through the phone.

Frost stopped pulling against Tank's hand.

“Chief, we're securing—” Frost began.

“You at his house?” Knox asked.

“Evidence of—” Frost tried.

“At his house, Wade?” Knox repeated.

Frost stared at the little rectangle on the table.

“Yes,” he said.

“Captain Aiden retains his command,” Knox told him. “You have no order from me. Bring whatever Redmond gave you to my office. Bring your own badge with it.”

Bell let out a breath. Frost's face reddened above his collar.

“Choosing this thug?” Frost asked Knox.

Aiden tilted his head. “Say Captain while you cry.”

Knox didn't laugh.

“Choosing the officer who carried people out while Redmond locked the doors,” she said. “Get out of his house.”

Frost jerked toward the phone. Tank released his wrist as Aiden came forward. The captain stumbled against the dining table. A candle tipped. One bag slid toward the edge.

Frost reached for the money with his left hand and drew his pistol with his right.

Aiden struck him before he could bring the gun level. Frost fell backward into a chair and took it down with him. The pistol hit the floor and skidded beneath the piano. Bills scattered around the fallen chair.

Bell had her own weapon drawn before Frost tried to rise.

“Stay down, sir,” Bell told him.

Frost looked from her gun to her face.

“His side too?” Frost demanded.

“My chief's orders,” Bell said.

“People keep explaining,” Aiden told Frost. “At some point you have to help.”

The captain tried to get up anyway. Tank put a hand on his shoulder and returned him to the carpet with the patient care of someone putting furniture back where it belonged.

Aiden picked up the folded paper. Red's handwriting covered the bottom in three short instructions: recover the money, disarm Aiden, use Shawna's name to make him comply.

He handed it to Bell. Her mouth tightened as she read.

“Sent you for her rescue money,” Bell said to Frost.

Frost said nothing.

The other officer retrieved Frost's pistol from beneath the piano while Bell cuffed him. Then the officer took the paper, folded it carefully, and put it away as evidence.

“Where do you need us afterward, Captain?” he asked Aiden.

Tank had knelt to gather the scattered bills. Shawna's money lay beneath a chair, beside a broken glass, against the police captain's shoe.

“With Knox,” Aiden told the officer. “Keep anybody else from coming here in a uniform Red borrowed.”

“Yes, Captain,” the officer said.

They took Frost out through the door he'd entered without permission. Blue lights moved across the ceiling and disappeared.

Knox was still on the phone.

“Aiden,” she said.

“Here,” he answered.

“He's moved her from the Saint,” Knox told him. “Officers are there. Your car's safe. We can bring it.”

“Keep the roads open,” Aiden said. “My people are coming to the unfinished casino. Desert. Midnight. That's where he's taking her.”

“I'll hold the outside,” Knox told him.

“Chief,” Aiden said.

She waited.

He looked at the badge in his bloodied jacket.

“Won't forget this,” Aiden told her.

“Then bring your wife home, Captain,” Knox said. “Give me something worth remembering.”

The call ended before he could correct a word he badly wanted to make true.

Tank put the final bundle back and righted the candle.

“You really are the police,” Tank said.

“Discussed it at graduation,” Aiden told him.

“Heard the words.”

“Seeing the picture?” Aiden asked.

Tank nodded. A small smile moved his swollen mouth. Aiden didn't make him take it back.

***

Rosa arrived in a man's coat over her gala dress. One earring was missing. She brought Marcellus through the broken doorway with a hand around his arm, then stopped when she saw the blood on the wall.

Tank went out to the Mercedes for the blue box. He brought it to Marcellus, who held it closed in both hands.

Aiden's phone rang before Rosa reached the table.

Rex Crown.

Aiden almost let it ring. He remembered the boxer hitting the guard from behind and answered.

“Captain,” Rex said.

“Busy,” Aiden told him.

“Know. Your woman.”

Aiden closed his eyes. “Everybody knows?”

“People outside still saying your name,” Rex told him. “Saw what you did.”

“Need her,” Aiden said. “Not my name.”

“Then give me a place to bring myself,” Rex answered.

Aiden looked at Tank, at the bags, at the blood slipping between his own fingers. Rosa put a clean cloth from the sideboard beneath his hand without interrupting the call.

“How bad you hurt?” Aiden asked Rex.

“Pride's worse than the face,” Rex said.

“Stay home with it,” Aiden told him.

“Didn't call for a bedtime,” Rex said. “You could've left me there. Same people watched you beat me, could've watched me die. You came back. Give me somewhere to put that.”

Aiden looked toward the dark glass facing the lake.

“Unfinished casino,” he told Rex. “Desert. Midnight.”

Rex repeated the location.

“There,” Rex said.

“Your friend with the gun,” Aiden began.

“Not coming,” Rex told him. “Got better friends.”

Aiden laughed once. It pulled his side hard enough to make Rosa look up.

“What?” Rosa asked him.

“Making sense,” Aiden said, nodding toward the phone.

Rex raised his voice when he heard her.

“Tell those women their check's good,” Rex said. “Don't stop being a gentleman because somebody rearranged my evening.”

Rosa took the phone from Aiden.

“Bring your gentleman ass ready to help,” she told Rex.

“Yes, ma'am,” Rex said.

She ended the call and returned the phone.

“Let them do something,” Rosa told Aiden.

He looked at her.

“People who love you,” Rosa said. “Let them.”

She touched his good shoulder, then looked toward the door.

“Which stupid motherfucker broke that?” Rosa asked.

No one answered. She looked down at the cloth beneath Aiden's hand.

“Of course,” Rosa said.

She took his wrist. Now that her fingers were holding still against his skin, he could feel them tremble.

“Where is she?” Rosa asked.

“Red—” Aiden began.

“Know fucking Red,” Rosa said. “Where?”

“Taking her to the casino,” Aiden answered. “Midnight.”

“Then why are you bleeding on the furniture?” Rosa asked.

He began to speak, and her grip tightened.

“Get angry after she's here,” Rosa told him. “Buy a mountain. Fight the whole mountain. Right now, stand up without falling over.”

“Don't talk to me like a kid,” Aiden said.

“Stop making it necessary,” Rosa replied.

Tank turned his face away.

“Funny?” Aiden asked him.

“No,” Tank said.

“Good,” Aiden answered.

Rosa sat him on the piano bench and went for a thicker towel. Marcellus remained by the table, looking at the bags.

“Yours?” Marcellus asked Aiden.

“Hers,” Aiden said.

The old man nodded, as though that was the only figure he needed.

***

Aiden's phone vibrated on the table while Rosa came back. She picked it up, glanced at the screen, and stopped walking.

Red had sent a photograph.

Shawna sat in a white chair. Her dress had torn at the shoulder. Hair covered one side of her face. Red stood behind her with his hand beneath her chin, turning his blue ring toward the camera.

Aiden stood so fast the bench fell backward.

Rosa caught his shirt.

“Aiden,” she said.

“Going to kill him,” Aiden told her.

“Look at her,” Rosa said.

“Am looking.”

“Her,” Rosa repeated.

He forced his eyes away from Red's fingers. Shawna faced the lens. Her mouth was slightly swollen on one side. Her eyes were open and fierce. Whoever had taken the picture had not made her lower them.

Below it, Red had written: *She remembers this room.*

Aiden called him and put the phone on speaker. Rosa remained beside him as it rang.

Red answered immediately.

“Touch her again,” Aiden began, “and—”

“Sitting right here,” Red said.

“Put her on,” Aiden ordered.

“Ask nice.”

Aiden couldn't get enough air. Rosa squeezed the back of his neck. He could feel every finger.

“Please,” Aiden said.

Red laughed softly. Aiden held the phone and listened to the man enjoying that word.

Then Shawna spoke.

“Aiden?” she asked.

“Baby,” he said.

“Are you hurt?” Shawna asked.

Aiden closed his eyes. “No.”

“Don't lie to me.”

“Here,” he told her. “I'm here.”

Fabric rustled near her phone. He heard a breath stop too early and couldn't tell why.

“Tell Rosa I ruined her dress,” Shawna said.

Rosa put a fist against her mouth.

“Doesn't care about the dress,” Aiden told Shawna.

“Does,” Shawna said. “Tell her I'll pay.”

Rosa took the phone.

“Owe me a fucking earring too, bitch,” Rosa told her.

Shawna laughed once.

Aiden stood motionless beside the fallen bench. That small laugh had reached the room with more of her in it than the photograph could hold.

Red came back onto the line.

“Midnight,” he said. “What you stole, my money, and an apology.”

Aiden took the phone from Rosa.

“Want me on my knees?” Aiden asked him.

“Now you're listening,” Red said.

“Keep her alive,” Aiden told him. “See me any way you want.”

Red hung up.

Tank picked up the bench and set it behind Aiden. He remained standing, looking at the darkened phone.

Marcellus held out a hand.

“The photograph,” Marcellus said. “Let me see.”

Aiden passed him the phone. He had run out of things he could break that Red would feel.

Marcellus enlarged the picture. His thumb stopped over the blue ring beneath Shawna's chin.

“There you are,” Marcellus whispered.

Aiden looked at him. The old man had begun to smile, and there was nothing happy about it.

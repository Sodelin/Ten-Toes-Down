## 6. Forty Million Reasons to Die

Red's note lay open on Aiden's knee the next morning. The sentence was written in red ink. He had looked at it through enough of the night to know exactly where the pen had pressed harder.

*Dante didn't die for three million.*

Tank drove. Aiden sat with a diamond pin hiding the damaged place on his jacket and the bullet itself hidden considerably deeper. He folded the note along its crease, then opened it again.

“You sleep at all?” Tank asked him.

“I can dream about firing you with my eyes open,” Aiden said.

Tank took Vega's small bottle from the console and handed it over. “Take what she told you.”

“I heard the doctor,” Aiden said.

“Listen to her, then,” Tank told him.

Aiden put the bottle in his pocket. Tank wore black again. A cut crossed his knuckles, and Aiden couldn't remember seeing him get it. Yesterday those hands had carried a coffin and an old man. Now they were taking him to breakfast with Red.

At the Saint, two men reached the Mercedes before the valet. Aiden stepped out holding the note.

“Your boss sent for me,” he said.

One man extended his hand toward Aiden's pistol.

“You can have the note,” Aiden told him.

The man looked back at the entrance. Red stood inside the glass doors in a cream suit. He raised one finger. Both guards moved away.

“Aiden,” Red said when they reached him. “Glad you came.”

“You'd have been sad?” Aiden asked.

Red smiled. “I had breakfast made.”

“Feed me before the conversation gets hungry,” Aiden told him.

Red put an arm around his shoulders. Aiden let it stay there. He wanted to see how close Red would come to a man who'd shot his partner. Close enough to squeeze, apparently. Aiden watched the hand lift away without returning the gesture.

Before taking them upstairs, Red led them past a white piano. Flowers crowded its polished lid. Above it hung a photograph of Shawna singing.

Red stopped beneath the photograph. Aiden looked because the man was waiting for him to look, then resented doing it.

“You play?” Red asked.

“Breakfast behind the piano?” Aiden said.

Red rested a hand on its gleaming edge. “I wondered what you brought to a woman who already sings.”

Tank closed his eyes.

“Please,” Tank said quietly.

Aiden turned toward him. “You got a request?”

“Eggs,” Tank said. “Before the people serving them retire.”

Aiden sat at the piano. The bench pulled at his side; he shifted until both feet rested flat. An arrangement lay open on the stand with Shawna's name across the top. Dense chords, long introduction, enough movement between the hands to require attention. He looked up at her photograph, then back at the notes.

“That's a difficult piece,” Red observed.

“Haven't heard your kitchen yet,” Aiden said. “Everything's difficult till somebody does it.”

His first chord stopped the nearest waiter.

The keys felt clean and slightly cool. His hands found the distances. He remembered a church piano with two chipped keys, an older woman's fingers correcting the angle of his wrist. Suppers earned by staying to play after everyone else went home. He'd come into plenty of rooms hungry and found the piano before the food.

He played the introduction without simplifying it. Then he brought the melody into his right hand and gave the left a rougher answer beneath it. The low notes filled the polished room. A waiter holding a coffee pot began shifting his weight in time, stopped, and left one foot going.

“Oh, this nigga,” Tank said beside the bench.

“Something wrong?” Red asked him.

“With Aiden? All morning,” Tank said. “With that? Listen.”

Aiden kept his eyes on the page. He could feel Red behind him, waiting for something to go badly.

“You just read that?” Red asked.

“You left it open,” Aiden told him.

Red stepped nearer. “Shawna never said—”

“You want me to play or collect witness statements?” Aiden asked.

The man at the door laughed. Aiden saw Red turn in the piano's reflection. The guard straightened fast enough that Aiden nearly lost a note enjoying it.

He finished and lifted his hands. The last chord stayed in the room.

A waiter clapped once. He looked at Red, looked at Aiden, and clapped again. Others joined him. Aiden rested his hands on his thighs until they were quiet.

Tank opened both palms toward the piano.

“You ordered that,” he told Red. “Asked what he brought. He unpacked.”

Red looked up at Shawna's photograph. “She never mentioned it.”

“I keep giving her things to find out,” Aiden said.

He closed the key cover gently. The piano had done nothing to deserve being punished for its owner.

“Try it sometime,” Aiden added.

Tank caught his elbow as he stood, before the pain could make the exit less satisfying.

“Breakfast,” Tank reminded him.

“You hungry?” Aiden asked.

“I've lived two years since we parked,” Tank said.

***

The penthouse had been cleaned since Aiden and Shawna fled it. Red took them through to a terrace with a table set for three. Six waiters stood nearby. Roses rose from crystal vases; the one red flower stood directly in front of Aiden's place.

Tank picked it up.

“Don't eat that,” Aiden told him.

Tank stared at him. “Wasn't going to.”

Red laughed, making little dimples Aiden disliked immediately.

Aiden sat and shifted against the chair's hard edge. Red watched the movement.

“T knew where to put a bullet,” Red said.

“Didn't know where to keep his own,” Aiden answered.

A waiter stopped pouring. Red raised his juice.

“To our absent friend,” he said.

Aiden drank water. Tank left his glass untouched until Red offered the bacon. He took a piece and looked annoyed with his own hand afterward.

“Where's Shawna?” Red asked Aiden.

“With people who like her alive,” Aiden said.

“I've always liked her alive,” Red told him.

“Expensive way of showing it,” Aiden said.

Red laid his fork down. “I bought the dress she was wearing when you met her.”

Aiden leaned forward before remembering his side. Pain burned through him. He kept looking at Red.

“Want me to send you the fucking dress?” he asked.

“I want you to understand how little you understand,” Red said.

“First thing you've said all morning,” Aiden told him.

Red smiled again. He described sheltering Shawna after Valentino died, protecting her from people who might have killed her over what she'd done. Then, he said, his own partner arranged to take her and three million out of his house. The partner died. Aiden inherited the business, the car, the chain, and the woman.

“A fortunate week,” Red concluded.

“Forgot the bullet,” Aiden said.

“No,” Red told him. “I can see you haven't.”

Tank's chair scraped. Aiden raised a hand toward him without looking away from Red.

“You think I planned it?” Aiden asked.

“I think you're young enough to be honest when it profits you,” Red said.

Aiden kept his palm raised until Tank's chair stopped moving.

“Here's some honesty,” Aiden told Red. “Send another man after her and I come back without an invitation.”

Red wiped his mouth. Around the terrace, waiters held trays and glasses without making a sound. Men older than him waited for permission to refill his juice. Aiden could feel the money in how little its owner needed to move.

“Keep T's streets,” Red said. “It's a generous beginning.”

“Keep what's mine?” Aiden asked. “Put a bow on it.”

“The money returns,” Red said. “Shawna comes home. We stop putting people in the ground.”

“Which ones you want back?” Aiden asked him. “T? Valentino? Dante? Got a favorite?”

The smile left Red at Dante's name.

Aiden let himself settle back. He waited for the pain to ease enough to breathe properly.

“You sent a note,” he said. “Finish it.”

Red glanced toward the staff. They went inside. He waited until the final door shut.

“Eight years ago I bought something extraordinary,” Red said. “From a man who didn't appreciate the opportunity.”

“What man?” Aiden asked.

“Marcellus Vale,” Red answered. “A jeweler. Violent when disappointed.”

Tank's fingers tightened around his glass. Aiden noticed Red glance at the movement too.

“His masterpiece was Blue Mercy,” Red continued. “A large blue diamond. Worth about forty million, depending on who's asking.”

Aiden had been carefully arranging his face all morning. Forty million knocked the arrangement loose.

“For one rock?” he asked.

“One,” Red said.

“Forty million, that bitch better call me when I'm lonely,” Aiden told him.

Red looked amused. “It's made several men lonely.”

“What does Dante have to do with it?” Aiden asked.

Red said Dante met Marcellus in prison and came home with unfortunate ideas. Decided money in Red's accounts belonged to a woman he'd never met.

“Which woman?” Aiden asked.

“Vale's dead wife,” Red said. He lifted his glass. “Men do peculiar things for dead women. Avoid the habit.”

Aiden stood. Tank stood beside him.

“Tell me about Dante,” Aiden said, “or keep talking about my woman until this breakfast gets expensive.”

Red stayed seated. “Shawna knows the story.”

“She told me what happened,” Aiden said.

“Ask again,” Red told him.

He pushed an envelope across the table. Inside were two invitations on heavy blue paper edged in silver. Aiden read Shawna's name before his own.

“The diamond is the centerpiece of my gala in three nights,” Red said. “She might like to see what her husband died chasing.”

Aiden pressed a thumb into the paper. “Printed these before the funeral?”

“I print a lot of invitations,” Red said.

“Print one for yours,” Aiden told him.

Tank touched his elbow. Aiden pulled free. He needed to cross that terrace on his own feet without giving Red anything else to watch.

At the door, Red called after him.

“Tell her I still have the piano,” he said. “The white one. She'll know.”

Aiden turned. Red was cutting into his breakfast again.

He went back across the terrace and picked up the vase of roses. One stem snapped against his fingers as he lifted it. He kept the broken rose in one hand and carried the vase to the railing.

Below, an empty red sports car stood beneath the terrace. Aiden threw the vase down onto it.

Glass broke. A woman screamed from somewhere near the entrance.

Red rose halfway from his chair.

“That's my fucking car,” he said.

Aiden put the wet broken rose across Red's plate.

“Tell it I still have the vase,” he said, and left.

***

Downstairs, the valet stood beside a red Aston Martin with a towel in his hands. The vase had gone through its roof. Roses and glass lay inside the cabin. Aiden couldn't see a promising use for the towel.

“Mr. Red's going to be upset,” the valet told him.

“Got a head start upstairs,” Aiden said. “Where's mine?”

Tank went toward the Mercedes. Its glass had been replaced, its rear repaired, the body polished until Aiden could see himself approaching. He liked the car a little more every time it survived him.

A man carrying a helmet came out of the next hotel. He looked at the ruined Aston, then at Aiden.

“You Aiden?” the man asked.

“Depends who's hoping,” Aiden said.

The man grinned. “You flew over a boat.”

“Word's getting around,” Aiden told him.

The man had a scar down his chin and a leather jacket with a silver eagle across its back. He pointed toward a black motorcycle.

“Saint,” he said. “Little Saint. I ride.”

“Good for you,” Aiden said.

Saint shook his head, still smiling. “I mean I ride. Thought you ought to know.”

Tank opened the Mercedes door.

“Everybody's got something this man ought to know,” Tank said.

Saint looked beyond them. His smile disappeared.

“Know those two cars aren't valets,” he said.

A gray sports car had stopped across the exit. Behind it, a black SUV rolled forward with its windows lowering. The passenger held a pistol against his chest. Aiden couldn't see anything in the man's clothes or face that told him whether this was about Red, T, or a sum of money.

“Inside,” Tank told Aiden.

The first bullet struck the Mercedes door while Tank's hand was still on it.

Aiden grabbed the valet by his collar and threw him behind the car. Glass burst from the hotel's revolving door. People dropped behind luggage. Saint ran toward his motorcycle, pulling his helmet on as he went.

Tank fired from beside Aiden's shoulder. Aiden got into the passenger seat. Tank went around the hood, slid behind the wheel, and put the car in reverse before his door was fully closed.

The Mercedes hit the gray car and moved it sideways. The other driver shouted through his window. Tank hit him again. The gap opened, and they scraped through with sparks beneath Aiden's door.

“Somebody really hates breakfast,” Aiden said.

“Head down,” Tank ordered.

“I need it where I can see,” Aiden told him.

“See lower,” Tank said.

The gray car followed with a fender hanging against its wheel. The SUV came behind it, its passenger firing through the open window. A shot broke the Mercedes's new rear glass. Aiden hunched against the dashboard as pieces struck the seat behind him.

Tank took the first corner too fast. A taxi braked in their path. He missed its bumper by inches and turned into a narrow street lined with delivery trucks. The gray car followed. Behind it, the SUV struck a streetlight and kept coming, dragging something metal beneath it.

Little Saint drew alongside on his motorcycle. Through his helmet visor, Aiden saw him grinning. Saint pointed ahead.

A tour bus stood across the next intersection. Its driver argued with a man in a yellow vest. Neither was looking toward the approaching cars.

“Tank!” Aiden said.

“I see it!” Tank answered.

“Keep hearing that before we hit something,” Aiden told him.

Tank braked. The Mercedes slid. The gray car couldn't stop and hit their rear quarter, spinning them halfway around.

Now Aiden looked directly at its driver. He pulled his pistol from his waistband, raised it toward the broken rear window, and fired. The man's windshield whitened. He ducked, lost the turn, and drove into the tour bus's empty luggage compartment.

The bus driver abandoned his argument and ran.

Tank straightened the Mercedes and pushed into the gap beside the bus. Their door scraped along it. Aiden watched a pink suitcase shake behind a little window each time the car struck another panel.

Then they came onto open road. Saint was still beside them. He raised a fist. Aiden lifted his own.

The SUV hit the motorcycle's rear wheel.

The bike snapped sideways. Saint flew across the hood of a parked car and rolled into the road. His helmet struck the pavement.

Tank swore and braked. Aiden had his door open before they stopped.

“Where the fuck—” Tank began.

“Get him!” Aiden shouted back.

He ran across the road. The SUV turned toward him. Its driver was smiling behind the windshield. Aiden could see the wheels change angle, lining up with him and the man struggling on the asphalt.

Saint had got himself onto one elbow. His helmet shell was cracked, blood running down his cheek beneath it.

“Leg works?” Aiden asked, catching his arm.

“Works,” Saint said.

“Work it with me,” Aiden told him.

He pulled Saint up. They stumbled toward the parked trucks while the SUV accelerated at their backs.

Tank drove the Mercedes across its path.

The crash filled the street. Aiden pushed Saint behind a truck and turned with his pistol raised. The SUV had stopped against the Mercedes. Its driver tried to open his door. Tank got out, slammed it back on him, then reached through the open window and hauled him out that way instead.

Aiden aimed at the passenger. The man dropped his pistol and put his hands on the dashboard.

“You can keep breathing,” Aiden told him. “That's all you're getting today.”

Saint stood behind him, jacket torn at one shoulder. He looked from Tank to Aiden and the smoking cars.

“You came back,” Saint said.

“You said you ride,” Aiden told him. “Haven't seen enough yet.”

Saint's grin returned slowly.

“Any road,” he said. “Any fucking road, Aiden. You call, I'm there.”

A crowd had formed at the corner. Someone called Aiden's name. A woman raised her phone and asked him to look toward her. He turned his unbloodied side toward it. If everyone insisted on recording him, they could at least be useful about the angle.

Knox's officers arrived while smoke still rose from the engines. One looked over the wrecked SUV and shook his head.

“Chief said you were something,” the officer told Aiden.

“Chief pays attention,” Aiden said.

Tank called for another car to collect Saint. Aiden waited beside the motorcycle rider until it came, then returned to the Mercedes. The engine still ran. Tank tied one damaged door shut with a belt.

They drove away with people watching from both sides of the street.

Before the highway, Aiden called Shawna.

“Baby, you want lunch?” he asked.

“Why are there sirens behind you?” she demanded.

“People wanted breakfast,” Aiden said. “I'm thinking ahead.”

***

At the house, Shawna stood barefoot on a little platform while a woman pinned a silver dress around her hips. Aiden stopped in the doorway. For a moment, he wanted everybody else out so he could look at her without an audience.

Then the watch slid down her wrist.

Dante's watch. The song. Now a diamond worth forty million and a prison story nobody had told him. Aiden had bought the water and bled getting her here, but it felt as though the dead man kept arriving ahead of him.

Rosa looked up from a chair.

“Why you got that face?” she asked.

“What face?” Aiden said.

“The one that's about to make the dress cost more,” Rosa told him.

Aiden held up the invitations.

“Blue Mercy,” he said to Shawna.

She stopped moving. The dressmaker kept pinning until Rosa put a hand on her shoulder.

“Let's get coffee,” Rosa told the woman.

“I don't drink coffee,” the dressmaker said.

“Good day to start,” Rosa answered, helping her up.

They left. Shawna stepped down from the platform. A pin fell between her and Aiden, clicking against the floor.

“What did he tell you?” she asked.

Aiden laughed once. It sounded ugly in the quiet room.

“Everybody asks what somebody else told me,” he said. “You knew about forty million dollars?”

Shawna drew back a little. “I knew about the stone.”

He saw the movement, disliked causing it, and kept going.

“Didn't seem worth mentioning?” he asked.

“You were shot,” Shawna said. “Then people shot at us yesterday. I was thinking about getting you through it.”

Aiden gestured toward the platform. “Time to choose a dress, though.”

Her face changed.

“I wanted one night where you looked at me without seeing somebody bleed,” she said.

He lowered his hand. He hadn't prepared an answer for that.

Shawna took the invitation from him. Her thumb moved over Red's name.

“He mentioned the piano?” she asked.

“Hard to miss it,” Aiden said. “White one. Apparently I'm meant to know what that means.”

“I broke the keys with a bottle the night I left,” Shawna told him. “He bought another so he could pretend I was coming back.”

Aiden looked toward the windows, then back at her.

“Said you knew what Dante died for,” he told her.

“I know what Dante told me,” Shawna said.

“Tell me, then,” Aiden asked.

She watched his face. “It starts before me.”

“Everything in this house starts before me,” he said.

Her gaze dropped to the cross at his collar.

“Want me to take that back?” Shawna asked.

Aiden's hand closed around it. “No.”

“Sit down,” she told him.

He sat in the chair Rosa had left. Shawna sat beside him with the pinned silver cloth gathered carefully over her knees.

“Dante told me after we married,” she said. “In the room Red paid for. I thought we'd go downstairs and drink with everybody, but he locked the door.”

Aiden felt his jaw tighten. He made himself leave it there without speaking.

“He said I ought to know something before deciding what kind of man I'd married,” Shawna continued.

She took his hand away from the cross and held it between both of hers.

“Then he told me about the old man,” she said.

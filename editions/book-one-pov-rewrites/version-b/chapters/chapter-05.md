## 5. Flowers for the Man I Killed

Aiden couldn't get his cuff straight.

He stood at the bedroom mirror, pinching the same fold while the rest of the room seemed half a second farther away than it ought to be. The medicine softened the edges of things without doing much for the pull in his side. The black suit hid the bandages. It did not make his hands steady.

Tank came downstairs dressed in black too and waited in the foyer until Aiden joined him.

“You don't have to go,” Tank said.

“I killed him,” Aiden reminded him.

“That's why you don't have to go,” Tank answered.

Aiden worked at the cuff again. He'd paid for the coffin, the flowers, the cars, the musicians, and a lunch afterward that half the guests might be too frightened to eat. T had called him son and then shot him. Aiden couldn't make the two things fit, so he'd bought the biggest funeral the chapel could hold.

“He had anybody else?” Aiden asked.

“Plenty people,” Tank said.

Aiden looked up. “People that loved him.”

Tank turned toward the window. Aiden left the cuff alone.

“You did,” he said.

Tank kept watching the drive.

“You can say it,” Aiden told him.

“He gave me work when I came out,” Tank said.

“Gave me a car,” Aiden answered.

Tank looked at him then. “Ain't the same.”

“I know,” Aiden said.

They stood quietly beneath the chandelier. Two men who had left T by a dry swimming pool, trying to get dressed for what came afterward.

Shawna appeared on the stairs in white.

White dress. White coat. White gloves folded in one hand. Her dark hair was pinned up, and Dante's watch shone against her wrist. Aiden watched her descend. She stopped on the last step when Tank turned.

“Oh, hell no,” Tank said.

“What?” Shawna asked.

“It's a funeral,” Tank told her.

“I know whose funeral,” she said.

Tank pointed at the coat. “You can't wear that.”

Shawna looked toward Aiden. He could have asked her to change. The morning was difficult enough without her walking into the chapel looking ready to get married on T's grave.

She was also beautiful. He could see the firmness in her mouth and the hand closed around the gloves. She had made her decision before coming downstairs.

“Get the car before she decides on a veil,” Aiden told Tank.

***

Rico met them outside the chapel, checking the cuffs of the suit he'd wear to help carry the coffin. He told Aiden his daughter had bought the gold links. Grown now, living in another state. Aiden said she had taste, and Rico grinned with his whole face.

“Thirty-two and she's dressing me already,” Rico said.

Aiden touched the edge of one cuff link before they went inside. It was warm from the man's wrist.

T's coffin stood beneath the chapel lights in deep candy blue. Car-show blue. The kind of blue that made a man pull alongside at a stoplight and hate his own payments. Gold handles gleamed along its sides. T's portrait stood six feet tall at the front, smiling above people who'd once been frightened to smile wrong around him.

Some guests rose when Aiden entered. Others stayed seated so firmly it looked like work. A woman near the front began crying louder. He couldn't tell whether she was grieving harder or hating him more. He kept walking with Shawna's hand in his and Tank behind them.

Someone whispered that he shouldn't have come.

Aiden felt his shoulders straighten. Until that whisper, he'd mostly felt sick. Now pride arrived, hot enough to frighten him a little.

“Say it loud,” he said without turning. “So I know who to look at.”

Nobody did.

They sat in the second row. Shawna held her knees together and looked straight ahead. Her pulse beat against Aiden's fingers.

The preacher described a generous man. Aiden could agree with that much. He knew people whose rent T had paid, men T had found work for, somebody's mother whose bill disappeared without explanation. T could put money into a hand and make accepting it feel like being chosen.

Then the preacher spoke of a man who loved his family.

Shawna let go of Aiden's hand.

He looked at her. She was staring at T's portrait. One white glove had become a knot in her fist, its seam pulling open between her fingers. Aiden rested his palm on her knee. She didn't move.

Tank got up.

Aiden hadn't known he meant to speak. He watched Tank carry a folded paper to the microphone, open it, look down once, and put it back in his pocket.

“T knew my mother,” Tank said.

The microphone made his voice too big for the chapel. He shifted back an inch.

“Knew she worried about me,” Tank continued. “Every time I came around, he made me call her. Didn't matter what we were doing. Call your mother, Tank. Tell her you ate.”

A woman near the aisle laughed through her crying. Tank looked at the blue box.

“She died while I was inside,” he said. “T paid for everything. Told me I didn't owe him for that.”

His hand moved down the front of his jacket, smoothing cloth already smooth.

“I owed him anyway,” Tank said.

Aiden could hear Shawna breathing beside him. The preacher shifted a foot when Tank said T had done some shit.

“Some shit I can't stand up here and fix,” Tank told the room. “I ain't going to try. But he picked me up when nobody else answered. I remember that.”

He stepped away from the microphone. On the way back, he looked at Aiden once.

Aiden nodded. It hurt. He kept his face turned toward Tank until his friend sat down.

***

Red had sent white roses taller than the coffin. A blue ribbon ran through them, his name on a card big enough to read from the aisle.

Aiden looked toward the door every time it opened. No Red. He hated realizing the man could make him wait at someone else's funeral without even attending it.

People began going up to the coffin. Shawna stayed beside him.

“You don't have to,” Aiden told her.

“I know,” she said.

Then she stood.

He went with her. T lay in a dark suit, the ring that had cut Aiden's mouth still on his hand. Someone had arranged his face into peace. Aiden remembered the hand behind his neck, the pleasure of hearing son in T's voice. Standing over the body, he felt ashamed of how much he had wanted the word to mean something.

Shawna took a folded photograph from her coat and placed it on T's chest.

It was another print from her wedding. Aiden recognized the chapel and Dante's arm around her. This photograph was wider than the one she'd shown him in the car. T stood at its edge with one hand on his brother's shoulder.

“Take your fucking picture with you,” Shawna whispered to him.

Aiden started to reach for her hand. She bent closer to the coffin.

“He loved you,” she told T. “You knew he did.”

Her voice broke on loved. Aiden looked down at the gold handle until she straightened.

Behind them, the preacher cleared his throat. Aiden had to keep his mouth shut to avoid telling the man to swallow it. She was having a minute. The dead could wait for a living woman to finish.

He touched T's cuff. The cloth felt wrong without a warm wrist moving inside it. He didn't know why he'd reached for it. He couldn't quite make himself touch the hand.

“All you had to do was let us go,” Aiden said.

Then he took Shawna's hand and walked out.

***

Rosa waited beside the cars in black sunglasses and a coat the wind kept trying to take. She told Aiden she'd come for Shawna. He had heard her tell two other people the same thing on his way down the steps.

“I ain't crying for that nigga,” Rosa said.

“Nobody asked you to,” Aiden told her.

“I know,” Rosa said. “I want the dead to be clear.”

Shawna leaned against her. Rosa wrapped the coat around both of them while the pallbearers brought T down.

Mercedes and Porsche stood near the black Mercedes with a tray of sandwiches between them. They had dressed for church and brought enough food to keep an emergency from becoming rude. Beside the Mercedes waited a new white Bentley. Porsche showed Aiden the card Leon had sent with it: watching the last one die had changed his feelings about ownership.

“That's love,” Tank said, taking two sandwiches.

“That's insurance,” Porsche told him.

Aiden saw the first black SUV coming around the chapel. Slowly. Another followed it. Dark windows, men leaning forward inside. His eyes stayed on the passenger side as the first vehicle turned toward the drive.

Tank stopped chewing.

“Down,” he said.

The first bullet shattered the sandwich tray in Porsche's hands.

Aiden caught Shawna around the waist and drove her behind the Mercedes. Rosa dropped beside them. Gunfire tore through the chapel doors. Glass fell against the steps in bright pieces.

Rico dropped beside the hearse.

Aiden looked for movement. The gold cuff links caught the sun. Nothing else moved.

Tank fired over the Mercedes's hood. The noise struck Aiden's chest. Porsche pulled Mercedes behind the rear wheel as another burst took the side mirror away.

“Everybody inside!” Aiden shouted toward the steps.

The doors stood open, but bullets crossed the drive between them and the cars. An older woman had stopped halfway down the stairs with her husband's hand in hers. The old man pulled at her arm, trying to get her down. She kept looking at Rico.

“Get them, Tank!” Aiden shouted.

Tank turned toward him. “You stay here!”

“I heard you!” Aiden answered.

Tank ran low across the drive. Shots stripped flowers from a wreath behind him. He caught the old man beneath one arm, put his other hand behind the woman's shoulders, and drove them through the chapel doors.

The first SUV came farther into the lot. Its passenger leaned out with a pistol. Aiden fired from beside the wheel. The man jerked back inside. The SUV clipped the chapel fountain and struck the empty wheelchair waiting for Aiden at the foot of the steps.

The chair spun into the flower bed.

“That nigga just hit my chair,” Aiden said.

Shawna fired twice toward the second SUV and ducked back beside him.

“You weren't using it,” she reminded him.

“Still mine,” Aiden said.

Across the drive, the hearse began to roll. Its driver lay behind the open door; Aiden couldn't tell whether he was hurt or sheltering. The engine ran. The slope carried the car slowly toward the road, T's coffin gleaming through the rear glass.

The first SUV turned after it. Its passenger got out and ran toward the hearse's open driver's door.

“They're taking T,” Rosa said beside Aiden.

He stared at the rolling car. Somebody had found a way to make the man's death more complicated.

Shawna caught Aiden's jacket before he rose.

“Baby, he's dead,” she said.

“Then he ain't driving himself home,” Aiden told her.

He kissed her once.

“Stay with the car,” he said, then went.

The drive tipped beneath his feet. Pain flashed across his ribs. Somebody shouted his name from the doors. He didn't turn to find out whether they were encouraging him or informing him of his own stupidity.

He hit the hearse as the gunman reached it.

The man had one hand on the wheel. Aiden caught his jacket and slammed him into the open door. The pistol went off skyward. The moving car dragged them sideways, Aiden's shoulder against the window, the man's elbow grinding toward his throat.

The attacker was bigger and healthy. That seemed to be his entire argument.

Aiden drove his forehead into the man's nose.

The man's grip loosened. Aiden shoved him clear and fell into the driver's seat. He pulled the door shut as the front wheels hit the curb. Behind him, T's coffin thumped against its restraints.

“You can shut up too,” Aiden told it.

The first SUV came after him. In the mirror, he saw it pass the man he'd knocked down and gather speed. The second SUV was still by the chapel when the buildings cut off his view.

He drove through the open cemetery gate across the road.

Gravel spat beneath the tires. White stones passed on both sides. He kept to the broad center drive between the trees. In the mirror, the SUV's windshield bloomed with holes as its passengers fired through their own glass.

The hearse's rear window broke. A gold handle flashed behind Aiden's shoulder.

“They're scratching your box, nigga,” he told T.

He took the stone circle at the cemetery's center hard enough to lean the hearse. The coffin struck a restraint but stayed in place. The SUV slid wide on the gravel. Aiden held the wheel tight, dragging himself upright against the pain.

At the second gate, a groundskeeper stood beside a watering truck with a hose in his hand. Aiden raised two fingers as the hearse passed. The man dropped the hose and raised two back, staring.

Aiden came onto the service road. The SUV followed, its front bumper bent, one headlight missing. Another engine roared ahead.

For a second he thought they'd boxed him in.

Then the white Bentley crossed the intersection.

Shawna was driving. Her coat was off, her white gloves on. Porsche sat beside her with a purse pressed against the dashboard. Aiden saw Shawna turn the wheel toward the SUV coming up behind him.

“Baby, don't,” he said, uselessly, through the glass.

The Bentley struck the SUV's front corner and knocked it sideways. White smoke rolled off the Bentley's tires. Porsche's door flew open and banged shut. The SUV climbed the curb and hit a stone wall; its hood sprang upward.

Aiden stopped the hearse beside them. Shawna looked across through her broken window.

“Your wife drives too, nigga,” she told him.

They weren't married. He couldn't find a reason to mention it while looking at her in those gloves.

A man stumbled from the SUV with a gun. Aiden opened the hearse door and fired. The man dropped behind the engine, and the weapon clattered into the gutter. Another attacker reached to pull him back inside.

Tank arrived from the far end of the road in Aiden's black Mercedes. When he got out, the second man lifted both hands so high they nearly touched the roof.

“Who sent you?” Tank asked, advancing.

The man kept his mouth shut.

Tank took another step. “This is the easy question.”

“T had friends!” the man blurted.

Aiden got out of the hearse, using the door to hold himself upright.

“Where the fuck were you when they passed the collection plate?” he asked.

The man's eyes moved from Aiden to the coffin visible through the broken rear window.

“You killed him,” the man said.

“And I'm burying him,” Aiden answered. “Get between those things again, you can ask him how he feels yourself.”

The attacker looked at Aiden's pistol, then at the red coming through his shirt. Shawna stepped out of the Bentley, glass shining around her shoes.

“You don't stop,” the man said to Aiden.

“Should've asked somebody before you came,” Aiden told him.

Police cruisers arrived behind Tank. Officers put the attackers on the ground and kicked the guns away. Knox got out looking directly at the hearse.

“Please tell me that's empty,” she said.

“Family in the back,” Aiden told her.

She leaned to see through the window, then straightened.

“You brought the deceased into a pursuit?” Knox asked.

“Nigga wasn't walking,” Aiden said.

Knox took off her sunglasses. For three seconds her mouth held still. Then she laughed, head tipped back, hands on her hips. The cuffed men looked toward her. Aiden watched the chief wipe beneath one eye before putting her glasses on again.

“You're wasting your fucking talents in civilian clothes,” Knox told him.

“You keep saying that,” Aiden said.

“Until somebody gives me what I want,” Knox replied.

Shawna reached him. There was a shallow cut across one hand. He took it carefully and kissed beside the blood before she could explain whose blood was whose.

“I asked you to stay with the car,” Aiden said.

“I did,” Shawna told him. “I moved the car.”

Porsche came around the Bentley and held its keys out to Tank.

“Tell Leon this one had a meaningful life too,” she said.

Tank looked at the crushed fender. “He's going to start sending bicycles.”

***

Aiden put his pistol away and got back behind the hearse's wheel. Shawna rode beside him on the return to the chapel. Police led them in. The doors opened as people heard the engines, and someone began applauding. A woman on the steps shouted his name so hard her hat shifted.

Aiden got out slowly. His side had begun collecting everything he'd promised it could collect later.

The old couple Tank had carried inside came down the steps. The woman took Aiden's face in both hands.

“You brought everybody home,” she said.

He looked past her at the white sheet near the curb.

Rico was still there.

Tank came close and quietly told him about Vernon, forty-one, who had died after they carried him inside. Aiden knew Vernon by his voice before he could remember his face. The name brought the face back. Two men gone while he was driving a dead man through a cemetery.

He held the woman's hands for a moment, then lowered them gently.

“Help me finish this,” he told Tank.

They put their hands on T's blue coffin. Four other men took the remaining places. Funeral workers guided them around the shattered glass. The musicians began the hymn again, uncertain at first, then loud enough for Aiden to hear it beneath the ringing still in his ears.

The funeral director had brought a replacement hearse around while officers cleared the drive. They carried T from the shot-up car to the waiting one. This time nobody stopped them.

***

Aiden missed the burial.

Vega was waiting at the house. People discovered business in other rooms as soon as she entered his bedroom. Shawna sat on the bed in her dirty white dress while the doctor examined him behind a closed door.

“You were told to rest,” Vega said.

“I was sitting at a funeral,” Aiden told her.

She looked at the blood on his shirt. “I'm interested in what happened after the sitting.”

He looked away.

She arranged what he needed, repeated the instructions slowly enough to offend him, and told him he would be back under her care if his condition worsened. Aiden believed her. From the way Shawna watched her leave, he didn't think there was much chance of appealing to the other authority in the room.

Tank came in with the names of the dead and the injured. He made Aiden repeat them. Rico. Vernon. The men being treated. Aiden said he'd pay for everything.

“Their mothers need a human being first,” Tank told him.

Aiden took the phone.

He called Rico's mother, gave his name, and said he was sorry. At first she asked him to say it again. Then she stopped asking anything he could answer. He sat with the phone against his ear, listening to her breathe between words that grew harder to understand.

Vernon's mother knew before he finished introducing himself. She wanted to know why her son had gone to a funeral and why anyone had brought guns. Aiden didn't try to turn his answer into a speech. He said what he knew. When she called him something worse than he had expected, he kept the phone where it was.

After the second call ended, he threw it across the room.

It struck the wall and broke its case. Shawna watched it fall.

“I want Red,” Aiden said.

“You don't know it was him,” she told him.

“Who else?” he demanded.

Shawna stayed seated. “People loved T.”

“I paid for the funeral,” Aiden said.

“You also killed him,” she answered.

He hated the steadiness of her voice. He wanted her angry beside him, wanted one person he could point all this toward without anybody telling him to wait.

“You defending Red now?” Aiden asked.

Shawna's face went cold.

“Say that again,” she told him.

He didn't.

She got up, unfastened Dante's watch, and placed it faceup on the bed between them.

“You think I forgot?” Shawna asked. “I got a house and some earrings, so I forgot where they left him?”

Aiden looked at the watch. “No.”

“Then don't talk like I'm sleeping through this,” she said.

She picked it up again. Her fingers trembled at the clasp. Aiden reached for her hand. She let him help, and he fastened the band around her wrist carefully.

“I'm scared,” he said.

Shawna looked at him.

He wished he'd found a different sentence. But he could still see her beneath the Mercedes, the glass breaking above them, her hand reaching for a pistol while he struggled to rise.

“They get to you while I'm lying here,” Aiden said. “That's what I keep seeing.”

Shawna sat beside him.

“Then stop trying to stand up every time somebody says your name,” she told him.

He rested his forehead against hers.

Outside the door, Rosa ordered Tank to eat. Tank said he wasn't hungry.

“I didn't ask for your fucking feelings about the sandwich,” Rosa told him.

Shawna laughed once through her nose. Aiden closed his eyes and held her there.

***

At sunset, Tank brought a black envelope upstairs on a silver tray.

Aiden stared at the tray. “Why are you serving me a threat?”

“Man from the Saint left it at the gate,” Tank said. “Didn't want to touch it more than I had to.”

Aiden took the envelope. Inside was an invitation printed in gold: breakfast the following morning, the Saint penthouse. His name and Shawna's appeared together like somebody's idea of welcome guests.

A smaller card lay behind it.

He read the sentence once. Then he handed it to Shawna.

She sat on the bed so suddenly he reached toward her elbow. Her thumb moved to Dante's watch and stayed there.

“What?” Tank asked them.

Shawna kept looking at the card. Aiden could still see the words though she was holding them now.

*Dante didn't die for three million.*

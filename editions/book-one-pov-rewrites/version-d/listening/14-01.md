## 14. What She Told Me After

Shawna told me this part later, sitting beside me with her bare feet tucked under my leg. She stopped when she wanted water. Started again wherever she could. I kept asking the same questions until she said the next one would cost me a glass against the forehead.

Some words she remembered exactly. Some things she wasn't giving anybody, and I had to learn that loving her didn't buy me admission to every bad minute she'd survived.

What follows is what she told me. That night, I was at home looking at a photograph. She was still inside the Saint.

***

Red took her through the back corridors with two men following and his hand locked around her arm. The party was coming apart around them. Alarms sounded. A few guests still held drinks, reluctant to abandon anything they'd paid for without a personal announcement.

A woman beside the elevators lifted a phone.

“Get my good side,” Shawna told her.

Red pulled harder. The woman lowered the phone.

“Always have to do that?” Red asked.

“Look good?”

“Make a fucking scene.”

“Dragged me out of one,” Shawna said. “Thought you liked them.”

He took her into a private room she remembered. White chairs, white carpet, a painting of a horse coming out of the ocean. Red had once spent twenty minutes explaining that horse while she tried to calculate whether she could leave without him noticing.

She said the horse looked surprised to be swimming. The artist had made a very expensive animal wonder how its night had gone wrong.

This time there was somebody's blood on the carpet.

One guard brought Shawna's purse from the office. Red took it and put it on the table. Then he sent the men outside and closed the door.

That click was when she got cold.

She told me it hadn't happened at the gunfire or while he dragged her through the corridor. It happened when she understood he wanted a room where nobody else could hear him talk.

“Sit,” Red said.

She remained standing.

He said her name, slower, as if that would make a chair appear beneath her.

“Give me a cigarette,” Shawna told him.

He took one from a heavy silver box, lit it, and offered it like a man bringing flowers. She took it. Her hand was steady enough then.

“Could've killed him,” Red said.

“Tried,” she answered.

“Ran.”

“Coming back,” Shawna said. “My nigga's coming back.”

Red laughed and reached for a bottle.

“Always come back for money.”

“Put me outside,” she told him. “Find out.”

He poured too fast and spilled beside the glass. Then he wiped it with his own sleeve.

Shawna noticed. She said Red usually waited for somebody to cross a whole room and pick up something beside his shoe. Angry enough, he'd forgotten the job description he'd assigned himself.

“Think he loves you?” Red asked.

She smoked, looking at the horse.

“Asked you something.”

“Heard it,” she said.

“Loves winning,” Red told her. “You're what he stole. Wait until you're just a woman in his house.”

She kept her eyes on the painting. He came closer.

“Dante begged, you know,” Red said. “Begged us to look after you.”

Shawna turned.

“Us?” she asked.

The bottle stopped halfway back to the table.

“You were there?” she asked him.

“Didn't say that.”

“Who told you?”

Red put the bottle down carefully.

“Everybody knew.”

“I didn't,” Shawna said.

He looked toward the door. She watched him do it. When she told me, I asked if that meant he'd been there with T and Valentino. She said she still couldn't prove that part. He had claimed too much, then hidden inside what other people supposedly knew.

She wanted the old order in her hands. Wanted to lay it in his lap and make him touch the words. All she had was Dante's watch. She turned it around her wrist.

“What did he call me?” Shawna asked Red.

“What?”

“When he begged. Which name?”

“Your fucking name.”

“Which one?”

“Don't remember every—”

“Trouble,” Shawna interrupted. “Called me Trouble.”

Red stared at her.

“Miss Trouble when he was happy,” she said. “Mrs. Trouble when I was mad. Come home saying, ‘Mrs. Trouble, I fucked up,’ and I'd know to sit down before the rest.”

She laughed.

When she told me, she laughed again. Dante had borrowed a car for their honeymoon and brought its bumper upstairs after knocking it off. The whole bumper. Carried it in both hands because, apparently, leaving any of the damage behind would have been disrespectful.

“Like flowers,” Shawna told me from the couch. “Nigga carried it like flowers.”

I hated him a little for making her laugh from three years away. Wanted her to tell it again anyway. That's where I was, baby. Improving without becoming convenient.

Back in Red's white room, she'd wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist.

“Don't know how he talked to me,” Shawna told Red.

“Know what he was.”

“Talk about that, then. Your dead man. Don't get to talk about mine.”

He came close enough that she could smell his drink.

“Keep making him a saint,” Red said.

“Remembering my husband,” she answered. “Stole money. Lied badly. Left socks in the bed. Loved him. Think telling me he was bad changes something?”

She touched the watch again.

“Tell me something I don't know.”

Red pointed at her with his glass.

“Dante said the same things. House, different life, music. Buy you a microphone and call himself a husband.”

“Was my husband,” Shawna said.

“Paid for the wedding.”

“Paid for a room.”

“Paid for everything.”

“Should've kept the receipts,” she told him. “Maybe the preacher would've married you.”

Red threw the glass.

It broke against the wall beside the horse. Shawna didn't move. She'd been proud of that until she explained it to me: her legs had gone stiff. Courage wasn't the only reason somebody could stand still.

He came toward her.

“Used to know how to talk to me,” he said.

“Used to need you to stop.”

Red pushed her into the white chair, caught her face in one hand, and called a guard inside. Told him to take a picture.

That's the photograph they sent me. Shawna wasn't posing in a chair because she had run out of fight. He was holding her there while someone found the camera button.

She stared into the phone.

“He'll see you touching me,” she told Red.

“Point of it,” Red said.

“Know.”

The man took the picture and stepped away. Red's ring scraped her jaw as he released her.

She looked at his hand.

“Wearing it now?” Shawna asked.

His fist closed. “Wearing what?”

“All that money. Still need somebody to notice.”

He put his hand in his pocket.

***

A telephone rang outside the room. The guard at the door said a name, and Shawna saw Red's expression change.

“Frost?” she asked.

She knew the name from old penthouse conversations. Red used it whenever he wanted her to believe the city would become reasonable on request.

The guard whispered more. Red took the telephone so hard the man stepped backward.

“What do you mean arrested?” Red demanded.

When Shawna told me this part later, she made me listen twice. Said the first time my smile had interrupted my ears.

Red stood over the chair, listening.

“Knox said what?” he asked into the phone.

Shawna lifted her cigarette.

“My captain?” she asked.

Red covered the mouthpiece. “Shut up.”

“Wondering who finally made your policeman nervous,” she said.

He went into the hallway to finish the call. The door stayed open a few inches. The guard remained inside.

His name was Arlo. Thirty-eight, she told me, with gray beginning in his beard. She knew him too. Once he'd carried groceries upstairs when the elevator broke, then apologized for asking if she was all right because Red had heard him ask.

Arlo stood between her and the door.

“Don't have to stay in here,” Shawna told him.

He glanced toward the hallway. “Job.”

“Turning into a funeral,” she said.

He didn't answer.

She laid her cigarette across the lip of the ashtray and stood. He stepped toward her. She lifted one hand as though asking him to give her room, then reached past him with the other for the heavy silver cigarette box.

She threw it into his face.

Arlo hit the wall. Shawna ran through the door, past Red with the phone at his ear, and into the corridor before either man had caught up with the change in her attitude.

Red shouted her name.

She kept running.

The borrowed gown caught the corner of a table. She pulled until a strip of blue silk tore away. At the end of the corridor stood open service doors. Light shone from the stairwell. She could hear radios below and somebody crying.

She hit the door with her shoulder and nearly fell over a waiter sitting on the landing. He held his wrist. Blood had reached his white cuff. Beside him stood a young-looking woman who told Shawna she was twenty-four when Shawna asked if she could help lift him.

“Outside door?” Shawna asked her.

“Two floors. Won't let us past—”

“Get him up,” Shawna said.

They lifted him together. Shawna took his good side; the other woman supported the injured one without pulling his wrist. They started down, three people crowded into a staircase that had probably been designed for workers to move discreetly and never bleed on the paying guests.

Her dress dragged through dust and dropped flowers. The waiter tried to apologize for slowing them. She told him to save his breath for getting outside.

Red came through the service door above.

“Shawna!” he shouted.

She didn't look back.

“Want to help, get his fucking feet,” she called.

He caught them on the next landing. The woman supporting the waiter stopped, staring at him. Red pointed down the stairs.

“You two. Go,” he said.

Neither moved.

“Go!” Red shouted.

Shawna held the waiter until the woman had him steady. Then she let them pass. She watched them reach the lower door, watched the woman look back once, and waited until both disappeared through it.

Red grabbed her arm.

“Made me run,” he said.

“Try it more,” Shawna told him.

He pulled her toward him.

“Think you're Aiden now?”

She looked down at his hand, then into his face.

“Know exactly who I am. That's what's bothering you.”

He tried to haul her up the stairs. She planted her feet and struck the wrist holding her with the hard case of Dante's watch. Red swore and loosened his grip. She drove an elbow into his chest, got free, and ran toward the lower door.

Arlo stood in it.

One eye was swelling. Blood ran from his nose. He looked at her, then at Red behind her. She told me that for one second she thought he might step aside.

He raised his pistol instead.

She stopped.

Arlo couldn't look her in the face while he aimed it.

“Sorry,” he said.

“Get better at it,” Shawna told him.

Red pulled her back up the stairs. Her shoulder struck the rail. She bit the inside of her cheek because she didn't want him enjoying the noise she'd nearly made.

He put her into the white chair again.

The cigarette box lay on the floor where she'd thrown it. The patch of table it had occupied looked absurdly clean among the glass, blood, and spilled drink. She remembered that clean little rectangle clearly enough to describe it to me twice.

Red bent over her.

“One more fucking idea—” he began.

“Several,” Shawna interrupted. “Going to need a longer night.”

He stepped back.

Her hands were shaking now. She tucked them beneath her thighs so he wouldn't see. She had failed to get out. Two other people had succeeded. She held on to the difference while he paced.

When she told me, I asked what she'd been thinking on the stairs. She pushed her feet farther beneath my leg and gave me a look like the answer was written across my forehead.

“What my man would've done,” she said. “Better dress.”

I kissed her before I asked another question. Wasn't improving that answer.

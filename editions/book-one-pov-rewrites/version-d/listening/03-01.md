## 3. A King with a Hole in Him

At the hospital, a doctor asked Shawna who she was to me.

“His wife,” she said.

I heard that. I had missed pieces of the drive: whole stretches of road, questions I must have answered because she stopped asking them. My mother’s kitchen kept appearing where the dashboard ought to be. Then I would see the empty swimming pool again, and hear T calling me son, and want to tell him he had lost the fucking right.

But wife came through clean.

I tried to live harder when she said it. Whatever blood I had left in me received a promotion.

The doctor asked for a name. Shawna gave her own.

“His name,” the doctor repeated.

“Aiden,” Shawna said. “His name is Aiden.”

They had brought a bed alongside the one from the entrance. Hands reached beneath me to move me across, but Shawna had both fists caught in my shirt. My blood had dried brown over the front of her black dress. One earring was missing. Her bare feet stood on a floor polished enough to show her reflection, in a hospital that smelled of flowers and probably charged extra for letting them die naturally.

The doctor told her to let go.

I managed to say, “Baby.”

Shawna looked down at me. I wanted to tell her something that would stay if I didn't. Instead, I felt the chain against my neck and saw the scissors in somebody’s hand.

“Don't let them cut my chain,” I told Shawna, trying to lift my hand toward it.

She opened her fists. Her fingers shook while she lifted the gold over my head, but she did it herself. She kissed the cross, put it around her own neck, and stepped back just far enough for the waiting hands to take me.

The ceiling moved. Then it went away.

When it returned, a woman in a white coat stood beside the bed studying a chart. I had a tube in my arm, a dry mouth, and the impression that somebody had removed the middle of my body and returned it without consulting the instructions.

“Dr. Marisol Vega,” she said when she saw my eyes follow her. “You’re alive.”

I asked for Shawna. Vega pointed toward the window.

She was curled in a chair with my bloody shirt gathered against her stomach. Somebody had put hospital socks on her feet. The cross lay against her collarbone, and Dante’s watch had slipped down over the narrow part of her wrist. In the morning light, I could see the darkened skin beneath her eyes. At the Saint she had held her back straight even while she stood still. Asleep, she curled around the shirt. I wanted to keep the room quiet around her.

I told Vega not to wake her.

“She’ll wake herself,” the doctor said. “She has been doing it every eleven minutes.”

I tried to sit up. Pain drove through my side so hard the room lost its edges. I fell back against the pillow and said the only word I had ready.

“Fuck,” I said into the pillow.

“Yes,” Vega agreed. “That. You will be doing quite a lot of that if you continue.”

She waited until I stopped gripping the sheet, then explained what I needed to know. They had treated the damage. The bullet was still inside me; taking it out would create risks I didn't need. What I required now was time, which was inconvenient because I had never considered time a serious participant in my plans.

I stared at the ceiling. T was dead and had still found a way to move in with me.

“How much time?” I asked Vega.

“More than you want,” she said, holding one hand beside the bed until I stopped trying to rise.

I told her there were men waiting downstairs and a whole west side I couldn't run horizontally. She said they could keep waiting. She checked the dressing, looked at the monitor, and continued talking to me as if owning territory did not exempt a man from the ordinary problems of having organs.

I couldn't lift my head without making myself dizzy. Vega held the cup while I drank. Beyond her wrist I could see towers, whole floors of bright windows. I might have owned every one of them and still needed her to tilt the fucking straw.

I tried to take the cup from her. My hand slipped. Water hit the sheet; the cup struck the floor.

Shawna came out of the chair before it stopped rolling.

Shawna said my name and reached for me, touching my face, my shoulders, the hair above my ear. Her eyes passed over the bandages without stopping. I held still under her hands.

“Right here,” I told her.

“You scared me,” Shawna said, pressing her forehead briefly against mine.

I apologized. She kissed my forehead, slapped my arm, and told me not to be sorry—to stop doing it. Then she kissed me again. Her tears reached my mouth. I could taste them when I tried to speak.

Vega set a fresh cup within reach and left us alone.

I watched Shawna wipe her face with the back of her hand. The cross moved when she breathed.

“You told them you're my wife,” I said to Shawna.

“They weren't going to let me in,” she told me.

“Could've said sister,” I suggested, watching her wipe her cheek.

She looked at me over her wet knuckles. “I wanted them to let me in. I didn’t want to make it weird when I kissed you.”

I laughed and regretted it physically. The joke was excellent. My side was simply a hater.

“Say it again,” I asked her.

Shawna brushed water off my wrist. “Which part?”

“What you told the doctor. About being my wife.”

She sat on the edge of the bed, careful of the tubes.

“Nigga, you trying to die married?” Shawna asked.

“Trying to live that way,” I told her.

Her hand stopped against my cheek. Her eyes widened, and she didn't answer. I wondered whether the question frightened her. I was asking for a life before we had finished surviving the night that introduced us. I meant it. Meaning it didn't make it a small thing to ask.

She lowered her head onto the pillow beside mine.

“Let me get you home first,” Shawna whispered.

That afternoon, Tank appeared in the doorway with flowers.

The suit was fresh. His shoes had dirt around the soles. A bruise marked his forehead, and he held the bouquet out from his body with the uncertainty of a man who could lift an engine but hadn't been briefed on stems.

“These for her,” he said.

Shawna stood beside my bed. She didn't reach for them.

“Put the flowers down,” I told him, “before we both start feeling strange.”

He set them on the table by the door. I watched his hands come away empty. Those hands had held me while T tried to kill me. They had also gone for the car when Shawna told him to. I wanted to hit him again. I wanted him close. I wanted a man that size between this room and the people looking for us, and I couldn't yet tell whether he was one of them.

I asked whether he had come alone. He said the men downstairs were waiting. T’s men, or what had been T’s men. They wanted to know whose men they were now.

Before he answered that part, he glanced at Shawna.

“I’m not leaving,” she said.

“Didn't ask you to,” Tank told Shawna.

“You looked,” she said, keeping herself between his flowers and my bed.

Tank spread his empty hands. “I got eyes.”

I held out my hand to her. She took it without taking her eyes off him.

T had gone to the funeral people, Tank told me. Of the two other men at the pool, one was alive and one wasn't. Shawna looked down. I felt her fingers stiffen around mine and squeezed them gently. There would be time for what she needed to say about the man she had shot. I wasn't going to decide for her whether that meant relief, guilt, or a silence we sat through together.

She asked about the money.

Tank studied the foot of my bed before answering. “Pool was empty when I got back.”

“Three million doesn't walk,” Shawna told him.

“Plenty people got legs,” Tank answered, still watching the foot of the bed.

She let my hand go.

“Look at me when you say it,” Shawna told Tank.

He did. He told her he had looked, that people moved fast when nobody was watching, that the money wasn't there.

I believed him. I wanted the man who had brought the car to be the man I could keep, and I was lying in a bed without enough blood to investigate every wish I had.

“Leave it,” I said. “She threw it away to save me. It saved me. That’s money spent.”

Tank looked from me to the monitors.

“You always this rich?” Tank asked me.

“I woke up,” I told him.

He stayed quiet for a moment. Then he nodded. “Yeah. You did.”

Tank told me what was happening outside. Suppliers were waiting with questions. Men who owed the club money had started asking whether their debts died with T. Red wasn't answering calls from the west side, and T's office remained locked. I could imagine what that looked like to men I knew: respect for the dead until somebody found a way to make it pay.

T had announced my place publicly. Keeping it was another matter.

“You keeping me there?” I asked Tank.

He worked his jaw and reminded me that he had driven the car.

“That ain't what I asked,” I said to Tank. “I'm asking about now.”

Shawna moved to the window. Sunlight caught Dante’s watch. Tank looked at it longer than he needed to, then looked back at me.

“I ain't putting another man in the desert,” Tank told me.

He went downstairs to bring the others up. I watched the doorway after he had gone. It wasn't the promise I had asked for, but it was the one he had managed to give me.

Vega returned to find nine men in my room, two in the hallway, and a duffel bag occupying the visitor’s chair. Shawna had stepped into the adjoining bathroom. I could hear the tap running. The men stood around my bed. Holsters showed when their jackets opened, and their faces stayed blank in a way I had learned to be careful around.

“Absolutely not,” Vega said.

The older man holding a stack of papers stopped halfway through a sentence. Nobody else moved.

Vega pointed at the door. “Two visitors. The rest leave.”

They looked at me. I could feel the little test taking shape: whose authority counted when the woman in the white coat contradicted the men in expensive shoes?

“She said move,” I told them. “You want the woman who kept me alive having to step over you?”

They moved so fast the last two reached the doorway together. Each stopped to let the other through, then stepped forward again. Vega pointed once more. They abandoned manners and fitted themselves through sideways.

Tank stayed, along with the older man from T’s club. The papers came onto the bed beside my uninjured hip. Money would arrive Friday, he explained, but people needed paying today.

I asked what was in the bag.

“Yesterday,” the older man said, nodding toward the duffel.

Tank opened it. Cash reached the zipper. Less than a day before, I had been counting what I could spend on food. Now men were describing a fortune by the day of the fucking week.

I told the older man to pay the people who had worked. He wanted to know whether I meant from this money.

“Unless you got some magic beans,” I told the older man. “They worked. Pay them.”

He made a mark on the paper. His pen sounded absurdly small in the room.

Then he said Red wanted a meeting. Face to face. I told him Red could call, because if he looked at my face he might notice I was busy trying to keep it attached to the rest of my life.

The man swallowed. Tank closed the duffel.

For a moment they both waited. I had seen T sit silent until the men around him stopped talking and began looking at their hands. I didn't know how to be him. I was twenty-four, leaking under a dressing, with Shawna’s lip gloss on my forehead. All I knew was that the chair T had given me came with people attached to it, and I wasn't giving him the last word about how they were treated.

“Nobody touches Shawna,” I said. “Nobody says her name to Red. Anybody got a problem can leave the keys on the bed.”

The older man looked at Tank. Tank looked at me.

“You heard him,” Tank told the older man.

The keys stayed in the older man’s pocket.

After he left, I sank back against the pillows. The conversation had emptied me. I could feel dampness beneath the dressing and couldn't hide the effort it took to draw a full breath.

Tank moved the bag closer and offered to count it.

“I want you watching that door like your brother in here,” I told Tank.

“Who am I watching for?” he asked, his hand on the duffel.

“Every fucking nigga,” I answered.

Before carrying the chair into the hall, Tank set a bundle of clean clothes on the low table beside my bed and laid my pistol across them.

“Floor of your car,” he told me. “Nearly stepped on it.”

“Car's got features,” I said.

He looked at me for a moment, then took the chair outside. That was the first night he guarded me.

Before it got dark, I heard a woman beyond the door. Then I saw the red velvet coat, the bags, and a face I would come to associate with some of Shawna's oldest stories.

“Where the fuck is she?” a woman shouted outside my door. A moment later I would learn her name was Rosa.

Shawna said her name and crossed the room. Rosa dropped what she was carrying and caught her. They held each other in the narrow space between my bed and the window while Tank quietly brought the abandoned bags inside.

Rosa had copper hair and a broken-heart tattoo beneath her ear. I came to know the rest through the two women in that room: she was thirty-four, sang at the Saint on weekends, and took paying men upstairs when the singing didn't cover what life demanded. She had known Shawna when the suitcase from Phoenix still had its tags. Watching them hold each other, I was seeing a piece of Shawna's life I had only begun to hear about.

“Why didn't you answer your fucking phone?” Rosa demanded into Shawna's hair.

“Broke it,” Shawna said.

“Borrow one. Send a pigeon. Don't let some nigga in a rhinestone hat be the one telling me you're dead.” Rosa tightened her arms around her.

“I’m here,” Shawna said.

“I see you,” Rosa told Shawna. “I'm mad anyway.”

Rosa held her at arm’s length and checked her face. Only then did she turn toward me. She asked whether I was the one who had shot T.

I said yes.

“Good,” Rosa said.

She came to the bed and kissed my forehead, putting another mark over the one Shawna had left.

“Stay alive,” Rosa told me. “She doesn't have another black dress in her.”

Shawna’s face crumpled. Rosa turned back and brought her close before I could see the tears properly.

The duffel sat beside me, full of answers to questions nobody in that room was asking. I pushed it farther down the bed so Rosa could sit beside Shawna.

Rosa unpacked clothes, food, and an old songbook. She told us she had kept it after Dante died. Shawna opened it in the middle, looked down at the page, and shut it fast enough for the paper to snap.

“Later,” Shawna said, keeping her hand on the cover.

Rosa put it on the table where she could reach it. “Whenever.”

That evening Rosa painted Shawna’s nails by the window. Tank ate in the hallway, where I could hear him negotiating with a plastic fork. More money came from downstairs, and I told them to leave it there. My room held two women talking softly, the smell of real food, and a man outside who kept shifting and making the chair creak but hadn't left it. I wanted to keep that arrangement for a while.

Later, Shawna fell asleep against me. Her hand rested near the bandage without touching it.

She whispered Dante’s name.

I heard it. Of course I heard it. I had been listening to every breath she took.

I lay still until morning. I didn't know yet how to make room for a man who was gone without feeling him take space from me. The only decent thing I knew to do was let her sleep.

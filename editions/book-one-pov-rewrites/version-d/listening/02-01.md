## 2. First Night in Vegas

The night I met Shawna, cocaine had settled in the fold of my collar and somebody else’s blood had worked its way inside my watch. It had dried beneath the rim of the glass. Every time I checked the hour, there it was: a little red reminder that somewhere in this city I either owed a man an apology or needed to finish the job.

I was twenty-four, and I thought damage was a way of keeping score.

My last car had burned outside my ex-boyfriend’s apartment. He told me that meant I wanted him back. I had reached the point in my life where even my insurance claims were being interpreted romantically. That afternoon I had eaten a gas-station sandwich in a borrowed car. Before the sun came up again, I would be carrying three million dollars, running with a woman half of Vegas wanted delivered to them, and bleeding around a bullet I would keep for the rest of my life.

But all that was still ahead of me. Behind the nightclub, under the yellow security light, twelve men were preparing to beat my ass, and I wanted them to like me when they finished.

Big T had walked me out with his hand on my shoulder. He kept it there while the door closed and the music became a deep thump on the other side of the brick. The rear lot smelled of hot engines and spilled beer. Cars stood at angles around an open patch of concrete. Men leaned against the hoods or rolled their shoulders beside the doors, blue rags showing from pockets, chains flashing when they moved beneath the light.

I knew some of them. Two had borrowed money from me. One still owed me ten dollars and had apparently decided paying it back would be easier if I lost the ability to remember numbers.

T drew me into the middle, his fingers pressing through my shirt.

“Once a nigga in, that nigga in,” he told me. “You understand what I'm saying?”

“I understand,” I told him.

T kept his hand on my shoulder. “Summer ends. Somebody drops. Suddenly a nigga got somewhere else to be. That you?”

“I got nowhere else to be,” I said. “That’s why I’m standing here instead of enjoying the fucking music.”

A laugh escaped from behind a Cadillac. T didn’t join it.

“This funny to you?” he asked.

I looked around the ring of men assembled to determine my sincerity through orthopedic damage.

“I think twelve is a committee,” I told T. “Which one of you takes the minutes?”

T drove me backward into the Cadillac. My spine hit the edge of the hood and the breath jumped out of me.

That answered it. T took the minutes.

The other eleven came in with amendments.

A fist split my lip. Something hard caught behind my ear before I had turned toward the first blow. I tried to get my elbows in, but a kick knocked my leg out and I hit the ground on my palms. Grit bit into the skin. Shoes filled the little space I could see beneath the cars.

I got up because I had come there to get up. That was about the size of my thinking at twenty-four. I had spent too long being somebody’s temporary guest, somebody’s good time, somebody’s useful man until the useful part was over. T was offering me a place where my name would stay after I left the room. I was willing to pay for that place with whatever these motherfuckers could reach.

Blood ran into one eye. I swung toward a shoulder with the eye that still worked, felt my knuckles connect, and heard somebody tell me to stay down.

“Y’all just told me to stay IN,” I shouted. “Pick an instruction!”

Then Tank hit me in the ribs.

He was twenty-eight—I learned that later—almost twice my size, and broad enough that his shirt had given up pretending the buttons were a permanent arrangement. The punch bent me around his arm. I caught a fistful of that failing shirt before he could draw back and drove with my legs, taking him into the next car.

The alarm went off beneath us.

“Not the paint!” somebody yelled.

I hit Tank again. “You should’ve thought about the paint before you started throwing people at it!”

He tried to turn me off his chest, but I was too close and too angry to assist him. I kept my fist in his shirt and used the other hand wherever I could find room. He called for somebody to get me off him. T laughed. The others watched. Through the alarm I heard Tank shout T’s name. He kept looking past my shoulder toward him while nobody moved to help. I wasn't the only nigga finding the induction process disappointing.

“You said he was little,” T reminded him.

Tank caught another blow on his forearm. “Nigga, I have new information!”

T finally hauled me away by the shoulders. I stood in the open with my fists still up. My legs shook. My mouth tasted of pennies and whiskey, and I couldn't uncurl my fingers even after the men stopped coming.

“Enough,” T said. He grinned as he looked around at them. “Twelve niggas. Look at him. Still shopping.”

I told him I was done. When he asked whether I was sure, I looked at the fists I was apparently still offering to the room.

“I’m waiting for my hands to hear it,” I told him.

T laughed, and several of the others joined him. Then he took a gold chain out of his pocket, opened it wide, and lowered it over my head. I bowed automatically to let him. A small cross dropped against the sore place above my ribs.

The men went quiet before I did.

“My brother wore that,” T said. “Real nigga.”

I lifted the cross. It was warm from his pocket. I asked where his brother was so I could thank him.

“Dead,” T said.

The car alarm chose that moment to stop. I heard T draw a breath through his nose, and because he was so close I could see the dampness at the edge of his eye.

“Don’t make me bury you too,” T told me, keeping one hand on my shoulder.

A minute earlier, he had been hitting me. Now he had found a way to make me stand there trying not to cry. I closed my hand over the gold as if accepting it required me to keep it warm.

“Not this nigga,” I promised.

T kissed my forehead. Then he turned me toward the club and called for the men to make room for his son.

I remember believing him. I won't improve the story by pretending I was cleverer than I was. He had hurt me, stopped the hurting, and called the space after it love. At the time, that felt enough like a home for me to walk through the door.

Tank held it open. Out of some bruised instinct toward manners, I reached to help him. He moved his shoulder away from my hand.

“Walk through, nigga,” Tank said, holding the door away from my hand. “I can still defeat a door.”

Inside, blue light lay across the smoke. The back room was warmer than the lot, crowded around a glass table buried in money and white bricks. Men separated bills into stacks. Bottles of Hennessy stood wherever the cash left room. Three women in diamonds drank beside a man with two pistols tucked into one belt, as if his trousers had enemies on both sides.

One of the women looked at my face and asked whether I had survived a war.

“Yeah,” I said, taking the chair somebody pushed toward me. “And I won.”

They cheered. A bottle arrived in one hand, a burning blunt in the other. I drank, smoked, and wiped my mouth against my wrist before I remembered what was already on the watch. The pile of cash beside my elbow was bigger than any amount I had ever been trusted to sit near.

The woman wanted to know if I was the one who had beaten Tank. T said I was. She folded a thousand-dollar bill into my shirt pocket. Ninety seconds back inside and somebody was paying me to be handsome with a broken face. I began to think the committee’s compensation package deserved another look.

Tank came in holding ice against his jaw. He took the bottle from me, drank, and gave it back. I waited to find out whether the next thing between us would be another punch.

Tank pressed the ice against his jaw. “Who taught you to fight dirty like that?”

“Same nigga taught you to fight unconscious,” I told him, keeping the bottle ready in case this resumed.

The room broke open. Tank stared at me while everybody else laughed. Then his face changed. He began laughing too, blood showing between his teeth, and dragged a chair up beside mine.

We drank out of the same bottle. I passed it over; he passed it back. I stopped watching his free hand quite so closely. Before the ice against his jaw had melted, I would have handed him my heart to hold while I fought with both hands.

T tossed a set of keys across the table. I caught them against the bottle.

“Black Mercedes,” he said. “That’s yours.”

I turned the keys over, feeling the little badge beneath my thumb. An hour earlier, I had wanted a place in his crew. Now he was telling me the west side needed a man and the west side had me.

I asked whether he was serious. He nodded toward the keys, as if there was no point discussing an answer already in my hand.

I sat taller. My ribs objected. I ignored them. The woman’s thousand dollars crackled in my pocket whenever I moved, adding its own applause.

Then T showed me a photograph on his phone.

The woman in it stood beside a pool at night. The Strip burned behind her. She wore black, diamonds at her ears, dark hair over one shoulder. She wasn't smiling for whoever held the camera. I looked at her face and forgot to admire the city.

“Shawna,” T said. “Twenty-six. Works for Red.”

I asked what sort of work. Whatever Red wanted, he told me, and moved on before I could decide how much I disliked the answer. She would be at the Saint, in the penthouse. At eleven she would bring down a case. I was to take her and the money to T’s house in Henderson.

Across the table, Tank watched me looking at the photograph.

“Why's she leaving Red?” I asked.

“Finally got smart,” T said.

“Red know about this little family pickup?”

T looked at me across the photograph. “If he knew, we wouldn't be having the conversation.”

Then he took the back of my neck and drew me close enough that his next words were only for me.

“Don't fall in love with her,” T said close to my ear.

I laughed into his face. He kept his hand on my neck until I looked at him properly.

“Laugh when the job's done,” T told me. “I'm serious.”

“Look at me, nigga,” I said. “Do I look like the nigga falls in love?”

He released my neck. I sat back with the keys and believed every word I had just said. I had managed to become wrong about my whole future before I had even learned the radio in my new car.

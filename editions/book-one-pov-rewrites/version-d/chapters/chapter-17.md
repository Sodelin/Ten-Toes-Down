## 17. Ten Toes Down Forever

They put Tank in a private room facing the mountains. I paid for the room next door for Rosa because she wouldn't leave him.

“Here for Shawna,” she told me.

Tank looked over from his pillows.

“Tell Shawna I'm doing better.”

“Shut up,” Rosa said.

“Tell Shawna you called me handsome.”

“You were bleeding. Keeping you interested.”

Tank turned toward me. “Write that down. She's interested.”

Rosa adjusted his blanket without looking at either of us. So gently, neither of us was stupid enough to mention it. There are men who've survived gunfire just to die discussing the wrong tenderness in front of a woman.

I bought him a black Cadillac. Sent a photograph while they brought it over.

My phone rang almost immediately.

“Buying me a car or showing me a car?” Tank asked.

“Picture wasn't good?”

“Can't see the color properly.”

It was black. Nighttime and a color named after nighttime. My nigga had found a complaint anyway.

I bought a second one and had both parked beneath his window. Called him from the lot.

“Look outside.”

I heard Rosa opening the blinds. Then Tank laughed, stopped because his shoulder hurt, and made the same bad decision again.

“Two?” he asked.

“Whichever side of the hospital you recover on.”

Rosa took the phone.

“Needs to learn to walk, Aiden.”

“Why I put them outside.”

Tank asked which one she preferred. Rosa called him an idiot, but I could see her still standing at the window, choosing. He sounded more nervous asking about the left Cadillac than I'd ever heard him before a fight.

I sat in my car a while after hanging up. Through the window, I could see Rosa beside his bed. He was alive enough to be greedy. She was there enough to complain about it.

I could live with that kind of hospital bill.

***

Shawna and I went back to the lake house three days after Red died.

There were flowers at the gate, flowers on the steps, flowers in the bedroom. Men I'd never met sent bottles and watches. Women sent perfume, silk, diamonds. Somebody sent an actual white horse with a blue ribbon in its mane.

A living one. Breathing. Had opinions about the man trying to lead it around the drive. After everything we'd done to Leon's property, I checked twice that it wasn't another statue waiting to fall into a car.

We had it taken around back with its handler, where Shawna could see it from the bedroom balcony. It walked beside the water, lowering its head to investigate grass I'd never once considered eating.

Shawna stood at the window.

“You buy that?” she asked.

“Somebody sent it.”

“Why?”

“Know who you are.”

She watched it a moment.

“Don't know how to ride.”

“We'll learn.”

She turned toward me. For a second I thought she was going to cry again.

Then she laughed.

I had been hearing gunfire in my sleep. T calling me son. Red saying she'd have gotten into a car with anybody. I'd begun to dread what my head would say when the room got quiet.

Then my girl laughed at the horse and came across to kiss me.

I picked her up. She wrapped her arms around my neck, and I carried her away from the window as the mountains took the sun. My side hurt. Didn't put her down until we reached the bed. Had carried worse for men who loved me less.

Later, we lay awake with the balcony door open, listening to the water.

Her watch rested on the nightstand.

I looked at it long enough that she noticed.

“Want me to put it away?” she asked.

I didn't answer immediately.

I wanted everything. Her mornings. Her nights. The face she made when somebody said my name. I wanted to be the man she thought of when every song began. Wanted to reach back three years and carry the cake into that club myself. Stand in every photograph that had made her happy.

I couldn't. I had bought half a city and couldn't buy one night three years earlier.

“No,” I told her.

She laid her head on my chest.

“Sometimes I miss him when I'm happy.”

I looked at the ceiling.

“With me?”

“Yeah.”

That hurt. I won't make myself sound wiser than I was. Went straight through me.

Shawna lifted her head.

“Wish he knew I wasn't hurting all the time anymore,” she said.

I looked at her then.

She touched the cross against my chest.

“Wish he knew somebody stayed.”

I took her hand and held it there. We listened to the lake for a long time without finding anything else that needed saying.

Then I asked her to marry me.

No shirt. No ring ready. Her husband's watch beside the bed where we could both see it. My mouth finally doing something useful before my money got involved.

She sat up.

“Aiden.”

“Mean it.”

“Just bought me a house.”

“Buy another.”

She looked at me. “Not what I'm saying.”

“Tell me, then.”

Her eyes filled.

“Can't fix every bad thing that happened to me,” Shawna said.

“Know.”

“Going to be angry when you find that out for real?”

I sat up beside her, moving slowly around the wound.

“Going to be angry about plenty. I'm a fucked-up man, baby. You know.”

She nodded. Couldn't even let me finish before agreeing. Sensible woman.

“Asking you to marry me,” I said. “Bring the watch. Bring the pictures. Bring every fucking thing you can't leave behind. Come with me.”

She put her palm over my mouth.

Then she started laughing and crying together.

“Yes,” she said.

I kissed her palm.

“Say it again.”

“Yes. I'll marry you.”

I had armed men outside, more money than I'd ever imagined, cars arriving when I complained about photographs. None of it had made me feel like that word.

***

We married beside the lake three weeks after I walked into the Saint and saw her across the room.

Blue roses and the gardenias Rosa had warned me about covered the aisle. The flowers cost more than every rent payment I'd ever made put together. Every table had a bottle of Hennessy; every man at the gate had a gun. Men from other cities came in expensive suits to congratulate me and decide whether the stories were true.

True enough that nobody tried me.

A woman sang the song Shawna used to sing in her mother's kitchen. I'd paid her more than she asked, paid her band, and bought the place where she'd been performing after Shawna told me its owner had once treated her like shit when she came for an audition.

The former owner tried to come in with the musicians.

“Used to employ them,” he told the man at my gate.

I was close enough to hear. Turned and pointed toward the parking lot.

“Enjoy the sound from there.”

He looked at the flowers, the tables, the guards.

“Guest?” he asked.

“Listener,” I told him.

Then the music changed, and I forgot to care whether he'd enjoyed my hospitality.

Shawna came down the aisle with Rosa on her arm.

White dress. Diamonds at her throat. Dante's watch on her wrist. Rosa wore blue and held her as carefully as she'd held pressure on Tank's wound. They reached me, and Rosa put Shawna's hand in mine before kissing her cheek.

“Here,” Rosa told me quietly. “Keep doing it.”

Then she went to the front row.

Tank stood beside me in a black suit, his arm still healing beneath the jacket. He watched Rosa sit down, then noticed me noticing and became very interested in the preacher.

The preacher asked if I would love Shawna in sickness and health.

I looked at the scar above her eyebrow. The fading mark on her mouth. The wrist she'd pressed against my bleeding side when all I had to give her was a promise about water and a house.

“I will,” I said.

The preacher asked her.

“I will,” Shawna answered.

Her hand shook inside mine. Tank wiped his eyes with the sleeve on his good arm. Rosa cried into a napkin she'd stolen from his table. My girl said yes in front of the whole city, and when I kissed my wife, the band started and the guests shouted loud enough to frighten the horse.

I had not hired the horse for ceremonial participation. It survived the assignment anyway.

***

The wedding presents came in a convoy longer than the one we'd taken to the desert.

Knox brought gold-plated handcuffs in a velvet box. She handed them to Shawna.

“Trust you to decide when they're necessary,” Knox told her.

Shawna lifted the cuffs, looked at me, and smiled.

Tank covered his eyes.

“Trying to eat,” he complained.

“Look at your plate,” Rosa told him.

Professor Halcyon came with Principal Mercer and six graduates who'd helped carry people from the auditorium. They brought a framed photograph of my Lamborghini coming through the doors with fire behind it.

Mercer had signed the bottom in gold: *Our valedictorian.*

“Signed it like you made me,” I told him.

He began crying again. Shawna took the frame before either of us dropped it.

“Know exactly where this goes,” she said.

“Over the bed?”

“Fireplace. Think enough of yourself in bed.”

Mercedes screamed laughing. Porsche nearly lost her drink. Mercer nodded gravely, as though he respected the academic distinction.

Rex arrived in a white limousine full of fighters. He introduced every one of them by explaining how fast I'd put him down. The time got shorter with each telling, and he got prouder of having been present for it.

“One punch,” Rex told the last man. “Before I got comfortable.”

“Weren't comfortable when you arrived,” I said.

“What I'm telling him. Never stood a chance.”

The fighter looked at me, then Shawna, and bowed his head.

“Congratulations, Captain.”

Rex clapped his shoulder. “See? Learns fast.”

Little Saint brought a dark blue motorcycle with my initials on the tank. He wheeled it into the drive and told me it was for days four wheels felt like too much company.

Tank came closer, inspecting it with his good hand tucked into his pocket.

“No day in his life needs fewer wheels,” Tank said.

“Jealous?” Saint asked.

“Watched him drive. I'm informed.”

Then Leon arrived with a bicycle.

Green. Little basket on the front. He pushed it through my gate in a tuxedo and looked directly at Porsche.

“This one floats,” he said.

She kissed his cheek.

“So did the first car,” Porsche told him. “For a minute.”

Leon shut his eyes while everybody laughed. When he opened them, he looked at the house, the band, the lights laid across the lake.

“Wouldn't have missed any of it,” he said.

Neither would I.

I would've made some adjustments to the hospital portion. But I wasn't trading the people for an easier route to the party.

***

For our first dance, Shawna wanted a song she could hear without remembering a room another man had paid for.

So we made one.

She'd started the melody during the week before the wedding. I found her at the piano after midnight, barefoot, chewing a pencil and arguing with the bridge. She said it kept sounding like goodbye.

I sat beside her and played the left hand while she sang.

“There,” I said when her voice rose.

She stopped. “Where?”

“Don't turn back. Keep going.”

She tried it again. The note opened instead of folding down. Her face changed.

“Baby,” she said.

“That's us.”

We stayed until morning. Papers on the floor. Cold drinks untouched. Her shoulder against mine whenever we went back to the beginning. The song became a woman hearing a car in the driveway and knowing the person she wanted was finally coming inside.

No one had to leave in the last verse.

Now the band played it beside the lake, and my wife put her hand in mine.

The first few steps were easy. Then she leaned farther into the music and I followed her, taking the turn slowly enough to keep my wound from announcing itself to the guests. She laughed at the concentration on my face.

“Know how to dance, don't you?” Shawna asked.

“Know how to dance with you. Different subject. Higher degree.”

She leaned back in my arm. I caught her, turned, brought her close. The wedding cheered. My side complained privately. I respected its discretion and continued.

Rosa stood first. Tank started to follow, remembered his shoulder, and stopped halfway out of his chair. She went to him instead, took his good hand, and brought him to the edge of the floor.

“Don't dance,” Tank told her.

“Tonight you do.”

“Rosa—”

“Got shot for these people,” she said. “Survive four steps with me.”

He survived them. Badly at first. Better when he looked at her instead of his shoes.

I watched him discover that standing still and being enormous wasn't the whole subject of romance. He looked toward me once, embarrassed, smiling so hard he couldn't hide it.

I nodded. Wasn't going to make him say anything either.

At the last chorus, fireworks opened above the water. Blue and gold spread across the lake. Guests turned toward them. Shawna kept looking at me.

I touched my forehead to hers.

“Missing the expensive part,” I told her.

“No, baby.”

She touched the cross.

“Looking right at him.”

Didn't have a single smart thing left in my mouth. She kissed me before I could find one.

***

After that, the schedule lost control of the wedding.

We went inside. Drinks kept appearing. There was cocaine in the upstairs bathroom, cash changing hands on the terrace, sex workers dancing with men who had brought other women and were about to learn what surveillance actually meant. Somebody drove a convertible onto the lawn.

I asked why.

The driver pointed toward the house.

“Parking's better here.”

Had a point. Terrible point, but it occupied the evidence.

Tank fell asleep with his good arm around Rosa. She threatened to kill the first person who photographed them. I saw two people slowly lower their phones and one woman pretend she'd been reading a message directly at Tank's face.

The next morning, I bought every photograph I could find.

Kept one.

Tank still insists he was awake. Rosa has the same picture beside her bed. Neither has yet explained why an awake man was drooling on a woman who supposedly came to the hospital for Shawna.

I don't press. A king needs hobbies that let other people remain alive.

***

Red dying didn't make Monday cancel itself.

Calls came. Money came. The west side still wanted decisions. Somebody in another city thought a dead man meant an available business. Someone else's brother still wanted blood. I wore a wedding ring and answered the same ugly telephone.

I hadn't become good by marrying her. Still had a temper, still had a gun, still woke with my hand over the place T had shot me. Still wanted too much.

But I knew where I was bringing it home.

One night that week, after handling business, I found Shawna in the room we'd made for her music. Only the little lamp beside the piano was on. Papers covered the floor. A half-finished drink stood on the windowsill. She sang something I hadn't heard.

I stayed in the doorway until she finished.

She turned and found me.

“How long?” she asked.

“Long enough.”

“Like it?”

“Yeah.”

“Always say that.”

“Always mean it.”

She held out her hand. I went to her.

Outside, men guarded the gates and the city waited for my next decision. She pulled me onto the bench and put my hand against her heart.

“Stay a minute,” she said.

I stayed all night.

That's what I mean when I tell you who you're riding with. I didn't come out of the desert clean. Came out with Shawna beside me and Tank in an ambulance, asking for cars as long as he could still form a request. Gave away the most expensive diamond I'd ever held. Took the power Red thought he'd be keeping. Kept a dead man's cross because remembering him didn't mean I had to disappear.

When she woke crying, I was there. When Tank called, I answered. A man could stand in a kingdom, or he could stand beside a hospital bed waiting for somebody to open his eyes. I'd done both. The bed taught me what the kingdom couldn't buy.

Shawna had stayed when leaving would've been easier. One hand on the wheel, one hand keeping me alive. Now I had a home, and staying was the first useful thing I knew to do with it.

Ten toes down. That's all those words had ever been worth if I couldn't bring them through my own front door.

***

The invitation arrived the morning we were packing for the honeymoon.

Blue envelope. Silver seal. It lay on the hood of my police Lamborghini when I came outside carrying Shawna's suitcase and my smaller bag. Nobody at the gate had reported delivering it.

Tank pointed at the envelope.

“Another college, I'm taking the test,” he said.

“Start with the envelope.”

He picked it up.

“Sky High.”

“Club?” I asked.

“School.”

“Just graduated. Got a return policy?”

Shawna came down with Rosa on her arm. Mercedes and Porsche stood by the cars arguing over luggage. Mercedes had brought enough to make me check whether somebody had sold her apartment.

“Their honeymoon,” Porsche told her.

“Aware.”

“Four bags?”

“Aware it's their honeymoon,” Mercedes repeated. “Need my own activities.”

Marcellus stood near the white horse, feeding it something the trainer had approved. Blue Mercy was safe in a proper case. He'd finally stopped carrying it from one room to another as though the house might steal it while he slept.

He let the horse finish chewing before looking over.

“Trouble?” Marcellus asked.

“Education,” Tank said.

“Expensive trouble, then.”

I set the bags on the drive and opened the envelope. A photograph slid out.

Campus. Lawns. Football field. Towers. All above the clouds, on a huge piece of ground with no apparent intention of rejoining the rest of the planet.

Tank bent over it.

“That's up,” he said.

“See it.”

“No. Up up. Our school had steps. These people got weather.”

Rosa took the photograph.

“What's underneath?” she asked.

“Lawsuit if somebody drops keys,” Mercedes said.

The letter came from Dean Seraphine Storm. An advanced adult hero program, every incoming student at least twenty-two. She'd seen footage of the graduation, the hospital rescue, the convoy, and enough other material to believe I'd been taking a long route to the appropriate school.

Wanted me after the honeymoon.

Wanted my people too.

I read that line again. My people. A dean who understood the application came with attachments.

“All of us?” Tank asked.

I handed him the page. He read it, then looked at Rosa while trying to make the looking appear accidental.

Rosa pointed at the photograph.

“Rooms?” she asked.

“Whole residences,” Shawna said, reading the next sheet.

“Piano?” I asked.

“Conservatory.”

“More piano?”

She smiled. “Yes, baby. More piano.”

Porsche took off her sunglasses.

“Cars?”

I found the line on the last page.

“Your automobile may accompany you, Captain,” I read aloud. “We would prefer that its first landing be supervised.”

Tank nodded gravely.

“Know you personally.”

I started smiling. Shawna saw it and held up one finger.

“Honeymoon first.”

“Absolutely.”

“Real honeymoon. Bed touches a floor. Floor touches the fucking earth.”

“Whatever my wife wants.”

“When I say beach, I mean beach,” she continued. “You will not enroll the sand in college.”

“Baby, if the sand tests well—”

She held out her hand for the keys.

I gave them to her. Marriage was going beautifully. Clear expectations. Prompt consequences.

Tank returned the letter while Rosa handed me the photograph.

“Think you can be best there too?” he asked.

I folded the pages beside my badge. The cross settled against my chest.

“Tank.”

“What?”

“Invited me. They know.”

Shawna opened the Lamborghini.

“Get in, valedictorian,” she said. “Driving before somebody gives you a planet.”

I put the luggage away and climbed in beside her. My people were still arguing over bags. My wife started the engine. A school in the sky wanted the entire unreasonable collection of us.

I had a wife, a badge, a kingdom, and somewhere new to be magnificent.

And she still made me carry the bags.

## 10. Forty Million and One Woman

The morning after I graduated, four men robbed a casino while I was buying coffee in uniform.

That was poor timing. Parking their van across my Lamborghini's bumper was personal.

I stood outside the coffee shop holding two cups. Shawna waited in the passenger seat with sunglasses on and my captain's jacket over her dress. She'd borrowed it because, she told me, the gold braid felt good against her wrists. I'd moved the badge to my shirt before handing it over. The department hadn't owned me long enough to begin inventorying what she'd taken.

Across the street, an alarm sounded. Four men came out of the casino carrying bags. One kept a pistol pointed back toward the doors. Another had a split lip. The biggest carried a bag in each hand and kept looking toward their van, which occupied the space my car needed if I intended to go anywhere with the coffee.

They crossed the road between stopped cars, the gunman still pointing back at the casino. The man who turned out to be the driver hurried ahead and opened the rear doors. I looked at him, then at the cups, then at Shawna.

“Hold these,” I told her.

She took both through the open window. “Getting involved?”

I stepped away from the car. “Involved me when they parked.”

The driver stood by the open back of the van, waving his men toward it. He saw me coming and laughed.

“Keep walking, pretty boy,” the driver said.

I turned so he could see the badge on my uniform shirt. His eyes went to it. The laugh stopped coming out of his mouth and stayed there as an unhelpful shape.

“Police?” the driver asked.

I looked past him at my blocked bumper. “Captain.”

He looked at my face again. “Don't look like police.”

I pointed at the van. “Police improving. Move it.”

The armed man had a short beard and a pistol coming up toward my chest. I caught the open rear door and drove its edge into his forearm. The shot struck the street. I shoved the door harder, pinning his wrist against the side panel until his fingers opened and the pistol fell beneath the van. He dropped to one knee, clutching his arm.

The driver ran for the front. The big man tossed his bags inside, grabbed the bearded gunman beneath his arms, and hauled him through the back of the van. The man with the split lip climbed in after them. All four were aboard when the engine rose and the van lurched forward, its rear doors still open.

A money bag rolled out and burst against the curb. The split-lip man reached for another one sliding after it. I caught the rear door handle as the van moved past me.

“Aiden!” Shawna shouted from the car.

I looked back once. “Coffee!”

I wasn't going to let these men ruin eight dollars of breakfast and a police jacket before I'd properly worn either one out.

The van pulled me around the corner. My shoes struck the curb, found purchase for one step, then slipped. I kept hold of the handle with both hands, got a foot onto the rear step, and hauled my knee up beside it.

The man with the split lip kicked toward my face. I ducked under his shoe and caught the doorframe.

“Let go!” the split-lip man shouted.

I pulled myself high enough to meet his eyes. “You let go.”

He kicked again. “It's our van!”

I caught his ankle. “Should've moved it.”

I came through the opening shoulder first, driving him backward into the money bags. The bearded gunman sat against the side panel holding the arm I'd struck. The biggest man crouched among the bags, trying to keep them from rolling out around us.

The driver looked back at the noise. The van climbed halfway onto the sidewalk. A newspaper stand burst against the windshield, and pages flew past the open rear doors. I saw my own graduation photograph turn over in the air before the paper went beneath a wheel.

Yesterday's miracle was having trouble with this morning's circulation.

The split-lip man reached toward his waistband. I put my foot on his wrist as his pistol came clear, grinding his hand against the floor until he let it fall. With one hand braced against a shelf, I drew my own gun.

“Don't,” I told him.

He looked up. His eyes moved over my face properly for the first time.

“You're Aiden,” the split-lip man said.

I kept my weight on his wrist. “Easier day knowing that at the coffee shop.”

The big man raised both hands when I looked toward him. The bearded man kept cradling the arm I'd crushed. I kicked the second pistol beneath the front seat before lifting my foot off its owner's wrist. He tucked that hand against his chest and left it there as the van turned.

Concrete closed over the daylight. The driver had taken us into a parking structure. Tires squealed on the first ramp. Through the open doors I could see a central well between two sets of ramps, cars parked around the outer edge, arrows on the floor going in opposite directions.

Then I heard my Lamborghini.

Shawna came through the entrance behind us with the siren on. She sat forward over the wheel, sunglasses still in place. I held the van's shelf with one hand and watched the blue nose appear at the foot of the ramp as though the garage had ordered something beautiful to correct its morning.

We reached the second floor. The driver took the outer lane toward the next ramp, glancing back through the open van at every turn.

“Still behind us!” the driver shouted.

I watched Shawna take the turn below. “She's thorough.”

The driver faced forward again. “Who asked you?”

I tightened my hold as the van tipped into another turn. “Brought me.”

Shawna cut across the second floor toward the inner ramp. Through the open well, I saw the Lamborghini climbing against the arrows while our van followed the longer loop around the parked cars. She disappeared behind a pillar. When we reached the third floor, the blue car came off the neighboring ramp and swung across our lane.

The driver hit the brakes.

The van slid. I caught the passenger seat with my free hand and kept the pistol pointed away from the three men tumbling into the money bags. They landed in a heap. It sounded financially painful.

Through the windshield, I saw Shawna get out of the Lamborghini with her silver pistol. She stood beside the door, keeping the car between herself and the van's driver.

“Everybody out,” Shawna called.

The driver stared at her. “Police too?”

I looked through the windshield at my woman. “Worse. Disappointed.”

He raised both hands.

We got all four men onto the concrete. I watched the driver while Shawna covered the others. She told me she'd called for officers on the way into the garage. Ten minutes later, Knox's people came up the ramp to find the money in the van and my Lamborghini across the lane. I pointed an officer toward the pistol under the front seat and told him where the first one had fallen, back at the coffee shop.

Once the officers had all four men in cuffs, I put my pistol away. A sergeant with a gray mustache shook my hand. I asked him to leave enough of it functioning for the coffee.

“Been after this crew for months, Captain,” the sergeant told me.

I looked at the van. “Parked wrong.”

He studied its position, then my car. “Should've tried that.”

Shawna put her silver pistol away and brought both coffees over. Mine was still warm. I looked inside the Lamborghini: dry cup holders, my jacket spread over the passenger seat. She'd set both drinks down before changing seats, she'd told me, then remembered the jacket and lifted it clear. Not a drop. She'd chased a van full of robbers and preserved breakfast while doing it. Every useful thought I'd ever had about marriage stood up to applaud.

The sergeant saluted her.

“Ma'am,” he said.

Shawna held her coffee away from the gesture. “Can call me Shawna.”

The sergeant nodded. “Yes, ma'am.”

Respect had declined her suggestion.

The casino owner came to the station before lunch. I was wearing my jacket again by then, though Shawna had told me she would need it back when the air-conditioning offended her. The owner brought two gold watches and an envelope. He kept trying to put everything into my hands at once.

Two of his employees sat nearby waiting to give statements. One held an ice pack against the side of his face. The other had a bandage over scraped knuckles. The robbers had knocked them down on the way out. I gave a watch to each of them and kept the envelope long enough to buy lunch for the whole shift.

Officers carried plates through the station and took turns getting pictures beside the Lamborghini. Knox watched from her office doorway. When I came past, she looked toward the car.

“First morning?” Knox asked.

I held up my cup. “I need bigger cup holders.”

Knox laughed. “Anything else?”

I leaned against the doorframe. “Raise.”

Knox glanced at the clock. “Four hours.”

I gestured toward the lunch arriving at the next desk. “Look what the four hours did for you.”

By evening, people all over the station called me by name. By the following afternoon, several had offered to wait outside the Saint in case my evening needed company. I told them to stay close to Knox. I wanted Red looking at the badge, wondering how much of the city stood behind the man wearing it.

That afternoon was the day of the gala. I went to buy a white suit.

The tailor began with black. When that failed, he brought out dark blue. Shawna sat behind me in a little velvet chair, drinking champagne and telling him I looked handsome every time he asked. She had not looked up from her phone once. I could have been wearing a fucking curtain.

I put my hand against the white cloth I'd chosen. “This. Want the whole room seeing who came.”

The tailor's eyes went to the bandage beneath my shirt. “With your injury—”

I looked at him in the mirror. “Suit going to heal me?”

He turned back toward the cloth. The conversation became more productive immediately.

Tank stood by the door in a black suit we'd bought forty minutes earlier. He reached toward a bowl of peppermints. A seam opened across his shoulder with a dry little ripping sound. The tailor paused, then continued smoothing my sleeve as though the noise had happened in another business.

I looked past him. “Get Tank another one.”

The tailor glanced at the torn shoulder. “Larger?”

I held my arms out while he worked. “Whatever size a cathedral is.”

Tank put the peppermint back.

I watched him in the mirror. “Eat. Rich enough for the candy and whatever seam comes with it.”

Shawna laughed then. She looked up at me, phone forgotten against her skirt. For one moment I wasn't trying to read Dante or Red or a missing fortune in her face. My woman was laughing because I stood full of pins and meant to dress Tank like a building.

I would have bought an actual church to keep her there.

The waiting had been harder than the public disasters. Graduation, the badge, the van: those gave me something to do with my hands. Sitting beside Shawna while she sounded like a woman considering Red again gave me nowhere useful to put them.

She'd called him on both evenings since graduation, following the first call we'd planned at the dining table the night before. Three conversations in three nights. On graduation night, she ended the call with baby. I knew why. I'd agreed to the plan. Every intelligent sentence I'd spoken in support of it left my body when I heard that word.

Afterward, I followed her from the bed to the window.

“You don't have to say it like that,” I told her.

Shawna turned with the phone still in her hand. “Like what?”

I pointed at her face. “With your mouth.”

She stared at me for a few seconds. “Aiden.”

I folded my arms. “He's got a name. Use it.”

She put the phone down and kissed me. I let the argument end there. The next evening, after the last call before the gala, she saw me getting ready to begin and put her hands on my waist.

“Come here,” Shawna told me. “You can do that without starting the fight first.”

I denied the accusation while moving closer.

Now, in the tailor's shop, her phone lit again. I watched her answer while the man knelt beside my shoes.

“Him?” I asked Shawna.

She kept typing. “Rosa.”

I tilted my head toward the screen. “Let me see.”

She held it up. Six gowns lay across a bed in the photograph. Rosa wanted to know whether I was paying for all of it.

I nodded. “Tell her yes.”

Shawna looked back at the picture. “Don't know how much.”

I faced the mirror again. “Capital fucking letters.”

Shawna sent the message, put her phone away, and came around the tailor to straighten my collar. She smoothed one edge, then the other. I watched her hands in the glass.

“You're allowed to be scared tonight,” Shawna told me.

I looked over her head at my reflection. “I'm not.”

She pressed the collar flat. “Okay.”

I looked down at her. “You are?”

Shawna met my eyes. “Yes.”

When she started to step back, I took her hand gently. Dante's watch slid against my fingers. I let it stay there.

“Then I'm scared too,” I told her.

She kissed the corner of my mouth that wasn't cut.

“Good,” Shawna said. “Maybe you'll listen.”

I listened to everything. The difficulty came afterward, when I sometimes decided the world needed correcting for what I'd heard.

At home, Rosa had turned our bedroom into a place where my belongings existed only until a woman needed the space. Shoes occupied the chairs. Perfume hung in the air. A champagne bottle stood in the bathroom sink beside my toothbrush. My pistol had moved from the dresser to a folded hand towel, making room for a heated hairbrush.

I stopped in the doorway. Rosa looked up from a pair of earrings and asked why I was there.

“Sleep here,” I told her.

Rosa pointed toward the hall. “Not while we're busy.”

She wore red with diamonds at her throat. Shawna sat farther inside with her hair being pinned up. I could see one eye in the mirror and the beginning of a smile when Rosa tried to send me away.

I put a hand against the doorframe. “Need Shawna.”

Rosa addressed her reflection. “Your nigga needs patience.”

I lifted my free hand. “Bought some. Arrives tomorrow.”

Rosa picked up a shoe. I retrieved my pistol, secured it beneath the white jacket, and clipped the captain's badge inside its lining. Then I went downstairs before she could explain the shipping options.

Marcellus sat by the windows in a gray suit. His shoes had been polished until they looked wet. We meant to pass him off as a wealthy jeweler under another name. The suit helped. The set of his mouth suggested the tailor had been unable to remove the part of him that wanted Red dragged behind a car.

Celeste's letter lay in its sleeve on the table. He had one palm across it and wasn't reading. I stopped beside the opposite chair.

“Ready?” I asked Marcellus.

He looked out toward the water. “For years.”

I touched the chair back. “Not what I asked.”

He turned toward me. A small smile returned.

“Yes, Aiden,” Marcellus said.

I lowered myself into the chair. My side complained before I finished sitting. That bullet had settled into my life like it intended to pay rent. Marcellus watched me pause until the worst of the pain passed.

“Does she know how much it hurts?” Marcellus asked.

I looked toward the stairs. “Knows everything.”

Marcellus kept his eyes on me. “Does she?”

I turned back. “Enough.”

He folded the letter inside its sleeve and put it into his inner pocket.

“The stone cannot love you back,” Marcellus said.

I rested my hand against my bandage. “Got somebody for that.”

Marcellus smoothed the pocket. “Remember which one you came for.”

Tank entered before I could answer. He held two phones. When he saw us, he turned one facedown against his palm.

“Cars ready,” Tank said.

I looked at the hidden screen. “Who's calling?”

Tank stopped beside the doorway. “Nobody.”

I pointed at his hands. “You need two phones for nobody?”

Tank slid them into his pockets. “In case somebody does.”

On another night I might have laughed. I watched his hands come out empty and thought about asking again.

Then I heard Shawna on the stairs.

I turned.

She wore dark blue, close against her body, with a train following down the steps. Diamonds touched her ears. Dante's watch lay on her wrist. Her hair was up, leaving the little scar above her eyebrow plain to see. I knew that small break in her skin as well as I knew my own hands. I wanted to kiss it before we went anywhere.

Shawna looked down at the dress as she reached the last step.

“It's Rosa's,” Shawna told me. “She never wore it.”

I got to my feet. “Said I'd buy anything.”

She smoothed the skirt. “Wanted this one.”

Rosa lifted the train off the step behind her.

“Want it back,” Rosa said. “Both of you hear?”

Shawna looked over her shoulder. “Yes.”

Her voice was soft. I heard it without giving it the attention I wish I'd given it then.

Rosa came down carrying a small envelope. She showed it to me before putting it into her purse.

“Watch paper,” Rosa said. “Original. Nobody gets it off me.”

Red's order was inside. Shawna had made a copy for the night and put that in her own purse. I watched Rosa close hers over the original and thought about how little paper it took to carry what had happened to Dante. If something went wrong with us, the truth still needed somewhere to be.

I looked back at Shawna because I didn't want to stay with that thought.

“Turn around,” I told her.

She made a slow turn, lifting the train clear of her heel.

I put my hand over my heart. “Again.”

Shawna smiled at me. “Aiden.”

I watched the blue cloth settle. “Trying to remember who I need to thank.”

She put the little silver pistol into her purse beside the copy of the note, then came close and rested both hands against my chest.

“Tear this dress,” Shawna told me, “I'm killing you before Red gets to.”

I touched her waist. “Buy the factory.”

Shawna brought her face closer. “Kill you in the factory.”

I kissed her. Tank cleared his throat behind us. I kissed her again, because that man did not determine how long I was allowed to be happy.

Outside, I counted the cars with one finger.

“I wanted six,” I told Tank.

He pointed to the Lamborghini, the Mercedes, Rosa's car and the green Bentley. “Four drivers.”

“Put somebody in the other two,” I said.

Tank kept his hand raised. “Which somebody?”

I looked toward our people. Shawna held her purse out to me.

“You can drive this,” she said.

I took it. “Four cars. Everybody got a fucking opinion.”

Apparently Tank had chosen the evening of a robbery to begin requiring arithmetic. I carried Shawna's purse to the Lamborghini, opened her door, and returned it when she sat down. Tank took the original black Mercedes. Rosa drove Marcellus, and Porsche brought Mercedes in the green Bentley.

Four cars reached the canopy together. Gold pillars. Flowers taller than the men guarding them. A fountain glittering with coins beneath lights that probably cost more to run than the wishes dropped into the water. I slowed beneath the entrance and looked at the building Red had raised against Marcellus's diamond.

White tie, the invitation said. I stepped out in my white suit with the cross at my chest and the badge inside my jacket. Shawna took my arm.

Cameras flashed.

That surprised me. I'd been preparing for guns.

“Keep walking,” Shawna said close to my ear.

I did, as if I'd bought the pavement and was pleased with the delivery.

A photographer asked us to stop beside a wall of white roses. Shawna put one hand on my shoulder. I settled mine at her waist. Before the flashes ended, a man with a microphone came alongside us and asked whether I was taking over T's interests.

I looked toward the lens. “Anybody owes them, owes me.”

The reporter moved the microphone closer. “And your relationship with Mr. Redmond?”

Redmond. He was using the whole government name for the evening. I respected the effort of dressing that much information for a party.

“Ask at the end,” I told the reporter.

Rosa stepped between him and us before I could improve the answer. Tank came close enough for his jacket to brush mine. He touched my arm.

“Anything happens,” Tank said quietly, “stay with me.”

I looked up at him. “Was the idea.”

He didn't answer. He took his hand away and followed us inside.

A staircase curved around a statue of a naked woman holding a bowl of real fire. I paused long enough to appreciate that. If you intended to waste money, you might as well make the building nervous.

I recognized people all over the room. Women wearing jewels I had seen being offered as security for drugs. Men shaking hands with people I'd heard them threaten across tables. A preacher from television stood beside a man I knew dealt in stolen cars. The preacher wore the larger watch.

Through the open door of a private lounge, I saw champagne buckets and cocaine laid out on a mirror. A man sitting between two women lifted his glass when he noticed me. I had spent a reckless, filthy weekend with all three before I knew Shawna. He looked entirely too pleased to find me dressed for public life.

“Disappear when you fall in love?” the man called to me.

I kept Shawna's hand in mine. “Got promoted.”

The women laughed. Shawna looked from one face to another, then up at me.

“All three?” Shawna asked.

I considered the possibility of improving the truth, then left it alone. “Long weekend.”

Shawna shook her head. “Nasty motherfucker.”

I smiled at her. “Love me.”

Her grip tightened. “Don't get confident.”

I got confident. Enough that when Red appeared on the far staircase in a dark green jacket, I smiled at him as though he'd arrived at a party I was considering letting him enjoy.

His eyes stayed on Shawna.

“There she is,” Red said when he reached us.

He held out both hands. Shawna gave him one. He lifted it and kissed her skin above Dante's watch.

I pictured his head going through the nearest arrangement. His green jacket catching fire in the statue's bowl. The preacher beside the stolen-car man explaining to God why everybody ought to let me finish.

I smiled instead.

“Good party,” I told Red. “Shame about your future.”

He looked at me at last.

“Captain,” Red said. The word came slowly, with his jaw tightening around it. “You clean up well.”

I met his eyes. “You still talking.”

Shawna's fingers tightened against my arm. Red laughed softly and turned toward a photographer.

“All three of us,” Red called. “Get a photograph.”

He guided Shawna into the middle before I could answer and placed a hand against her upper arm. A broad, dark ring sat on that hand, a blue stone set almost level with the surface. For a moment the green sleeve pulled back enough to show it clearly.

Marcellus had been approaching a few feet behind us with Rosa. When Red put his hand on Shawna, the old man stopped. I couldn't tell what he was looking at from where I stood.

The flash went off.

Red asked for another. I looked straight into the lens. If that picture ever reached a museum, I wanted visitors to have no trouble identifying which man had come with a plan to ruin the evening.

An attendant returned with small glossy copies while Red greeted Rosa. She took two and put them into her purse. Her smile stayed in place while the papers went inside. I watched her fingers close around the clasp before she let go.

Red turned back to Shawna and offered his arm.

“Promised this woman a dance,” Red said.

Shawna looked at me.

I remembered the plan. I remembered helping make it. She needed him close, distracted, willing to believe he'd already taken her back from me.

I also remembered having hands.

“One dance,” I told Red.

He smiled at Shawna. “See what she wants.”

She took his arm. I let her hand leave mine.

Then he led her onto the floor, and I followed with my eyes as if somebody had carried my heart into traffic and asked me to admire how carefully he was driving.

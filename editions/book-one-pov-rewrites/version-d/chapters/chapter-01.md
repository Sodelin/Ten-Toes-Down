## 1. The Funeral Had a Two-Drink Minimum

“Nigga, it’s my motherfucking funeral,” I told the man taking tickets.

He sat behind a folding table with a cash box between his knees and a rubber stamp beside his elbow. Velvet curtains covered the ballroom entrance behind him. Through them came bass, laughter, and the smell of hot butter. I stood there hearing a roomful of people enjoy my death while this motherfucker prepared to charge me for access.

“Forty dollars,” the ticket man said, holding out his hand.

I waited. The memorial banner above him had my name on it. The photograph beside him had my whole face. I gave him another clue anyway.

“I’m Aiden.” I pointed at the picture.

He checked the photograph, then checked me. Whoever had made the poster had started with a picture of me getting arrested. They had cropped out the cuffs and added angel wings but left the officer’s hand on my shoulder. I had a police escort into heaven.

“You look better in the picture,” the ticket man decided.

“Nigga, I’m alive.” I spread my arms so he could inspect the evidence.

The ticket man tapped his cash box. “Then you can pay.”

This was Vegas, after we came home. I had been back long enough for my problems to acquire a cover charge. Beneath those gold letters, I wondered whether a genuine corpse got in free or whether somebody checked its pockets.

I stepped out of the line and called Tank. A fork tapped a plate on the other end. I could hear a faint version of the same sound through the curtains.

“Why there a cover charge at my funeral?” I asked my brother.

“The shrimp was expensive,” Tank said around something he was chewing.

I watched a woman come through the curtains with cocktail sauce shining at the corner of her mouth. “Why do I have shrimp?”

“You died successful,” Tank told me.

I listened to him chew again. “Nigga, are you eating?”

“Grieving,” Tank said.

I hung up, paid the forty, and offered my wrist. The ticket man stamped DECEASED above my watch. I looked at the wet purple letters while he put the stamp away.

“That gets you one well drink,” he said.

I pointed at the sign beside his table. “The sign says two-drink minimum.”

“Second one’s your responsibility.” The ticket man reached around me for the next person’s money.

A man waiting behind me leaned forward, looking from my wrist to the picture.

“Hold up,” that man said. “Ain’t that the deceased?”

“Paid deceased,” the ticket man answered after checking my stamp.

The man in line brought out his phone. “Get a picture? People'll think I knew you personally.”

“Meeting personally right now,” I told him. “Both our hearts fucking beating.”

He turned the phone upright and shook his head.

“Yeah,” he told me, “but the funeral picture hit different.”

He put his arm around my shoulder and raised the phone. I held my own memorial program against my chest while he took the picture. On the screen, he looked delighted to have lost me. I left him beside the poster checking whether my death needed a filter and went in.

The ballroom opened around rows of round tables. Along the right wall, a mirror ran behind the bar; at the far end, two shallow steps led to a low stage. The band occupied its back half: drums, piano, two keyboards, and enough brass to invade somewhere with confidence. My coffin stood on a wheeled stand at the front of the platform, covered in flowers. A small card resting on the flowers reminded mourners to tip the staff.

I stopped beside the chocolate fountain and looked for my people.

Tank stood near the stage with a plate. Porsche moved behind the musicians carrying our duplicate silver case. Mercedes waited below the steps in a red suit, pressing her thumb into her palm. I had watched her do that all week.

Then I found Shawna coming toward me, and the rest of the room became something I could check later.

My wife wore black, with a slit up one leg and a diamond at her throat. I had watched her arrange the hat at home. She tilted it, considered the mirror, and tilted it farther. By the time she reached me at the funeral, I would have paid to be the dead man who got to disappoint that widow by coming back.

I held up a program I had picked off a table. “Why this say I’m survived by eleven children?”

“Those are sponsors,” Shawna said, putting a finger beneath the small print.

I followed her finger. “Nigga, one of them’s Jeremiah.”

“Jeremiah’s Collision Repair,” she read to me.

I folded the program and slipped my hand around her waist. “You enjoying widowhood?”

Shawna looked up at me beneath the brim. “The parking is excellent.”

I pulled her closer. She let me, then put two fingers against my mouth. Her smile stayed where the room could see it. The pressure of her fingers told me to listen.

“Eight minutes,” she said. “Kitchen. Stay there until we get the case.”

The kitchen door stood left of the stage. I could see its round window above a waiter’s shoulder. Beyond the piano, another doorway led backstage, where Mercedes was supposed to inspect the recordings Silas Gold had stolen. Porsche would switch the case during the inspection. I had attended the planning conversation. My contribution, according to the women who loved me, was to remain out of fucking sight.

“Soon as he walks in, I could take it,” I told Shawna.

She turned her eyes toward the crowded tables. “And his men could start shooting into everybody who paid to eat shrimp.”

“Apparently excellent shrimp,” I said, following her glance.

“Aiden.” Shawna took her fingers off my mouth and put her palm flat against my chest.

I stopped arguing.

Silas had the original recordings of Mercedes’s first album. She had told me exactly what was on that drive: the takes, the musicians, the late nights in a studio that had since been demolished. She could sing the songs again. She couldn't put those people back in that room on the same night. In the final chorus of one track, her voice had cracked. She had kept it because, she told me, that was where she sounded most like herself.

I had seen her explain this without crying, her thumb pressed so hard into her palm that the skin changed color. Watching her beside the stage now, I wanted to get the drive into her hand before she had to explain a damn thing again.

Silas usually conducted business from behind bulletproof glass. He sent other men out to be unpleasant. But when Shawna invited him to a private auction of my belongings after the memorial, he agreed to come. My watch, my cars, pieces of the collection: she had described the offer to me afterward, including the way his voice warmed up when she mentioned the cars. He would bring Mercedes’s recordings so everything could be settled together.

Apparently my death offered the motherfucker a compelling reason to leave his aquarium.

Mercedes looked up, met my eyes, and came over. She adjusted my collar. It had been straight when she arrived, but I held still beneath her hands.

“Don’t do the thing,” she told me.

“You gotta narrow that down,” I said. “I got a substantial range.”

Mercedes looked around my funeral. “The thing where this becomes about you.”

I nodded toward the entrance. “My photograph is six feet tall.”

“Exactly,” she said. “The nigga has enough publicity.”

I stopped smiling and covered her hand where it rested on my shoulder.

“You’ll get your music,” I told her.

Mercedes squeezed my shoulder. “I know. I want to enjoy getting it.”

I took my hand off hers. For once, I had managed to promise something without deciding how she'd have to enjoy it.

She returned to the stage. Shawna kissed me, put both hands on my shoulders, and turned me toward the kitchen.

“In there,” my wife said. “Be a beautiful secret for eight minutes.”

“Y’all asking a lot of secrecy,” I told her, but I went.

The cook gave me a place beside a steel prep table. An old speaker above the shelves carried the stage microphones; a little service microphone beside it allowed staff to make announcements. I could see the stage through the round window in the door. The cook kept peeling potatoes while Tank climbed the steps with a plate in one hand.

My brother took the microphone in the other. “We gathered here because Aiden would want these niggas supporting the bar.”

Glasses rose across the ballroom. I watched Tank wait for them to come down before he continued.

“My brother was generous,” he said. “Gave me the shirt off his back.”

I leaned toward the service microphone. “Police was looking for a man in a red shirt.”

The laugh reached me through the wall before it came through the speaker. I pushed the door open to appreciate my work. Shawna raised one finger without turning around. I went back inside, and the cook moved a tray of shrimp away from my elbow.

Tank let the room settle.

“But my nigga came back,” he said.

I put my palm against the door.

He told them about the station. I had said he hadn't done shit, paid what they demanded, waited six hours, and bought him breakfast when they finally let him out. As he spoke, I remembered the hard chair and the cold coffee. I remembered Tank coming through the door and seeing me still there. His shoulders had dropped before he put the smile on.

My brother sniffed into the microphone. “Then the nigga asked for his shirt.”

The drummer put a small roll beneath the laugh. In the kitchen, I wiped my mouth with a napkin I hadn't needed a moment earlier.

The laughter hadn't quite stopped when Silas arrived.

I saw the white fur coat moving between the tables. Four men followed. The smallest carried the silver case against his leg. The largest walked with his elbows out, although his shoulders had already taken responsibility for blocking most of the aisle.

Silas held up a bottle as he approached the stage.

“Miss the good part?” he asked Tank.

My brother looked at the coffin. “Depends how this speech goes.”

Silas laughed. His men joined a beat later. From the kitchen, I watched that little delay and filed it with the other things I disliked about him.

Shawna met him at the steps. He bent over her offered hand to kiss it. She moved just far enough for his mouth to meet his own thumb. I pressed my lips together to keep from making another contribution through the service microphone.

“The recordings,” Shawna said, holding her hand out again.

“After my tribute,” Silas told her.

My wife kept her hand where it was. “Mercedes inspects them first. That was the agreement.”

Silas gestured at the crowd with his bottle. “Look at this room. Let a man have his moment.”

He climbed onto the platform, took the microphone from Tank, and put the bottle on my coffin. Tank picked it up and handed it back. Silas put it down again. Tank returned it again. The drummer followed the bottle with his eyes and supplied a little tick each time it changed hands.

Silas turned toward my brother. “You got somewhere to be?”

“Eventually,” Tank said, still holding the bottle out to him.

Silas took it and faced the room.

“Aiden and I had differences,” he announced.

I remembered one of those differences very clearly. I had owned my boat. He had stolen it. I waited to hear whether he would mention that part.

Instead, he talked about my appetites. Then he looked at Shawna and suggested I had left people unprovided for.

“I would never presume—” Silas began.

“Then don’t,” my wife told him.

A woman near the front slapped her table. “That nigga got instructions!” she called.

Silas stood through the laughter with his teeth showing. I could see his fingers tighten around the microphone. When the room quieted, he called Mercedes’s name.

She stayed below the steps until the smallest guard carried the case onto the platform and opened it. Silas put his bottle back on the coffin, took out the drive, and held it by one end. He waved the guard away. The man came back down the steps with the open, empty case in his left hand. Mercedes’s eyes followed the drive. Each time Silas moved his hand, her eyes moved too.

“Sing us a tribute,” Silas told her. “Then you can apologize for what you’ve been saying about me.”

Mercedes put one foot on the bottom step. “You promised an inspection.”

“Sing first.” Silas offered her the microphone, then pulled it back before she could reach it. “Ask nicely.”

Through the window, I saw the biggest guard move across the backstage doorway. Porsche stopped behind him with the duplicate case. She leaned to see past his shoulder, but he shifted with her. The route we had planned was closed.

Mercedes took her foot off the step. Her empty hand opened at her side.

Her heel settled on the carpet. That little movement stays with me more clearly than the gunshot that came afterward. Silas had got her to step away from her own music. My hand closed around the button of my jacket.

I took my jacket off.

The cook looked up from his potatoes. “You want me to hold that?”

“Please,” I said, handing it to him.

He hung it clear of the steam. “You coming back?”

I picked up the shrimp tray and checked the window one last time. “Apparently I do.”

I came through the kitchen door without hurrying. Shawna saw the tray, looked toward the stage, and pulled her chair clear of the aisle. Tank put down his plate. I saw both movements and kept walking.

A woman beside the aisle turned from me to the coffin.

“Lord,” she whispered.

Her companion began crossing himself. The woman caught his forearm.

“Don’t do that,” she told him. “He got shrimp.”

“Could still be spiritual,” the man answered, watching my tray.

His companion pulled at his chair. “Move your chair, nigga.”

He moved it. I went past with the shrimp level and stopped beside the smallest guard at the foot of the stage.

“Shrimp?” I asked, tilting the tray toward him.

The guard took one. I offered the sauce, and he dipped carefully. I watched him get it all the way to his mouth before climbing the two steps onto the platform. If he was going to have a bad evening, I could at least make sure he ate first.

Silas heard me speaking to his man. He spun around so fast the fur collar slapped his mouth. His eyes went to the coffin, then back to me.

Forty dollars. Worth it.

“Nigga, it’s my motherfucking funeral,” I told him.

He shoved the microphone into its stand and reached inside his coat. I saw the pistol clear the fur. I drove the edge of the tray up beneath his wrist.

The shot went into the ceiling. The gun flew out of his hand, struck the stage, and skidded under the piano. Plaster burst over the musicians. The trumpet player ducked with both arms around his instrument, and shrimp scattered across the platform.

People screamed. Tank jumped down the steps and hauled two guests behind a table. Shawna stood in the aisle holding the chair she had moved. I saw her settle both hands around its back as I caught Silas by his coat and pulled him away from the edge.

“Everybody else brought flowers!” I shouted at him.

Silas grabbed the bottle from the coffin and swung. Glass broke against my shoulder.

Pain ran down my arm. My grip loosened, but a fistful of fur stayed caught in my fingers. For one breath I couldn't tell whether the warmth spreading beneath my shirt was liquor or blood. Then I could tell. The improvement in my understanding did nothing for my fucking disposition.

I hit him with the tray.

His head went sideways. The drive flew from his other hand, and Mercedes stepped into its path, catching it against her chest.

“Got it!” Mercedes shouted as she climbed the last step onto the platform.

The big guard left the backstage doorway and hurried toward me. Porsche came through behind him. At the top of the steps, he planted a foot to turn. She caught up and swung the duplicate case into his ankle.

His foot stopped. The rest of him went down the steps into Shawna’s chair.

Wood broke across his mouth. He landed sitting among the pieces, touching his teeth with one hand. Shawna stood above him holding what remained of the chair back.

“Stay,” my wife told him.

He looked up at her and stayed.

I still held Silas’s coat. In the mirror behind the bar, I saw another guard draw behind me. Mercedes had tucked the drive inside her jacket. She followed my glance toward the mirror, seized the microphone stand with both hands, and swung it as I ducked.

The stand caught the guard across the chest and drove him into the drum kit. A cymbal folded against its mount. The drummer stood up, looked at it, and then looked at the man tangled underneath.

“Nigga, that’s vintage,” the drummer said.

He picked up his stool and brought the padded seat down across the guard’s shoulder and back. The man collapsed over the bass drum with his arms covering his head. I had never seen a percussion section make its complaint so directly.

Tank came back up the steps. At the same moment, Silas wriggled out of his coat and ran straight toward him. My brother caught him around the waist, lifted the coffin lid with his free hand, and tipped him inside.

The lid banged shut. Something squealed underneath it.

Tank froze.

I knew that look. I had seen it on him before an important detail arrived much too fucking late.

The lid flew open. A little man in a gold tracksuit sat up underneath Silas. He wore my spare sunglasses, knocked sideways across his face. He shoved one lens back over his eye and pointed at Tank.

“NIGGA, I TOLD YOU NINE-FIFTEEN!” the little man shouted.

I let Silas’s fur fall out of my hand. “You hired a corpse?”

Tank looked at his shoes. “Resurrection package.”

“A resurrection package,” I repeated, looking at the man climbing out from beneath Silas.

“It included the dance,” Tank explained.

The performer lifted a finger. “And fog.”

My brother pointed at the dust hanging above the stage. “We got atmosphere.”

“That’s ceiling,” the performer told him.

“Work with the venue,” Tank said.

The little man climbed over Silas’s stomach, stepped onto the platform, and straightened his tracksuit. He explained that he did the surprise, he did the dance, and he left. Nobody, he informed us, had mentioned wrestling.

Silas’s fourth man began edging toward the entrance. Tank saw him and pointed without lowering his voice.

“Sit your ass down. The nigga hasn’t danced,” my brother told him.

The man sat. Below the steps, the smallest guard lowered the empty case to the floor. He still had a shrimp tail in his other hand.

“I’m with catering,” that guard announced.

The cook leaned out of the kitchen. “No the fuck you are not.”

It took us seven minutes to gather the guns, tie up the men, and convince the room that the bar remained open. I watched people come out from behind tables, feel around for bags and shoes, and begin telling their neighbors what those same neighbors had just seen.

Silas kept trying to sit up in the coffin. Each time, the performer eased him down with a gold sneaker against his forehead.

“Stop touching my face,” Silas complained.

“Stop being in my office,” the little man told him.

Mercedes carried the drive to the sound desk. I followed far enough to see her take it out of her jacket and hand it to the technician. He connected it, moved aside, and gave her headphones. Files filled the screen.

She chose one. Porsche took her hand beneath the desk. I started closer, and Shawna caught the back of my belt.

I stopped beside my wife. The cut in my shoulder throbbed, but I could keep it bearable by holding the arm still. I watched Mercedes close her eyes. None of us spoke while she listened.

She removed the headphones and wiped beneath one eye.

“All here,” Mercedes said.

Tank let out a breath beside me. “Then play one, my nigga.”

Mercedes looked around the room. I followed her glance across fallen flowers, memorial napkins pressed to bleeding faces, and my huge photograph leaning crookedly near the entrance. Then she went toward the stage.

The drummer was fitting a spare cymbal. Mercedes stopped beside him.

“You good?” she asked him.

“You doing something slow?” the drummer asked, holding the wing nut in place.

“No,” Mercedes said.

He tightened the nut and sat down. “Then yeah.”

She gave the technician a track number. A hiss came through the speakers, then saxophone, warm and rough. I knew from what she had told me that I was hearing the room they couldn't rebuild. The band listened for a bar and joined the recording.

Mercedes pointed toward my photograph.

“Turn that nigga around,” she told me. “I need the room.”

I turned it myself.

She began low, almost speaking to the nearest table. Then the bass came beneath her. She planted one heel against the monitor and raised her chin. Her next line reached the back of the ballroom. People stood between the fallen chairs, reaching for one another’s hands.

The little man closed the coffin, stepped onto it, and checked the time. Silas pounded beneath his shoes.

“Get off!” Silas shouted from inside.

“Nine-fifteen, bitch!” the performer answered.

Then he danced. Tank threw both hands up.

“That nigga earned his deposit!” my brother called.

I watched Porsche laugh with her face lifted toward Mercedes. The cook came out with my jacket over his shoulder and a tray in his hands. A woman took the tray, put it on a table, and caught his hand before he could retreat.

“Nigga, I’m working,” the cook protested as she pulled him toward the open floor.

“Not anymore,” the woman told him.

Shawna slipped her fingers into my good hand. I looked down and found her studying the blood on my sleeve.

“You’re bleeding,” my wife said.

“Little bit,” I told her.

She touched near the cut. I winced, and she took her hand away.

“We’ll get it looked at,” Shawna said.

I turned toward the stage. “After the song.”

She stayed against my unhurt side.

The last chorus came. Through the speakers, Mercedes’s younger voice caught for half a breath. I heard the crack she had described. Onstage, Mercedes sang beside her own recording and smiled. Porsche stopped laughing to listen. I saw Mercedes look toward her and keep singing.

I loosened my grip on Shawna's hand. Mercedes had her heel on the monitor, her voice in the room, and no need to ask anybody nicely.

Shawna leaned close to my ear. “Eleven sponsors is a lot of mouths to feed.”

I laughed, hurt my shoulder, and laughed again. Shawna smiled against my cheek. She had caught me with that kind of straight-faced bullshit plenty of times, and I still walked right into it.

The ticket man pulled the entrance curtain aside for a new group.

“Sixty dollars,” he told them.

I pointed across the room. “Nigga, it was forty!”

He gestured at Mercedes, the coffin dancer, and my upright, bleeding ass.

“Live show now,” the ticket man explained.

By the time the police took Silas away, the band had demanded overtime and Tank had begun negotiating for the leftover shrimp. I stood near the sound desk with Shawna, reluctant to leave the room while it still held the music.

The young technician brought two clean glasses. His headphones hung around his neck. He kept looking between Shawna and the DECEASED stamp on my wrist, trying so hard not to ask something that I could almost hear the question.

“Can I ask something?” the technician said.

I held out my stamped wrist. “Apparently you can charge for it.”

He smiled and looked at Shawna. “How’d you two meet?”

My wife laughed before I could start. “Let me tell it.”

“Baby, I was there,” I reminded her.

“So was I,” Shawna said. “One of us remembers who saved whom.”

The technician pulled up a chair. Tank called for somebody to get him a plate.

“I’m on the clock,” the young man told my brother.

“So am I,” Tank answered. “I organized a death.”

Mercedes handed the technician a napkin. Porsche moved the bottle closer. I watched him loosen the headphones around his neck and sit down. I pulled up a chair across from him, lowering myself carefully so my shoulder wouldn't argue. Tank dragged over a chair for himself. Shawna sat beside me, against my good side, with one of the clean glasses.

I looked at the man who had asked the question. “The night I met her in Vegas. You want the whole thing?”

He nodded. My wife made herself comfortable.

“Start before you became reasonable,” Shawna told me.

“We still waiting on that chapter,” I said.

Tank laughed into his shrimp. I took a drink and let the ballroom quiet around the edges. The musicians were packing their instruments. Somebody swept plaster off the stage. My wife’s knee rested against mine.

Before the house, before the ring, I'd have seen a man holding hands at his own funeral and wondered what the fuck he'd done to his life. Shawna moved her knee against mine. I let myself remember how little I'd known when I first took her hand.

The bass had been shaking a different wall that first night. Big T had his hand on my shoulder. Eleven other men waited beside the cars.

I was twenty-four, and I still thought I could see all the trouble in front of me.

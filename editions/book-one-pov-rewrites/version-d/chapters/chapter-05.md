## 5. Flowers for the Man I Killed

Big T's coffin was candy blue.

I had approved that color myself. The funeral director slid a little rectangle of it across his desk, and I held it against the light until it shone like the door of a car nobody could afford. Then I pointed at the gold handles in his catalog. By the time I finished spending money, the man had stopped using the word package. We were developing a fucking estate.

I paid for the flowers, the cars, the musicians and the meal afterward. I ordered a portrait six feet high. T smiled in it with one hand tucked into his jacket, looking pleased to be the most expensive thing in the room even after dying.

He had called me son. Then he had shot me. I couldn't get those two things to occupy the same place in my head, so I kept buying things that could occupy the chapel.

On the morning of the funeral, I stood before the mirror downstairs trying to close my cuff. My black suit covered the bandages. It didn't cover the fact that my fingers kept missing a button directly in front of them. The medicine left a little delay between what I wanted and what my body agreed to do.

Tank came down wearing black too. He stopped behind me, large and dark in the glass.

“You ain't gotta go,” Tank said.

I tried the button again. “I killed him.”

Tank took the cuff from my fingers. “That's why you ain't gotta go.”

He closed it for me without looking up. That was worse, somehow, than if he'd told me I looked sick.

I watched his face in the mirror. “He got nobody else?”

Tank smoothed the sleeve. “Plenty people.”

I turned toward him. “People that loved him.”

Tank went to the window. Beyond him, men were waiting beside the cars. I could see one of them adjusting his tie in a windshield, using both hands with a freedom that irritated the shit out of me.

I let my arm drop. “You loved him.”

Tank kept looking outside.

“You can say it,” I told him. “I'm standing right here.”

Tank rubbed his thumb along the window frame. “He gave me work when I came out.”

I looked down at the cross resting against my shirt. “Gave me a car.”

Tank finally turned. “Ain't the same.”

I nodded. “I know.”

I did know, though I had needed him to say it. A gift could be a kindness. It could also be a hook. Those didn't cancel each other just because it would have been easier to bury a man who had only ever been cruel.

We stood there in our funeral suits, the two men who had left T beside a motel pool. I could still remember the sound his body made against the fence. Now I was wondering whether the flowers I'd bought were enough. There was no dignified way through a morning like that. I was hoping for a way through at all.

Then Shawna came down the stairs dressed entirely in white.

Tank's head went up. “Oh, hell no.”

She stopped on the bottom step. Her coat hung open over a white dress. She carried white gloves in one hand, and the watch on her wrist caught the light when she rested her other hand on the banister. Dante's watch. I could see my own black sleeve reflected in its face.

Shawna looked at Tank. “What?”

Tank held both hands out toward her outfit. “It's a funeral.”

Shawna came down the last step. “I know whose.”

Tank looked at me, then back at her. “You can't wear that.”

Shawna waited for my answer.

I could have asked her to change. I could have explained that we already had enough problems without arriving as the bride and groom of the man I'd killed. But she stood in that white dress with her chin level and her husband's watch on, and I wasn't about to make her put on mourning for his murderer.

Also, she looked fucking beautiful.

I picked up my jacket. “Tank, get the car before she decides on a veil.”

The chapel stood above a circular drive, its front steps facing a little fountain and the cemetery entrance across the road. Black cars filled the spaces along both sides. Our men had left room for the hearse by the steps. A wheelchair waited there for me too. I looked at it as Tank opened my door, and then I looked away before he could make a reasonable suggestion.

Shawna helped me out. Once I was upright, a pallbearer came over and held out his hand. Rico, thirty-two. He had his wrist turned toward the light before I finished shaking it.

“Daughter sent these,” Rico told me. “Moved out of state, still telling me how to dress.”

He turned his wrist so the design caught the sun. I held his sleeve lightly and looked.

“Girl got taste,” I told Rico.

“Told her that,” he said, smiling with his whole face. “She said I better wear them where people could see.”

He stepped aside for us. I took Shawna's hand and climbed the steps slowly. Tank stayed close enough behind us that I could hear his shoes stop every time mine did.

Conversation thinned when we entered the chapel. Some people stood. Others remained so firmly seated I could see their backs press into the pews. A woman near the front began crying harder. I didn't know whether seeing me had increased her grief or given it somebody to blame.

I kept moving.

A week before, T had sent me to pick up a woman and a bag of money. Now he lay at the far end of this aisle, his men followed me, and the woman had her fingers between mine. I hadn't felt proud of that arrangement until a man behind a pew whispered that I shouldn't have come.

Pride rushed into me hot enough to scare me.

I stopped without turning. “Which nigga said it? Say it loud so I know who to look at.”

Nobody repeated himself. I waited one breath longer than I needed to, then made my legs carry me to the second row.

Shawna sat with her knees together. Her unused gloves lay in her lap. I held her bare hand while the preacher told us about a generous man.

That part was true. I knew men T had found rooms for when nobody would open a door. I had seen him pay a bill, fold the receipt, and tell the person thanking him to go home before he changed his mind. Some of the people crying around me had eaten because he'd answered his phone. I could hear them agreeing with the preacher, little yeses rising from different parts of the room.

Then the preacher began talking about T's love for his family.

Shawna's fingers slipped out of mine.

I turned. She stared at the portrait. One of the white gloves was balled in her fist, and a thread at its seam stretched until it broke. I put my palm on her knee. She didn't look at me, but she let it stay.

Tank stood up beside us.

He hadn't told me he was speaking. He walked to the front with a folded sheet of paper, opened it at the microphone, and looked down. I watched his chest rise inside the black jacket. He folded the page again and put it away.

“T knew my mother,” Tank said. His first words boomed through the little chapel. He moved back from the microphone and tried again. “Knew she worried about me.”

The preacher took a step aside. Tank put one hand on the lectern.

“Every time I came around, he made me call her,” Tank told us. “Could be three niggas waiting on money and one nigga waiting to get his ass beat. Didn't matter. T would hand me the phone and tell me to call my mother. Tell her I ate.”

A woman near the aisle laughed while wiping her nose. Tank glanced toward the sound, then looked at the blue coffin.

“She died while I was inside,” Tank said. “He paid for everything. Told me I didn't owe him for that.”

He rubbed his free hand down the front of his suit. The microphone picked up the cloth scraping beneath his palm.

Tank looked out over the pews. “I owed him for it anyway.”

Beside me, Shawna drew a long breath through her nose. I kept my hand still on her knee.

Tank's voice grew rougher. “He did some shit.”

The preacher shifted his weight. Tank looked at him, then returned to us.

“Some shit I can't stand here and fix,” Tank said. “I ain't gonna try. But he picked me up when nobody was answering. And I remember that.”

He left the lectern without taking the paper out again. When he reached our row, he looked at me. I nodded and moved my knee so he could pass.

It hurt to hear him. I kept my face turned toward Tank until he sat down. His hands lay open on his knees, and I left him room to keep them there.

Red's flowers stood beside the coffin, taller than every other arrangement. White roses. Blue ribbon. His name printed on a card large enough to qualify as attending in person.

He hadn't come. I looked at the door every time it opened anyway, and hated him for making me wait at a dead man's funeral.

When the preacher invited us forward, Shawna stayed seated. Other people filed past our row, touching one another's shoulders. I waited until there was room in the aisle, then leaned toward her.

“You don't have to,” I told Shawna.

She opened her fist and smoothed the torn glove against her thigh. “I know.”

Then she stood and took my hand.

Up close, T looked almost comfortable. Somebody had cleaned the blood from him and settled his head against white satin. The ring that had cut my mouth lay on his folded hand. I remembered wanting to impress him. I remembered how quickly I had believed him when he called me son.

I stood over a corpse embarrassed by how much I had wanted from it.

Shawna took a folded photograph from her coat. She opened it against her palm before placing it on T's chest, and I recognized the little chapel where she and Dante had married. In this print, his brother hadn't been cut out of the edge. T stood with a hand on Dante's shoulder. Dante smiled as if there were nothing in the world behind him he needed to watch.

Shawna pushed the photograph beneath T's folded fingers. “You can take your fucking picture with you.”

I thought she was finished. Then she bent closer to his face.

“He loved you,” Shawna whispered. “You knew he did.”

Her voice broke on loved. I looked at the gold handle nearest my knee. Behind us, the preacher cleared his throat, and I wanted to tell him to swallow whatever the fuck was bothering him. Let her say it. Nothing left on his schedule could matter more to T.

Shawna straightened and wiped beneath one eye with the side of her finger. I touched T's cuff. I still don't know why. The cloth was cold and smooth. I could imagine his arm moving inside it, reaching up to pull me into that proud embrace he had given me behind the club.

“All you had to do was let us go,” I told him.

I took my hand away before I could wait for an answer.

Outside, Rosa stood beside the cars in sunglasses, holding the front of her coat against the wind. I'd heard her tell two people already that she was there for Shawna. When I reached her, she made sure I had the information too.

“I ain't crying for that nigga,” Rosa told me.

I glanced back toward the chapel. “Nobody asked you to.”

Rosa lifted her chin. “I know. I just want the dead to be clear.”

Shawna leaned against her. Rosa opened the coat enough to pull her inside it, and the two women stood together while the pallbearers brought T down the steps.

Mercedes and Porsche waited by our Mercedes with a tray of sandwiches. They had dressed for church and brought provisions. In our circle, even an ambush had a chance of being catered.

A new white Bentley stood a few spaces away. Porsche showed me the card Leon had sent with it. He'd written that watching the last car die had changed his feelings about ownership. I admired a man capable of learning the wrong lesson so expensively.

Tank took two sandwiches. “That's love.”

Porsche held the tray steady for him. “That's a nigga with insurance.”

Rico adjusted his cuffs before taking the coffin's weight again. He and the other five pallbearers brought T to the hearse. The driver held the rear door open while they slid the coffin onto its rollers and secured it. Rico stood back, smoothing his sleeve over the gold link.

The first black SUV came slowly around the chapel. A second followed close behind. Sunlight lay on their dark windows. Through the first windshield, I could make out men leaning forward. One turned sideways, and a gun barrel appeared beside the lowered passenger window.

Tank stopped chewing. “Down.”

The sandwich tray burst in Porsche's hands.

I caught Shawna around the waist and took us down behind the Mercedes. My knees struck first. Then my side hit the door, and pain went through me so hard I couldn't get air for a moment. Rosa fell beside us, her coat spread across the pavement.

Glass showered from the chapel doors. I heard screams and more shots, close together, and looked past the rear wheel toward the hearse.

Rico was lying beside it. One arm lay straight out from his body. Sunlight flashed from the cuff link I'd admired. Another pallbearer crawled toward him and caught his shoulder. Rico didn't turn.

Tank fired over the Mercedes's hood. Porsche dragged Mercedes down behind the rear tire as a burst tore our mirror off. It struck the ground beside Rosa, who pulled her coat away from it as if the mirror had been deliberately rude.

“Everybody inside!” I shouted.

The doors were open, but the drive between the cars and the chapel had become a place nobody could stand. Halfway down the steps, an older woman remained upright with her husband's hand in hers. He was trying to pull her down. She stared at Rico and didn't move.

I pointed them out to Tank. “Get her!”

Tank looked down at me. “You stay here.”

I pressed my back against the car. “I'm sitting, ain't I?”

Tank ran bent low across the drive. A wreath behind him jumped as bullets stripped flowers from its frame. He reached the couple, caught the old man under one arm, and pushed the woman ahead of him into the chapel. The three of them disappeared past the broken doors.

The first SUV moved toward us. Its passenger leaned out farther, trying to get an angle behind the Mercedes. I drew my pistol, braced my wrist against the tire, and fired. The man jerked back inside. The SUV clipped the fountain, swung wide, and ran straight over my empty wheelchair.

I watched a wheel spring free and roll down the drive.

“He hit my fucking chair,” I told Shawna.

She fired twice at the second SUV before ducking back beside me. “You weren't using it.”

I looked at the folded wreckage. “Still mine.”

Across the drive, the hearse began rolling.

The pallbearers had got T inside before the shooting. I could see blue through the rear glass. The driver's door stood open, and the driver lay on the pavement behind it. I couldn't tell whether he'd been hit or had dropped to avoid the bullets. The engine was still running. With nobody at the wheel, the hearse crept down the slope toward the street.

The first SUV turned after it. Its passenger jumped out and ran toward the open driver's door.

Rosa caught my sleeve. “They're taking T.”

For a second I just looked at her. I'd bought the man a coffin. I'd got him into it. There should have been a point in this process where he stopped generating fucking errands.

Shawna caught my jacket as I started to rise. “Aiden.”

I watched the gunman reach the hearse. “They don't get him.”

Shawna pulled me back enough to make me face her. “Baby, he's dead.”

I kissed her once. “Then he ain't driving himself home. Stay with the car.”

I tucked my pistol into my waistband and went across the drive. I needed both hands for the door, the man, anything that might keep me on my feet. My ribs felt as if somebody had hooked a finger beneath them and was pulling. A voice shouted my name from the chapel. Another man called for everyone to look at me. I wished one of them would look at the fucking shooter.

He had a hand on the hearse's wheel when I reached him. I caught the back of his jacket and slammed him against the open door. His gun went off above us. We stumbled alongside the moving car, his shoulder driving me into the window frame.

He was bigger than me and had arrived with all his blood inside him. That seemed to be his entire argument.

He jammed an elbow against my throat. I couldn't get a full breath. I pulled his jacket close and drove my forehead into his nose. His grip broke. Blood spread over his mouth, and I shoved him backward off the door before climbing into the driver's seat.

The hearse hit the curb while I reached for the wheel. Both front tires thumped up and down. Behind me, T's coffin struck its restraints.

“You can shut up too,” I told him.

I got the door closed and drove across the road into the cemetery.

The main drive was wide enough for the hearse, though it didn't feel that way with gravestones flashing past both windows. I kept to the gravel between the rows, hands tight on the wheel. The first SUV followed through the gate. The second had still been beside the chapel when I left; I lost sight of it as I turned between the trees. In the mirror I saw a flash inside the pursuing car's windshield, and then the hearse's rear window broke apart.

Glass scattered over the coffin. One gold handle shone through the mess.

“They're scratching your box,” I told T. “You better appreciate what this cost.”

Ahead, the drive widened around a stone circle. I took it hard, felt the hearse lean, and waited for the coffin to move. It held. Gravel rattled beneath us. In the mirror, the SUV slid sideways before its driver got the nose pointed after me again.

I took the narrower drive toward the service gate. A groundskeeper stood beside a watering truck, holding a running hose. When the hearse came at him, he stepped back so quickly he left the hose hanging in the air for a moment before dropping it.

I lifted two fingers off the wheel as I passed.

He lifted two fingers back. Water ran over his shoes while he watched us go. The man maintained a professional greeting through the entire fucking thing.

The hearse cleared the service gate onto a paved road. The SUV came after me with a bent bumper and one headlight dark. Another engine rose ahead, and I had just enough time to think they had sent a third car before the white Bentley crossed the intersection.

Shawna was driving. Her coat was off. Her white gloves held the wheel. Porsche sat beside her with her purse braced against the dash, grinning so broadly I could see it through the windshield.

Only later did Shawna tell me how they'd got clear. Our men had pinned the second SUV behind the parked cars. She'd handed her coat to Rosa while Porsche fetched the Bentley's keys, then they had driven out through the other end of the lot. Tank stayed until two men took his place at the chapel doors. Then he followed them in the Mercedes.

At that moment I knew none of it. I saw the Bentley and the two women inside it, and I saw exactly where Shawna was aiming the fucking thing.

Shawna turned toward the pursuing SUV.

“Baby, don't—” I shouted through my open window.

The Bentley hit its front corner before I finished. The SUV spun toward a low stone wall. The Bentley turned the other way, tires pouring white smoke, Porsche's door flying open and slamming shut with the spin. Then the SUV struck the wall and its hood sprang up.

I stopped the hearse beside the Bentley. Broken glass covered Shawna's lap. She looked across at me through the space where her side window had been.

“Your wife drives too,” Shawna said.

We weren't married. I found no useful reason to raise the subject. I was busy falling in love with her for the fourth fucking time before lunch.

A man got out of the SUV holding a gun. I opened my door, drew mine, and fired from behind its edge. He dropped behind the engine. His weapon hit the gutter. Another man reached out to drag him back toward the vehicle, then froze when our Mercedes appeared at the other end of the road.

Tank got out with his pistol raised. The second man's hands went up so high I thought he might leave the rest of his body behind.

“Who sent you?” Tank called.

The man looked past him at the Mercedes.

Tank advanced a step. “That's the easy question.”

The man turned back. “T had friends!”

I got out of the hearse, keeping its open door between me and the wreck. “Where the fuck were you when they passed the collection plate?”

The man looked at me, then through the hearse's shattered rear window.

“You killed him,” he said.

I pointed toward the coffin with my free hand. “And I'm burying him. Come between those things again, you can ask him how he feels about it yourself.”

His eyes dropped to the pistol in my hand. I followed his gaze and saw the blood beginning to show through my shirt. Shawna opened the Bentley's door behind me. Glass fell onto the road around her white shoes.

The man looked from me to her. “You don't stop.”

His voice had lost its shout. I thought I heard something almost admiring in it, though I wasn't interested in interviewing him about the finer points.

I held the gun steady. “You should've asked somebody before you came.”

Police cruisers arrived behind Tank. Knox got out of the first. I hadn't called her. She told me afterward that enough other people had called to describe three separate wars, each starring me.

Her officers spread along the road and took over from Tank. Guns were kicked away. The men from the SUV went face down on the pavement. I lowered my pistol when an officer reached me and pointed it safely at the ground.

Knox stopped beside the hearse. “Please tell me that's empty.”

I nodded toward the back. “Family.”

She looked through the broken window, took off her glasses, and looked again.

“You brought the deceased into a pursuit?” Knox asked.

I wiped sweat from my upper lip. “He wasn't walking.”

For three seconds she stood absolutely still. Then Knox put her hands on her hips and laughed toward the sky. One of the men in handcuffs raised his head to watch her. An officer pressed him back down, leaving the chief free to enjoy the evidence.

Knox wiped an eye beneath her glasses. “Aiden, you're wasting your fucking talents in civilian clothes.”

I looked at the blood on my civilian shirt. “You keep saying that.”

Knox put her glasses back on. “Till somebody gives me what I want.”

I secured my pistol as Shawna reached me. I took her hand and eased the torn glove back from a shallow cut. There was more blood on the cloth than the little wound seemed able to account for.

“Most of that's yours,” Shawna told me before I could ask.

I kissed the unbroken skin beside it. “I asked you to stay with the car.”

Shawna looked back at the Bentley. “I did. I moved the car.”

Porsche came around the crushed front fender and gave Tank the keys.

“Tell Leon this one had a meaningful life too,” Porsche said.

Tank looked at the wreck, then down at the key in his palm. “He gonna start sending bicycles.”

We returned T to the chapel in the same hearse, a cruiser ahead of us and Shawna beside me. I drove slowly. With the shooting finished, I could feel every movement I'd made catching up to my body. Shawna kept one hand on my knee, and I tried to breathe without showing her how carefully I had to do it.

The second SUV stood where I'd last seen it. Its doors were open now. Men sat along the curb with their hands behind them while officers watched over them. Medical workers moved through the guests on the steps.

People came out when they heard the engines. A woman on the steps clapped above her head until her hat slid crooked. Somebody shouted my name. I stopped by the curb and waited a moment before opening the door. Then I lowered my feet to the pavement and pulled myself upright against the doorframe.

The older couple Tank had brought inside came toward us. The man leaned on his wife's arm now. He told me he was seventy and had never seen anything like it. Then his wife took my face in both hands.

“You brought everybody home,” she told me.

I looked beyond her shoulder. A white sheet lay beside the curb where Rico had fallen. The breeze lifted one edge and put it down again.

Not everybody.

Tank came close and told me Vernon had died inside after the men carried him in. Forty-one. Two people gone while I had been racing around with somebody who was already dead.

I held the woman's hands gently and lowered them from my face. I couldn't explain what her gratitude had just done to me, and I didn't want to make her regret surviving.

“Help me finish this,” I told Tank.

The funeral workers brought a replacement hearse alongside ours. There was glass all over T's coffin. One man brushed it away with a folded cloth while the rest of us took our places. Tank and I stood on one side. Four other men joined us at the handles.

We lifted together. My side pulled. I held on.

The musicians started the hymn again while we carried T between the cars. The first few notes wavered. Then the voices came in, and people stopped talking long enough for us to lay him down.

Nobody interrupted him that time.

Tank told me later they buried T that afternoon. I wasn't at the graveside. By then, Vega had me upstairs at the house with my ruined shirt open, and the look on her face had persuaded every armed man in the hallway to discover an urgent duty elsewhere.

Shawna sat on the edge of the bed in her dirty white dress. Vega stood over me, removing the dressing.

“You were told to rest,” Vega said.

I watched her hands. “I was sitting at a funeral.”

Vega looked up. “I'm interested in what happened after the sitting.”

I turned my head toward the window. That was the only part of me I could move without inviting further discussion.

Vega cleaned me up and wrote out her instructions. She put the paper on the nightstand and tapped it with her pen.

“If this gets worse, he comes back under my care,” Vega told Shawna. “Not after another event. Not when he finishes a telephone call.”

I opened my mouth. Vega held up one finger without looking at me. I closed it again.

Shawna leaned over to read the paper. “Say that last part again.”

I lay between them while they discussed my body like a damaged car neither one trusted me to drive.

When Vega left, Tank came in with the names. Rico and Vernon. The others who'd been hurt. He made me repeat them, and I did.

I reached toward the phone. “I'll pay for everything.”

Tank put it in my hand. “Their mothers need a human being first.”

So I called.

Rico's mother answered after several rings. I gave my name and sat straighter against the pillows.

“Rico was at the funeral with me,” I told her. “I'm sorry.”

“They told me,” his mother said.

I looked down at the blanket. Somebody laughed in the hall before a door closed over the sound.

“Was he by himself?” Rico's mother asked.

I closed my hand around a fold of the blanket. “One of the men carrying the coffin went right to him. I saw him reach him.”

She drew a breath. I waited while she cried. Shawna moved her hand beside mine without trying to open my fist.

“Showed me his cuff links,” I told Rico's mother when she grew quieter. “The ones his daughter sent. He was wearing them.”

“Called me about those,” she said.

I shut my eyes. “He looked good.”

She began telling me about him. I stayed on the line. When she went back to something she'd already said, I listened again. My hand had opened beside Shawna's before the call ended.

Vernon's mother made me give my name twice. She wanted to know why her son had gone to a funeral and why anybody had brought guns. I told her what I'd seen. There were things I couldn't answer. She kept asking them, and I kept the phone against my ear. I didn't tell her about the hearse or how many people had thanked me. None of that could have given her a son to answer for himself.

After the second call ended, I threw the phone against the wall.

It struck below a painting and fell to the carpet. Shawna watched it land. Tank picked it up, set it on the dresser, and left us alone.

“I want Red,” I told Shawna.

She turned toward me. “You don't know it was him.”

I pushed myself up against the pillows. “Who the fuck else?”

Shawna folded her gloves together. “People loved T.”

I looked toward the door Tank had closed. “I paid for his funeral.”

Shawna kept her voice level. “You also killed him.”

I wanted her angry beside me. Instead she had said something true, and I hated the truth enough to reach for the worst thing I could accuse her of.

“You defending Red now?” I asked.

Shawna set the gloves down. Her mouth became a straight line.

“Say that again,” she told me.

I didn't.

She opened the clasp of Dante's watch and laid it on the bed between us. The face pointed upward. I could hear its bracelet settle against itself.

Shawna rested a finger beside it. “You think I forgot what those men did?”

I looked at the watch.

“House and earrings,” Shawna said. “That's what you think it took? Now I don't remember where they left him?”

I shook my head. “No.”

Shawna picked the watch up. “Then don't talk to me like I'm sleeping through this.”

Her fingers shook while she tried to close the clasp. I reached toward her hand and stopped short of touching it. She held her wrist out. I fastened the watch, carefully, feeling how little force it took to help with something I couldn't bear to see.

“I'm scared,” I said.

Shawna sat still beside me.

I'd rather have taken another punch than repeat it. I looked down at the white dress bunched against her knees and saw her on the pavement again, glass falling around her head.

I kept my fingers beneath her wrist. “I'm scared they get you while I'm laying here.”

Shawna shifted closer on the mattress. “Then stop trying to stand up every time somebody says your name.”

I bent my forehead to hers. For a while we stayed like that, with the watch between our hands and the curtains moving in the air from the vent.

Outside the bedroom, Rosa ordered Tank to eat something. His answer came through the door, low and tired: he wasn't hungry.

“I didn't ask for your fucking feelings about the sandwich,” Rosa called back.

Shawna let out one small laugh through her nose. I felt it against my cheek.

I held on to her.

At sunset, Tank returned carrying a black envelope on a silver tray. He told us a man from the Saint had left it at the gate. I recognized the tray from the kitchen and watched him bring it toward the bed with both hands beneath it.

“Why you serving me a threat?” I asked Tank.

He lowered the tray onto my knees. “Didn't want to touch it more than I had to.”

I took the envelope. Inside was a breakfast invitation printed in gold: tomorrow morning, penthouse at the Saint. My name and Shawna's sat together on the card as if somebody had been delighted to arrange our company.

A smaller card lay behind it.

I read the handwriting once. Then I passed it to Shawna.

She had been standing beside the dresser. She sat down so abruptly that the mattress moved beneath me. Her eyes stayed on the card while her thumb found Dante's watch.

Tank stepped closer. “What?”

Shawna didn't answer. Neither did I. I watched her thumb stop against the metal and thought about the photograph now buried with T, his hand on his brother's shoulder.

Red had written one sentence.

*Dante didn't die for three million.*

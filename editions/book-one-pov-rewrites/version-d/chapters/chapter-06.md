## 6. Forty Million Reasons to Die

I went to see Red the morning after the funeral with a bullet in my body and a diamond pin covering the hole in my coat.

I had better coats. Wearing that one felt like an answer, though I hadn't decided which question I wanted him to ask. Tank drove. I sat beside him with Red's little card open on my knee and read the same sentence I'd been reading all night.

*Dante didn't die for three million.*

Red ink. The motherfucker had coordinated the threat with his name. I'd lain beside Shawna wondering what she hadn't told me, then hated myself for letting his handwriting into our bed. By morning I had resolved to look like a man who hadn't thought about him at all.

Tank glanced at the card. “You sleep?”

I turned it facedown. “I can dream about firing you with my eyes open.”

Tank opened the console at a light and handed me the bottle Vega had left. “Take what she said.”

I put it in my pocket. “I heard the doctor.”

Tank returned his hand to the wheel. “Then listen to her.”

He wore black again. Yesterday he'd carried a coffin. Today he was driving me toward a man who had sent flowers to it. A cut crossed his knuckles. I didn't remember seeing it happen, and I realized there were whole pieces of the previous day I'd never asked him about.

At the Saint, the valet had barely reached our car before two other men stepped past him. I opened my door slowly and stood with the card in my hand. One of the men looked at the shape beneath my jacket and held his palm out.

I offered him the card. “You can have the note.”

He left his hand where it was.

I nodded toward the building. “Your boss sent for me. He know what I come with.”

The man looked over his shoulder. Red stood inside the glass entrance in a cream suit. He raised one finger. Both men moved aside.

Red came forward as I reached the door. “Aiden. Glad you came.”

I tucked the note into my jacket. “Would've been sad if I didn't?”

Red took my hand. “I had breakfast made.”

I let him keep the handshake for a moment. “Then feed me before the conversation gets hungry.”

He put an arm around my shoulders and guided me inside. I let that happen too. I wanted to know how close he would come to a man who had killed his partner. Close enough to squeeze my shoulder, as it turned out. Close enough for his cologne to follow me after he took his hand away.

We passed through a room with a white piano by the windows. There were fresh flowers on its polished lid and a photograph of Shawna singing on the wall above it. Red slowed beneath the picture. I could have kept walking, but he had arranged the little pause so neatly that I stopped and looked.

The Shawna in the photograph held a microphone in both hands. She wore a dress I hadn't seen. The man beside me owned the wall, the piano and the photograph. I felt the deliberate placement of all three as plainly as a finger against my chest.

Red looked at the piano. “You play?”

I glanced behind it. “Breakfast back there?”

Red smiled. “I wondered what you brought to a woman who already sings.”

Tank closed his eyes behind me. “Please.”

I looked back at him. “You got a request?”

Tank opened one eye. “Eggs. Before the niggas serving them retire.”

I pulled out the piano bench.

There was sheet music on the stand with Shawna's name at the top. I sat carefully and drew it closer. The introduction ran across crowded staves, both hands busy, inner notes tucked where a careless player would lose them. Red stood behind my right shoulder while I looked. I could feel him there without turning.

“Difficult arrangement,” Red said.

I set my fingers on the keys. “Haven't heard your kitchen yet. Everything difficult till somebody does it.”

The first chord filled the room. A waiter carrying coffee stopped by the door.

My hands remembered before the rest of me relaxed. A church piano with two bad keys. An old woman tapping my wrist until I quit trying to pound the sound into it. Staying after everybody else had gone because there would be supper if I played while she put the room away. I had learned what an open score could give me long before I could afford anything in this room.

I played the introduction as written. Then I took the melody into my right hand and let the left answer it with something that had been downstairs after church. The room's expensive little silence broke apart. I could hear the sound returning from the windows, clean and bright above the lower notes.

Tank opened both eyes. “Oh, this nigga.”

Red turned toward him. “Something wrong?”

Tank pointed at me, then the keys. “With him? All morning. With that? Listen.”

The coffee waiter shifted his weight in time. He stopped moving his shoulders when Red looked toward him, but one foot kept tapping. I put a little bounce beneath the melody. His heel caught it, his knee joined in, and he glanced down at his own leg as if it had sold him out.

Red moved beside the bench. “You just read that?”

I turned a page. “You left it open.”

Red looked up at the photograph. “Shawna never said—”

I kept playing. “You want music or witness statements?”

A man at the door laughed. Red turned. The man straightened so quickly that I nearly lost the next measure enjoying him.

I finished the piece and lifted my hands. For a moment the last chord hung in the room without anybody helping it. Then one waiter clapped. He looked at Red, looked back at me, and clapped again.

The others joined him.

Tank spread both hands toward the piano. “You ordered that. Asked him what he brought. He unpacked.”

Red waited for the applause to end. “She never mentioned you played.”

I closed the cover gently. “I keep giving her things to find out. Ought to try it.”

Tank caught my elbow as I rose. Pain had started pulling beneath the bandage while I was playing, and he got there just before it showed on my face.

“Breakfast,” Tank said.

I let him steady me. “Hungry?”

Tank guided me toward the lift. “I've lived two years since we parked.”

Upstairs, the penthouse looked scrubbed clean of the night Shawna and I had left it. No overturned furniture. No broken glass. I looked toward the place where she'd stood holding the bag, then followed Red through the terrace doors.

Six waiters surrounded a table set for three. Beyond the railing, the city spread out in the morning sun. Roses stood in narrow crystal vases. The only red flower had been placed beside my plate.

Tank picked it up before sitting.

“Don't eat that,” I told him.

Tank looked at the rose, then at me. “Wasn't going to.”

Red laughed. He had dimples. I hated learning that.

I lowered myself into the chair and shifted to take pressure off my side. Red's eyes followed the movement.

“T knew where to put a bullet,” Red said.

I settled both hands on the table. “Didn't know where to keep his own.”

The waiter filling my water glass stopped with the pitcher tilted. A drop struck the tablecloth. Red raised his orange juice.

“Our absent friend,” he said.

I drank water. Tank set the rose down and left his glass alone. When Red offered him the bacon, though, he took two pieces before looking down at his plate as if breakfast had betrayed him personally.

Red cut into his food. “Where's Shawna?”

I watched his knife. “With people who like her alive.”

Red raised his eyes to mine. “Always liked her alive.”

I leaned back enough to breathe. “Expensive way of showing it.”

Red laid down the fork. “I bought the dress she was wearing when you met her.”

I moved forward too fast. Pain ran through my side, and I held the edge of the table beneath the cloth until it passed.

“Want me to send you the motherfucking dress?” I asked him.

Red rested his hands beside his plate. “I want you to understand how little you understand.”

I let go of the table. “Then use the invitation for something. Explain.”

Red smiled again. “Valentino dies. I give Shawna a room. Protect her from people who might shorten her life over what she did to him. Then my partner arranges to take her and three million out of my house.”

He paused and looked toward Tank. Tank kept his eyes on him.

Red returned to me. “My partner dies. You get his business. His car. His necklace. The woman. Fortunate week.”

I touched the diamond pin on my coat. “Forgot the bullet.”

Red glanced beneath it. “I can see you haven't.”

Tank's chair scraped backward. I raised a hand toward him without turning. He stopped.

I looked straight at Red. “You think I planned that?”

Red picked up his glass. “I think you're young enough to tell the truth when it pays.”

I kept my hand raised until Tank sat back. “Here's some for free. Send another man after her, I'll come without an invitation.”

Red wiped his mouth. He was thirty-six; I'd seen that in a business profile. A waiter older than him approached with more juice. Red lifted a finger without looking back, and the waiter stopped where he was. Another man stood ready to clear the plate. Neither moved until Red laid down his napkin. I watched the little finger return to the table and wished I could make him ask for something instead of pointing at it.

“You can keep T's streets,” Red said. “Generous beginning.”

I looked down at my plate. “Can keep what's mine? Damn. Put a bow on it.”

Red folded his napkin. “The money comes back. Shawna comes home. We stop putting people in the ground.”

I met his eyes. “Which one you want back? T? Valentino? Dante? You got a favorite?”

The smile left his face at Dante's name.

I sat back. Pain made the movement slow enough to look deliberate. For once, the bullet contributed something.

I took his card out of my jacket and laid it beside my plate. “You wrote me a note. Finish it.”

Red looked toward the waiters. One opened the terrace door. The others followed him inside, and Red waited until the last door closed.

“Eight years ago,” Red said, “I bought something extraordinary from a man who didn't appreciate the opportunity.”

I kept my hand on the card. “What man?”

Red turned his glass slowly against the tablecloth. “Marcellus Vale. Jeweler. Violent when disappointed.”

Tank's fingers tightened around his water glass. I saw it because his wrist moved beside my plate. I looked at him, but he kept watching Red.

Red went on. “His masterpiece was called Blue Mercy. A large blue diamond. Around forty million dollars, depending on who's asking.”

I had been pretending all morning that nothing in that house could surprise me. Forty million kicked the door off that performance.

“Forty million for one rock?” I asked.

Red held up a finger. “One.”

I stared at it. “For forty million, that bitch better call when I'm lonely.”

Red lowered his hand. “Made quite a few men lonely.”

I stopped looking at his finger. “What does Dante have to do with it?”

Red pushed his plate away. “Met Vale in prison. Came home with unfortunate ideas. Decided the money in my accounts belonged to a woman he'd never met.”

I felt my jaw tighten. “What woman?”

Red looked directly at me. “Vale's dead wife.”

He let that sit between us. I thought of the watch on Shawna's wrist, the song she'd sung at my club, the picture she had put into T's coffin. Red took another drink.

“Men do peculiar things when they love dead women,” Red said. “Best avoid the habit.”

I stood, pushing the chair back with my knees. Tank rose beside me.

I leaned over the table. “Tell me what you know about Dante. Keep talking about my woman, this breakfast gets expensive.”

Red stayed in his chair. “Shawna knows the story.”

I shook my head. “No.”

Red's voice stayed soft. “Ask her.”

I felt Tank's hand near my elbow and moved it away. “I did.”

Red drew an envelope from beside his plate. “Ask again.”

He pushed it across the table. I opened it standing up. Two invitations lay inside, heavy blue paper with silver edges. Shawna's name came before mine.

“The diamond will be the centerpiece of my gala,” Red said. “Three nights from now. Thought she'd like to see what her husband died chasing.”

I looked from the printed names to his face. “Had these made before the funeral?”

Red spread a hand. “I print plenty of invitations.”

I put the cards back in their envelope. “Print one for yours.” I picked up the handwritten note too and put all of it inside my jacket.

Tank touched my elbow. I pulled away. I wanted Red to see me walking out under my own power, even if my own power was presently borrowing against tomorrow's ability to stand.

At the terrace door, Red spoke behind me.

“Tell her I still have the piano,” Red called.

I turned. He had picked up his fork again.

Red looked at me over it. “The white one. She'll know.”

I went back across the terrace. Tank said my name once. I kept walking until I reached Red's vase. I pulled one rose halfway out, caught its stem between my fingers, and lifted the heavy crystal with my other hand.

Water ran over my wrist. I swung the vase over the railing and let it go. The rose stayed in my fist with its stem snapped crooked.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then glass broke below us and a woman screamed.

Red got out of his chair and came to the railing. He looked down. I watched his cream jacket tighten across his shoulders as he gripped the stone edge.

“That's my fucking car,” Red said.

I passed his chair on the way to the door. “Tell it I still have the vase.”

I laid the wet, broken rose stem on his plate and left the flower beside his eggs.

Downstairs, his red Aston Martin had a hole in the roof.

The valet stood beside it holding a towel. He dabbed one patch of water on the door, looked at the hole in the roof, and stopped. Rose petals clung to the glass. A piece of crystal large enough to be furniture sat where the driver would have put his head.

“Mr. Red's going to be upset,” the valet told me.

I looked up toward the terrace. “Got a head start. Where's mine?”

Tank went to our Mercedes. Its windows had been replaced, the body repaired and polished between the attacks. I could see myself approaching in the door, a little bent and trying not to show it. I loved that car. Every time somebody fixed it, it looked willing to meet me again.

A man came out from the next hotel carrying a motorcycle helmet. He stopped when he saw the Aston Martin, then turned toward me.

“You Aiden?” the man asked.

I paused beside my door. “Depends who's hoping.”

He came closer, grinning. “You flew over a boat.”

I glanced at Tank. “Word getting around.”

The man introduced himself as Saint, then corrected it to Little Saint. Thirty-one, he told me, with a scar running down his chin and a silver eagle spread across the back of his leather jacket. He pointed toward a black motorcycle parked beyond the valet stand.

“I ride,” Little Saint said.

I looked at the bike. “Good for you.”

Little Saint shook his head, still smiling. “I mean I ride. Thought you ought to know.”

Tank opened my door. “Everybody got something he ought to know today.”

Little Saint looked past us. The smile left his face.

“Then know those ain't valets,” Little Saint said.

A gray sports car had stopped sideways across the exit. Behind it, a black SUV rolled toward the entrance with its windows lowering. The front passenger held a pistol against his chest. I could see no badge, no hotel uniform, nothing that told me which dead man or living man had purchased his morning.

Tank put a hand against my shoulder. “Inside.”

The first bullet struck the Mercedes's open door while his other hand was still on it. A second shattered the window above his fingers.

I caught the valet's collar and pulled him behind the car. Glass broke in the revolving entrance behind us. People dropped behind luggage carts and planters. Little Saint ran toward his motorcycle while Tank fired from beside my shoulder.

I folded myself into the passenger seat. Tank came around the hood, dropped behind the wheel, and put the car in reverse before his door had shut. The open door swung hard as we backed away from the gray car blocking the exit. He caught the door and hauled it closed. I pulled mine shut and braced my hand against the dash.

Tank stopped, turned the wheel toward the gap beside the gray car, and drove forward into its front corner. It shifted but didn't clear the exit. Its driver shouted something I couldn't hear through the glass. Tank backed up far enough to hit him again, then forced us through the wider gap before the man could close it.

Metal scraped down my door. Behind us, I caught a glimpse of Little Saint fastening his helmet strap as he kicked the motorcycle upright.

“Somebody really hates breakfast,” I told Tank.

Tank turned the wheel. “Head down.”

I slid lower in the seat. “Need it where I can see.”

Tank looked past the mirror. “See lower.”

The gray car followed with a loose fender beating against one wheel. The SUV came behind it. A shot struck the back of the Mercedes. Another took out the rear glass. I heard the pieces hit the seat behind my head and crouched until I could see the following cars through the mirror without sitting upright.

Tank swung around the first corner. A taxi braked across our path. He missed its front bumper by inches and put us into a narrow street lined with delivery trucks. The gray car made the turn. Behind it, the SUV clipped a streetlight. I saw the pole bend in the mirror and heard the metal strike pavement as the vehicle kept coming.

Little Saint appeared beside my window on the motorcycle. His visor was up. He pointed ahead, laughing loudly enough for me to hear him over the engines. I couldn't tell what he found funniest. I was mostly concerned with the tour bus blocking the next intersection.

Its driver stood outside arguing with a man in a yellow vest. Neither looked toward us until I hit the horn.

“Tank!” I shouted.

Tank's foot was already on the brake. “I see it!”

I caught the handle above my door. “Keep hearing that right before we hit something!”

The Mercedes slid. The gray car couldn't stop behind us. It struck our rear quarter and swung us sideways, turning my window toward its front bumper. For a moment I looked directly at its driver. His hands climbed over each other on the wheel as he tried to turn away.

I drew my pistol and fired through the broken side glass. His windshield starred white. He ducked, and the gray car ran into the bus's open luggage compartment with its whole nose.

The bus driver abandoned his argument and ran, still holding up one finger at the man in the yellow vest. Apparently they weren't finished. I could see one pink suitcase jumping inside the torn compartment while Tank found the gap beside the bus and drove us into it. Our door scraped along the bus panels. I held my arm in and kept the gun pointed down until we emerged onto the open road.

I secured the pistol in my waistband. Little Saint was beside us again. He raised a fist. I raised mine back.

Then the SUV hit his rear wheel.

The motorcycle snapped sideways. Little Saint came off it, rolled across the hood of a parked car, and fell into the street. His bike slid ahead in a shower of sparks. I saw his helmet strike the pavement before he got an arm beneath him.

Tank swore and braked. I opened my door while we were still slowing.

“Where the fuck are you going?” Tank shouted.

I was already outside. “Get him!”

The SUV turned toward Little Saint. I ran across the road and reached him as he pushed himself onto one elbow. His visor had cracked. Blood ran from beneath the helmet down his cheek. He looked up at me, then at the car coming toward us.

“Your leg?” I asked him.

Little Saint pulled one knee beneath him. “Works.”

I put his arm over my shoulders. “Work it with me.”

We stumbled toward the parked trucks. His first step nearly took us both down. I tightened my hold, felt the wound in my side pull, and kept moving. The SUV's engine grew louder behind us.

Our Mercedes shot across its path.

The crash sounded close enough to happen inside my head. I pushed Little Saint behind a delivery truck and turned. The SUV had stopped against Tank's car. Steam or smoke rose between the hoods. I didn't care which until I could see Tank move.

His door opened. He got out, crossed to the SUV's driver, and caught the man's door as it began to swing toward him. Tank slammed it shut again. The driver folded inward. Tank reached through the window, broken in the crash, and hauled him out over its lower edge.

I drew my pistol again and moved far enough past the truck to cover the passenger. He was still holding his gun, looking from me to Tank with his mouth open.

“Keep breathing,” I told the passenger. “That's all you getting today.”

He dropped the gun into the footwell and put both hands on the dashboard. I stayed where I could see them while Tank held the driver against the SUV.

Little Saint came out from behind the truck. His jacket was torn at the shoulder. He lifted his helmet off carefully and looked at me with blood drying along his jaw.

“You came back,” Little Saint said.

I kept one eye on the passenger. “Told me you ride. Ain't seen enough yet.”

Little Saint looked down for a moment. When he raised his face, the grin came slowly.

“Any road,” Little Saint told me. “Any fucking road, Aiden. You call, I'm there.”

A crowd had gathered by the corner. A woman held up her phone and called my name, asking me to look toward her. I turned enough to give her my good side. If Vegas intended to put every mistake I made on a screen, I could at least participate in the lighting.

Knox's officers arrived before the cars stopped smoking. One took charge of the passenger while another cuffed the driver Tank was holding. A second patrol stopped farther down the street beside the sports car jammed into the bus. I watched an officer pull its driver clear of the wreck. Then I lowered my gun and stepped back. A young officer looked from the SUV to me and shook his head.

“Chief said you were something,” the officer told me.

I leaned against the truck. “Chief been paying attention.”

Tank called for a car to take Little Saint. I waited until it arrived and watched him lower himself into the back seat, his damaged helmet in his lap. He raised two fingers before the door closed.

Our Mercedes still ran. Tank examined the door that wouldn't latch, took off his belt, and tied it shut from inside. I put my pistol away and climbed in on the other side. People watched us leave, one man jogging a few steps after us before running out of things to shout.

Before we reached the highway, I called Shawna.

“Baby, you want lunch?” I asked.

Shawna's voice came back sharp and clear. “Why there sirens behind you?”

I looked at Tank's belt holding his door. “They wanted breakfast. I'm thinking ahead.”

At home, Shawna stood barefoot on a little fitting platform beside the piano while a woman pinned a silver dress around her hips. Rosa sat beside a tray of sewing supplies, holding a strip of ribbon against the fabric. The afternoon light touched the dress every time Shawna moved, and for a moment I wanted everyone else out of the room so I could look at her without sharing the view.

Then I saw the watch.

Dante's watch. Dante's wife. A forty-million-dollar stone connected to Dante that she hadn't mentioned. I had driven home carrying Red's account of him, and suddenly every beautiful thing in my house seemed to have arrived through somebody else's life first.

Rosa looked up. “Why you got that face?”

I stopped in the doorway. “What face?”

Rosa lowered the ribbon and pointed at me. “The one gonna make this dress cost more.”

I took the invitations from my jacket. “Blue Mercy.”

Shawna stopped moving. The seamstress kept working until Rosa touched her shoulder.

“Let's get coffee,” Rosa told the woman.

The seamstress looked at the pins still in the dress. “I don't drink coffee.”

Rosa helped her to her feet. “Good fucking day to start.”

She took the tray so the woman wouldn't have to reach back for anything. They left through the side door. Shawna stepped off the platform, holding the skirt clear of her feet. A pin fell onto the floor between us. I heard its little click and hated how quiet the room had become.

“What did he tell you?” Shawna asked.

I laughed once. It sounded ugly even to me.

“Everybody asks that,” I told her. “What she tell you? What he tell you? What the dead man tell the other dead man? How about you tell me something?”

Shawna drew back a little. I saw it and knew I ought to stop long enough to change the way I was speaking. I didn't.

I held up the blue cards. “You knew about forty million?”

Shawna looked at them. “I knew about the stone.”

I let my hand fall. “Didn't think it mattered?”

Shawna took a breath. “I thought you being shot mattered. Thought getting you through yesterday mattered.”

I glanced at the silver cloth gathered in her hand. “Time to pick a dress, though.”

She let the skirt go. It fell around her ankles.

“I picked it because I wanted one night,” Shawna said, “where you looked at me and didn't see somebody else bleeding.”

I looked at the dress properly then. Pins caught the light along one side. She stood barefoot in the middle of the room while I held the price of a diamond between us as if that were the only thing worth asking about.

I had no answer ready.

Shawna came close and took the invitation from my hand. Her thumb moved across Red's printed name.

“He tell you about his piano?” Shawna asked.

I looked toward our own black piano by the water. “Told me. Tell everybody?”

Shawna shook her head. “I smashed the keys with a bottle the night I left. He bought another one. Now he can act like I'm coming back to finish the song.”

I remembered Red standing beside the white piano, waiting for me to look at the photograph. Remembered his voice telling me what he still had. I had let him put a whole relationship into one piece of furniture, and she had already broken the fucking thing.

I looked back at Shawna. “He said you knew what Dante died for.”

She held the invitation against her thigh. “I know what Dante told me.”

I rested a hand on the back of a chair. “Tell me.”

Shawna glanced at the chair. “Starts before me.”

I couldn't keep the bitterness out. “Everything in this house starts before me.”

Her eyes lowered to the cross visible through my open jacket.

“Want me to take that back?” Shawna asked.

My hand closed around it before I answered. “No.”

Shawna touched the chair beside me. “Then sit down, Aiden.”

I sat. The relief in my side made it impossible to pretend I hadn't needed to. Shawna picked up the fallen pin and laid it on the piano before pulling another chair close. She arranged the unfinished dress over her knees, keeping the pins away from me.

“Dante told me after we got married,” Shawna said. “We were in the room Red paid for. I thought we'd go downstairs and drink with everybody. He locked the door.”

My jaw tightened. I made myself loosen my hand around the cross.

“I'm listening,” I told her.

Shawna took my hand off the chain and held it between hers.

“He said there was something I ought to know,” she told me. “Before I decided what kind of man I'd married.”

She looked down at our hands, then back at me.

“Then he told me about the old man,” Shawna said.

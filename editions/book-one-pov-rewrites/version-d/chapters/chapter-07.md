## 7. The Man Who Cut the Heart Out of a Mountain

Before Shawna got much farther, my body interrupted her.

I shifted in the chair and couldn't keep the pain off my face. Shawna called Rosa back. Tank took one look at me and put a hand beneath my arm. Between the three of them, they got me upstairs before I could make sitting down difficult for anybody else.

Tank draped my coat over a chair and helped me onto the bed. Rosa took Shawna into the bathroom. I heard a pin strike the sink, then Shawna asking how many were still in the dress.

“Enough to keep you standing still,” Rosa answered through the door.

Rosa emerged carrying the silver dress, and Shawna came back in a robe. By then I was propped against the pillows with one leg straight and the other bent because apparently that was the arrangement under which my ribs would permit information.

Shawna put the blue invitations on the bedside table and sat beside me. Dante's watch rested loose against her hand.

She told me their wedding room had rose petals all over the bed. Dante had begun gathering them into the ice bucket as soon as he locked the door.

“I asked if they were bothering him,” Shawna said. “He said they had dirt on them.”

She smiled a little at the memory, then repeated his answer in her own quiet voice: Dante hadn't wanted her sleeping in dirt. She had laughed and kicked off her shoes. According to her, he watched the shoes land as though he hadn't quite believed she was going to stay.

That fucked with me more than the picture of the wedding bed. I had looked at things she set down in my house the same way. The watch beside a lamp. A bag left open on a chair. Little proofs that she might mean to be there in the morning.

I asked her to go on.

Dante had sat beside the ice bucket and told her about prison. Nine months of his life, a little more than three years before the afternoon when I heard the story. He had been twenty-eight. Shawna made a point of giving me the age, as if she wanted me to see a man then rather than only the one she had buried.

His first afternoon, he saw another prisoner spill soup down an older man's shirt.

The older man looked down at the stain and kept eating. The man who'd done it stayed beside his table. Dante told Shawna the silence bothered him before the violence did: a whole room carrying on as if everyone already knew which person was allowed to do this to which other person.

Shawna described the bully leaning over the older prisoner's bowl, telling him to look up. When the old man finally put down his spoon, he pointed to the bully's hand.

“You have my soup on your hand,” Shawna quoted him.

The man slapped him.

Shawna drew a breath. “Dante said he got up. Next thing he remembered, he had the man against the wall and blood on his shirt. He was telling somebody to bring the old man another bowl.”

I looked at her. “What happened between those things?”

Shawna gestured from one end of the bed to the other. “I told you what happened.”

I shook my head. “Told me somebody got hit. Skipped where the nigga found out.”

She stared at me for a moment. Then Shawna settled back against the pillows and pulled her robe clear of her knees.

“The man had two friends,” she told me.

I made myself comfortable enough to pay the proper attention. “There we go.”

One friend moved into the aisle when Dante crossed between the tables. Dante described him to Shawna as a big, shaved-headed man of forty, with a towel over his shoulder. The man planted himself in Dante's way and ordered him to sit down.

Dante kept coming. The man asked whether he was deaf.

“Dante told him no,” Shawna said. “Then asked if he was finished.”

The man swung. His fist caught Dante across the jaw. Dante still had his food tray in both hands, and he drove its edge into the man's face. The man folded over the end of a bench with both hands against his nose. Food flew across the aisle. A cup fell beneath somebody's shoe. Men stood around them, benches scraping the floor.

The soup-spiller grabbed the older man's collar and pulled him halfway off the seat. Now the table lay between Dante and the two of them. Shawna held one hand out to show me the distance, then lifted it across the bed.

“He went over it,” she said.

In my head, I saw his feet leave the floor. I knew that stupid certainty: once you decided you were getting to somebody, the furniture could work out its feelings afterward.

I didn't enjoy finding that much of myself in her husband. I listened anyway.

Dante hit the man holding the prisoner with both knees. They went down together. The older man fell against the bench, steadied himself, and reached beneath the table for his spoon.

“He got the spoon?” I asked Shawna.

She nodded. “He liked that spoon.”

I rested my hand over the cross on my chest. “Respect an attachment.”

The last man came behind Dante with a chair raised. According to Dante's account, the older prisoner put his foot against the man's ankle as he passed. The man's step broke. His chair struck the table instead of Dante's head, and Dante turned into him before he could lift it again.

Shawna showed me how Dante said he'd caught the chair. One hand against its back, the other closing where a leg met the seat. He tore it sideways. The man held on, so Dante drove it back into his ribs and shoved him against the table. Dante had broken a finger somewhere in the fight. He hadn't known which movement did it until the guards were holding him and it began to hurt.

The man who'd spilled the soup tried to rise. Dante hauled him up the rest of the way and put him against the wall. That was the part Shawna had given me first: blood on Dante's shirt, his hand at the man's collar, his voice carrying across the room asking for another fucking bowl.

The guards arrived. Dante let the bully drop. Three men were on or near the floor, and the old prisoner stood by his table with the spoon in his hand.

Shawna told me a guard ordered Dante toward the door.

“He said the old man hadn't eaten,” she said. “The guard told him to move again.”

The older prisoner touched Dante's shoulder and said he could wait. Dante looked at the empty bowl, picked it up, and put it into the guard's hand.

“So can they,” Shawna quoted Dante, nodding toward the place she'd used to indicate the men on the floor.

She smiled after saying it. I let her have the smile. Her dead man had earned that much.

I held up three fingers. “Three.”

Shawna looked at my hand. “Three.”

I lowered it. “I had twelve.”

She tapped my good shoulder with a pillow, gently enough that the bullet declined to comment.

“You asked for the story,” Shawna reminded me.

I caught the pillow before she could put it back. “Can listen and hold the record.”

She laughed. I put the pillow behind her, and when she leaned against it, her shoulder rested against mine. For a moment, hearing about Dante didn't feel like being pushed out of my own bed.

The fight had bought him punishment and a friendship.

The old man's name was Marcellus Vale.

When Dante returned, Marcellus sat on his bunk with a little paper box. Inside were a broken comb, some buttons, and a gold-colored wrapper folded into a heart. He offered Dante the heart for his trouble.

Shawna made the shape between her fingers. “Dante asked what he was supposed to do with it. Marcellus told him to give it to somebody.”

Dante had asked whether he meant a woman. Marcellus said that depended on whether Dante liked women. Dante assured him that he did.

Shawna lifted her chin slightly as she repeated the old man's answer. “Then perhaps one will like a heart.”

I looked at her hands. I could picture the little folded wrapper more easily than forty million dollars. Somebody confined to a room had still found a way to make a gift with what the room allowed him.

The folds were so fine that Dante asked whether Marcellus made jewelry. Marcellus said he used to. Dante admitted that he used to steal it.

“Then we may have met professionally,” Shawna quoted Marcellus.

Dante laughed. The old man shifted along the bunk so he could sit down.

They ate together for two months. Marcellus taught him cards. Dante offered advice about which men in the prison were dangerous and which ones mainly needed other people to look frightened. Shawna said Marcellus listened, nodded, and sometimes greeted those same men by name when they passed. I asked her how long he'd already been inside.

“Years,” she told me.

I shook my head at the ceiling. “Dante gave him the tour anyway?”

Shawna rested her head against the pillow. “You never told somebody something they already knew?”

I looked back at her. “Not in the last few seconds.”

She went on before I could improve that record.

At night, Dante saw Marcellus holding a photograph where the corridor light reached his bunk. The picture showed a woman on a little stage, singing with her eyes closed. A blue stone rested at the base of her throat.

Celeste. His wife.

When Dante said she was beautiful, Marcellus said he knew. When Dante asked whether she still visited, Marcellus turned the photograph over and answered no.

Dante left it alone until a letter arrived. Marcellus carried the unopened envelope through dinner. Dante offered to read it for him, but the old man said he already knew what it said.

Shawna glanced toward the invitations on my bedside table. “Dante asked why they sent it, then.”

She gave me Marcellus's answer slowly: some people thought they could finish a love affair by closing a bank account.

Celeste had died three months earlier. Dante learned that sitting on the opposite bunk, looking at an envelope the old man couldn't bring himself to open. It wasn't the letter from Celeste that I would read later. It was one more official piece of business arriving after the person who might have made it bearable was gone.

After a while, Dante asked him about the stone in the photograph.

What came next was Marcellus's account, told to Dante in that cell and repeated to Shawna in their wedding room. She kept his watch between her hands while she passed it on to me.

The man who sold Marcellus the rough stone brought it in an empty cigarette packet. Marcellus paid enough to empty his account. Then two men stopped him on the way out, one with a hand extended and the other blocking the doorway.

Shawna told me how Marcellus had buttoned his coat over the packet. The first man claimed a share. Marcellus told him that if he put his hand inside the coat, he'd need help using it afterward.

“He put it away,” Shawna said. “The other one hit him.”

The punch caught Marcellus in the jaw. He struck the doorframe, kept one hand flat over the packet, and drove his free elbow into the man's chest. Then he shoved him into his companion. Both hit the wall. Marcellus pulled his sleeve free and left with the stone and blood at the corner of his mouth.

He had given Dante all of that. The part he hadn't been eager to reach, Shawna told me, was the conversation waiting in his kitchen.

Celeste was frying chicken. According to the story Marcellus told Dante, she turned down the burner when she saw his face and asked how much he'd spent.

He put the cigarette packet on the kitchen table and asked her to look first.

Shawna held her hand flat as if setting it down herself. “She asked him again. How much, Marcellus?”

Then Celeste opened the packet. Marcellus described the rough blue surface to Dante as something that looked as if a piece of sky had been buried in dirt. I had only Red's price in my head until Shawna told me that. The little packet and the chicken pan made the number feel absurd in a different way.

Celeste looked at the stone, then at her husband.

“We are not eating that,” Shawna quoted her.

Marcellus held it toward the window. Light passed through and laid a blue shape across Celeste's white sleeve. She stopped talking. He remembered that she said only one little word when she saw it: oh.

He watched her instead of the stone. That was what he later told Dante. It mattered to me that he'd once known where to look without being reminded.

Cutting the diamond took months. Marcellus worked in a room behind the house. Celeste sang at a lounge that barely paid her and brought him food between sets and errands. He told Dante about finding meals cold beside his tools because he'd forgotten she had come in.

At the end of the first month, she brought his pillow to the workshop.

At the end of the second, she put a photograph of herself beside his lamp.

Shawna reached toward our bedside lamp as she gave me Celeste's explanation. “So you remember who the fuck lives out here.”

Marcellus reached for her. Celeste put his dinner in that hand and went back into the house.

I laughed before I meant to. Shawna looked back at me, and I put my hand down from the cross. I had been holding it again.

At the end of the third month, Marcellus put on his good suit and went to the lounge.

Shawna described him walking toward the stage with a small box in his hands. Celeste was halfway through a song. The pianist saw him coming and lost his place. Celeste turned, saw her husband, and spoke into the microphone.

“Don't propose again,” Shawna quoted Celeste. “I fell for that shit once.”

People laughed. Marcellus reached the steps and told her he'd made something for her. She came down to him. He opened the box.

On their wedding night, Shawna said, Dante had held her hand beneath a lamp, moving it in and out of the light while he tried to show her the blue Marcellus had described. The color deepened in the shade of his hand. It looked wet even when you knew it wasn't. Now I watched the light on her fingers while she told me. I wanted to touch them, and I waited until she had finished showing me.

Celeste put her fingers over her mouth. Marcellus lifted out the necklace and asked her to turn around. He fastened it behind her neck. Small pale stones surrounded the blue one, making it appear darker against her skin.

Shawna touched the base of her own throat. “She asked what he called it.”

Marcellus answered Blue Mercy. Celeste asked what needed mercy.

“The last three months,” Shawna quoted him.

She told me Celeste put her arms around him and kissed him in front of the room. The pianist found his place and started playing again. I pictured Marcellus still holding the empty box behind her back, unable to put it down because she had both his arms occupied.

Red had been in the lounge with Valentino. Twenty-eight then. Marcellus identified both men in the story he told Dante. I wondered whether they'd clapped along with the room. Knowing what followed made that possibility harder to stomach.

I wanted to interrupt Shawna. I wanted to ask whether Dante had kissed better, promised more, understood faster. Those were the questions rising in me while she told me about a man who had spent months forgetting his wife for a stone.

I kept my mouth shut.

For once, I wanted to hear what happened more than I wanted to hear myself.

Two weeks after the lounge, Red came to Marcellus's workshop with T and a bottle of wine. Shawna said Marcellus had claimed the wine cost more than his first car. Red asked to buy the diamond. Marcellus refused.

Red named more money. Marcellus told him the necklace belonged to his wife.

“Then ask your wife,” Shawna quoted Red.

She paused before giving me Marcellus's answer. “She isn't for sale either.”

T laughed. Red left the wine behind when they went.

Celeste read the label and carried the bottle to the sink. Marcellus caught her wrist and asked whether she knew what it was worth. She pulled free and poured it out.

Shawna looked at me while repeating Celeste's words. “That's why I don't want to find out what we owe him if we drink it.”

But a necklace worth a fortune didn't pay the power bill when nobody wanted to sell it. Valentino offered to arrange a private showing. There were clients who wanted work in a similar blue setting, men who wanted Marcellus to examine stones they owned, people willing to pay for his hands rather than his wife's necklace.

Marcellus agreed. He told Celeste there would be enough work to stop worrying.

“She asked whether he'd ever have enough work to stop,” Shawna told me.

Before the showing, he made a second necklace. This mattered, and I asked Shawna to slow down while she explained it. A lesser blue stone, convincing under warm light, set in an identical necklace. It could sit in the display while Celeste wore the true diamond.

Marcellus marked the removable mounting in a hidden place. The real diamond also carried a tiny irregularity inside it, something he hadn't made.

I looked at Shawna. “Find his mark, find the stone?”

She shook her head. “The mounting comes off. Could put either stone in it. Dante said you have to look inside the diamond.”

I nodded. “The little flaw.”

“That one,” Shawna said. “Marcellus knew exactly where it was.”

It didn't save either necklace. The men who came took both.

They took the loose jewels from the back room too, and Marcellus's book of customers. T held him against the workbench. Valentino removed the necklace from Celeste's throat carefully enough that, in the account Marcellus gave Dante, her hair stayed where she'd pinned it.

Red stood in the doorway while the others did the work.

“Perhaps now we can agree on a price,” Shawna quoted him.

Marcellus lunged toward Red. T hit him. The lamp fell from the bench, and Marcellus went down beside it.

Celeste dropped to her knees. She told Red to take the stone and leave. Red said she should have agreed in the beginning, then put an envelope on the bench. A fraction of what he'd offered. Enough paper money to hold up afterward and call payment.

I pushed the blankets aside and got out of bed. Shawna stopped speaking until I reached the window. I rested one hand against the frame and told her to keep going.

Marcellus had stayed on the floor after the men left. Celeste pressed a towel to his face and kept asking him to look at her.

“He told Dante he couldn't stop looking at her throat,” Shawna said behind me.

I stared at the lake. I could hear her shift on the mattress.

Marcellus had been alive on the floor, with Celeste alive beside him. The necklace was gone, and he had kept looking at the empty place on her throat while she tried to help him. I hated how easily I could imagine doing the same thing with different names in the room.

Marcellus went after it.

He wore the same good suit he'd worn to give the necklace to her. When Shawna told me that, I looked down at my own coat, thrown over a chair with the diamond pin still fastened above the hole.

I turned halfway toward the bed. “He told Dante what he wore?”

Shawna nodded. “Said Celeste tried to stop him.”

I leaned against the window frame. “With words?”

Shawna lifted one hand. “His car keys.”

I nodded toward her. “Smart woman.”

He took a taxi. A gun in his coat, Red's envelope of money on his knee. He told Dante he looked back and saw Celeste in the doorway holding the useless keys. She didn't shout after him. He remembered that silence long afterward.

Valentino's club had gold doors and a stairway lit from beneath. Marcellus recognized the singer onstage. She stopped singing when she saw him. He crossed to Valentino's booth, where two men sat beside him, and put the envelope on the table.

Shawna repeated Marcellus's words: he had brought back their money. He had come for what was his.

One of Valentino's men stood. Marcellus hit him with the pistol before he got upright. The man fell across the table, knocking a champagne bottle sideways. The other reached beneath his jacket. Marcellus shot him.

The bullet struck the man's shoulder. He lived; Shawna told me Marcellus had heard that repeated through the case afterward. Valentino dropped behind the booth and disappeared through a door near the bar while the room emptied around them.

Marcellus followed him into a corridor, then through another door into a room full of jewelry under bright lights. Watches and rings lay in open boxes. Some carried names Marcellus knew. His own tools sat on a table.

Shawna described him picking one up and finding his initials on it. He had engraved them when he first bought the tool. He turned it over in his hand while the gun remained in the other.

The diamond wasn't there.

Two men came through the door behind him. A third appeared at the far end of the room. Marcellus turned and asked where the stone was. Someone hit him behind the ear. He struck the table, and rings scattered across the tiles. Hands caught his wrist before he could bring the pistol around. Another blow folded him forward. He fell among the jewelry.

Shawna's voice had become slower. I watched her in the window's reflection. She was looking down at the watch while she described what Dante had told her, and I knew she was passing along something she had once heard in a wedding dress.

When the police arrived, Marcellus could hear Valentino in the doorway explaining how dangerous grief had made him. Marcellus tried to rise. The men holding him pressed him down, then carried him out through the club.

The taxi was still outside. According to Marcellus, its driver held up the meter when the police brought him through the gold doors. He pointed at the fare, then at his car, and followed them halfway to the cruiser before anybody answered him.

I let out a breath that wasn't quite a laugh. The meter had survived the whole fucking expedition.

Celeste came to the station wearing blue slippers. One heel was crushed beneath her foot. Marcellus told Dante he saw the slippers before he looked at her face.

He told her he hadn't found the necklace.

“I found you,” Shawna quoted Celeste.

I looked back at the lake.

Marcellus hadn't answered. He told Dante he kept returning to that moment in his mind, trying to arrive with something better to say. I could imagine the cold chair, her slippers, all the time in the world afterward to find a sentence he needed right then.

“Red has to die,” I told Shawna.

She came to the window and took my hand. “Keep listening.”

I held on to her fingers. “I am. That's what the listening's doing.”

Marcellus went to prison. Red opened the Saint with money raised against a diamond he claimed to have bought from a respected jeweler. Marcellus learned that from a magazine left in the visiting room. Shawna told me he'd remembered the photograph of the new hotel almost as sharply as he remembered his own workshop.

Celeste visited while she could. At first, she wore bright dresses and brought stories from the lounge. A pianist had arrived in two different shoes. A couple had argued through an entire song, then both requested it again. If Marcellus interrupted to ask who'd been seen with the necklace, she pushed the candy she'd brought closer to him and told him to eat.

Later, she came less often. She was sick. Marcellus admitted to Dante that he'd believed the missed visits were punishment until they argued about a doctor he hadn't known she was seeing and money he couldn't send. Hearing Shawna repeat that admission, I looked at the watch on her wrist and kept my hand open around hers.

On Celeste's final visit, she wore earrings Marcellus had made before either of them knew Red. She asked him to remember the first thing he'd given her.

He guessed the earrings, then a bracelet. Celeste told him no. He kept trying to name an object.

“A ride,” Shawna quoted her. “I was standing in the rain. You drove me home. You didn't ask for my number till we got there.”

Marcellus said he remembered.

Shawna's fingers tightened around mine as she gave me Celeste's reply. “Then remember that man too.”

Celeste put her hand against the visiting-room glass.

After she died, a letter came among her things. Marcellus carried it until the folds began to split. One night he read it to Dante. Dante wrote the words down after his release because, Shawna told me, he was afraid of losing them. He read them to her on their wedding night.

Shawna could give me parts from memory. Later that same day, Marcellus let me read the worn letter itself. I remember Celeste's words in full, including the ones Shawna couldn't finish saying.

*My love,*

*You think I never understood that stone. I did. I understood every hour you spent getting it right. I know the little words you hid beneath the blue mounting. I know you were waiting for me to find them, and I know you were disappointed when I liked the front so much I forgot to look behind it.*

*I found them the night you fell asleep with your shoes on. I was angry with you for missing supper. I took the necklace off and nearly dropped it, and there you were underneath, saying what you used to say at my door.*

*I came for you.*

*I wore those words against me every night afterward. You never asked whether I'd found them. You just watched me wear it and smiled.*

*I am so tired now, Marcellus. I can't keep driving out there. Don't let anybody tell you I stopped because I stopped loving you. I am stopping because I am sick, and I want the strength I have left for things that don't require me to pass through a locked door.*

*If you get your beautiful stone back, put it down before you touch somebody you love. I know your hands. They are warmer without all that metal in them.*

*And if you are still angry when you come home, be angry. You always were. Just open the curtains. Pay the piano man what I owe him. There is chicken in the freezer because I never learned how to cook for one.*

*I would take the ride again.*

*Celeste.*

At the window, Shawna fell quiet after telling me the last part she remembered. I stayed facing the water until I could trust my face. My hand lay in hers. I was grateful she didn't pull it away to make me look at her.

“What did Dante do?” I asked.

Shawna stood close to my shoulder. “Promised he'd get it back.”

I watched a little boat moving out on the lake. “For a man he met in prison?”

Shawna followed my gaze. “For him. For her. For whatever he thought that would make him.”

I turned toward her. “And you?”

Her voice was quiet. “I thought I married the kind of man who'd do that.”

I nodded. I didn't like Dante better. I just had a harder time hating him in a way that left me looking good.

Shawna led me back to the bed. I sat, and she remained beside me, holding the watch between us while it was still fastened to her wrist.

“Dante took the three million first,” she told me. “Wanted a lawyer for Marcellus. Wanted a way out for us when Red found out what else he was after. He thought T would choose him.”

She looked down at the metal. “His brother.”

I kept my voice low. “He didn't.”

Shawna shook her head. “No.”

She turned the watch over until the clasp faced upward.

“Last night Dante came home,” Shawna said, “he asked if it still looked too big on me. I told him everything he owned was too big. He said he'd put something in it that fit.”

I looked from the clasp to her face. “What something?”

Shawna's fingers paused against the catch. “Thought he meant himself.”

Then she opened it. Her hand was shaking, and I slid my palm beneath the watch so it wouldn't fall when it came free.

For the first time since I'd met her, I wanted to see what her dead husband had left inside.
